## Kora

- **Tradition**: Mande jeliya (griot) practice — Mandinka and Maninka peoples of the Gambia, Senegal, Mali, Guinea. `tradition_scope`: Mande jeli lineage practice generally; no single school claimed.
- No `MUSICAL_SYSTEMS` file exists for Mande griot practice. Would need a new file; content would cover cell-based melody within named tunings (see below), not a fixed scale.
- Best candidates: Eric Charry, *Mande Music: Traditional and Modern Music of the Maninka and Mandinka of Western Africa* (U. Chicago Press, Chicago Studies in Ethnomusicology) — tradition-institution-adjacent academic fieldwork across Mali/Guinea/Senegal/Gambia; openly readable partial (academia.edu PDF exists, faculty page has excerpts, full text likely needs library access). Second: Yusuf/Joseph Progler's field-note blog piece on teaching and learning kora in West Africa (ethnomusicologist, fieldwork-based) — openly readable, section depth.
- Djembefola.com's "Jeliya" article and the Grinnell College instrument-collection page are lower-tier general references, useful for cross-checking construction detail (calabash, cowhide, antelope-gut-now-nylon strings) but not specialist-grade alone.
- Gate verdict: **likely pass**, contingent on actually reading Charry's book (or a substantial excerpt) at section depth rather than relying on the Progler piece alone.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: jeliya performance rights (kora, balafon, kontingo, né) are **hereditary and exclusive to jeli lineages**; named tunings (Silaba, Tomora, Hardino) are each tied to specific historical epic repertoire that is lineage-transmitted, not freely arrangeable.
- Biggest risk: presenting jeli repertoire (e.g. praise epics, historical genealogies) as generic "African harp" material, or flattening the three named tunings into a single scale — directly the rule-1 failure the system-file index warns about.

## Balafon

- **Tradition**: Mande bala tradition, Guinea and Mali, tied to the Sosso Bala lineage (13th-century Mali Empire). `tradition_scope`: Mande balafon broadly; UMD dissertation likely narrows to a specific fieldwork community — needs confirming on full read.
- No system file (Mande griot practice) — would share a file with kora if one is built.
- Best candidates: UMD PhD dissertation, *Transmitting the Mande Balafon: Performing Africa at Home and Abroad* (drum.lib.umd.edu) — full text is hosted and openly downloadable, but this pass could not render the PDF as text (binary), so depth is unconfirmed beyond the abstract. Second: Lynne Jessup, *The Mandinka Balafon: An Introduction with Notation for Teaching* — a published teaching text by a scholar-performer who studied balafon in the Gambia; only a Google Books preview was checked, not full text.
- The heptatonic ~171-cent-step tuning (~7-TET) is reported by multiple general sources but needs a named, sourced measurement per rule 7 (every cents table is one instance) before it goes in a page.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — a strong dissertation exists and is openly hosted, but this pass did not confirm its readable depth; the teaching text is not open online. Needs one actual full read before commitment.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: same jeliya exclusivity as kora — bala is one of the jeli's hereditary instruments; the Sosso Bala itself is a specific sacred instrument in Guinea (UNESCO-recognized) with restricted access, distinct from balafon playing generally.
- Biggest risk: treating the Sosso Bala's sacred status as generic balafon lore, or presenting the 7-TET approximation as "the" tuning rather than one measured instance.

## Shona mbira (mbira dzavadzimu)

- **Tradition**: Zezuru Shona people, Zimbabwe. `tradition_scope`: Zezuru Shona bira-ceremony practice specifically; other Shona mbira types (njari, matepe) are different instruments not covered by this search.
- No system file for Shona tradition.
- Best candidates: mbira.org, run by the MBIRA nonprofit (Erica Azim, an American player trained and recognized within the Shona tradition, working with named Zimbabwean master musicians) — practitioner/institution; this pass got HTTP 403 on fetch, so depth is confirmed only via search snippet (bira ceremony role, "telephone to the spirits," family-specific tunings). Second: ResearchGate paper "Mbira dzavadzimu and its space within the Shona cosmology: tracing mbira from bira to the spiritual world" — academic with fieldwork framing, only abstract-level access confirmed this pass (ResearchGate typically paywalls full PDF).
- Construction is well-documented in general sources (22-28 forged metal keys on a gwariva hardwood board, three registers) but that is not itself specialist-sourced yet.
- Gate verdict: **borderline-to-pass** — mbira.org is the right kind of source (practitioner-institution operating inside the tradition) but was not actually read this pass; the academic paper needs a non-paywalled copy.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: **central to this instrument** — the mbira dzavadzimu's primary traditional context is the bira, an all-night spirit-possession ceremony contacting vadzimu (ancestor spirits) and mhondoro (chiefly/guardian spirits). This is exactly the rule-5 case: bira repertoire and function should not be treated as sample material or generic "trance music," and the page must state plainly what is declined.
- Biggest risk: presenting mbira as a "world percussion" timbre for a track without naming that its core repertoire is ancestor-communication ceremony, or implying one family's tuning (e.g. Nyamaropa) is universal.

## Shakuhachi

- **Tradition**: Japanese — the historical Fuke sect (Zen Buddhist, komusō monks) and its secular successor schools (Kinko-ryu, Tozan-ryu, Myoan-ha, Chikuho-ryu). `tradition_scope`: state which lineage/school a claim describes; Fuke history and Kinko-ryu honkyoku are not interchangeable with, e.g., Tozan-ryu repertoire.
- No system file for Japanese tradition.
- Best candidates: International Shakuhachi Society, komuso.com — confirmed via fetch to be a practitioner/institution site with moderate scholarly depth on Fuke history and honkyoku (covers 8th-century origins through 1867 disbanding, is explicit about what is legend vs. documented). Second: Christopher Yohmei Blasdel & Kamisango Yuko, *The Shakuhachi: A Manual for Learning* (Ongaku no Tomo Sha, 1988) — Blasdel trained directly under Living National Treasure Goro Yamaguchi; a genuine practitioner-teacher method, but a print book not confirmed openly readable in full online.
- Additional: "Sacred Abjection in Zen Shakuhachi," *Ethnomusicology Review* (UCLA) — open-access academic article, not opened this pass but likely reachable at full depth.
- Gate verdict: **likely pass** — komuso.com clears the institution/practitioner bar at confirmed section depth; a second specialist (the Ethnomusicology Review article, open access) is plausible without further blockers.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: **honkyoku and the Fuke tradition** are explicitly named in the task brief as restricted/ceremonial — these were originally suizen (blowing-Zen meditation) pieces played by komusō monks, not concert repertoire; komuso.com itself is cautious about claiming certainty on how suizen was actually practiced historically.
- Biggest risk: presenting honkyoku as generic "meditative flute" content divorced from Fuke/suizen context, or collapsing distinct schools' honkyoku repertoires into one undifferentiated corpus.

## Koto

- **Tradition**: Japanese sōkyoku (koto music), from Edo-period solo/chamber repertoire through modern Sawai-school and conservatory contexts. `tradition_scope`: needs naming per lineage (e.g. Ikuta-ryū vs Yamada-ryū schools were not surfaced this pass — a gap).
- No system file for Japanese tradition.
- Best candidates: Wesleyan University PhD dissertation, *Hōgaku Pedagogy: Traditional Japanese Music in the Twenty-First Century* — full text hosted openly on Wesleyan's digital collections, academic, likely covers teaching transmission in depth (not read past confirming full-PDF availability). Second: "Koto scales and tunings," *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* — genuine specialist acoustics source but paywalled at AIP Publishing (abstract only).
- Sawai Sōkyoku-in (Sawai Koto Academy of Music, Tokyo, founded 1979 by Sawai Tadao and Sawai Kazue) is a real tradition institution surfaced by name but its own site was not located/verified this pass.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — one likely-strong open academic source (the dissertation), but no confirmed practitioner/institution source read at depth; the acoustics paper doesn't clear the practitioner requirement even if read.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass — koto's court-gagaku origins raise the possibility of historically restricted repertoire, but this is unconfirmed; flag as an open question for the writer to check directly against a koto-tradition source.
- Biggest risk: presenting the hirajōshi tuning as "the" koto tuning rather than one of several named tunings selected per piece, or ignoring the ryū (school) distinction entirely.

## Shamisen

- **Tradition**: Japanese, multiple distinct branches under one instrument family — nagauta (kabuki accompaniment, hosozao build), tsugaru-jamisen (Tsugaru region, futozao build, percussive soloistic style), gidayū/jōruri, min'yō. `tradition_scope`: must be stated per branch; nagauta and tsugaru-jamisen are functionally different practices on differently built instruments.
- No system file for Japanese tradition.
- Best candidates: Bachido (bachido.com), run by Kevin Kmetz — an American tsugaru-jamisen player trained in Japan under named teachers; a genuine practitioner-teacher resource with moderate-to-good depth on styles and technique per the search snippet. Second: Sonica Instruments' technical note on Tsugaru sukui/hajiki articulations — practitioner-adjacent (written by/for sample-library development with technical specificity) but commercial in origin, so borderline as a specialist source on its own.
- No academic or tradition-institution source (e.g., a nagauta iemoto school, a Japanese conservatory) was surfaced this pass — a real gap.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — one confirmed practitioner-teacher source, but the second candidate is commercially adjacent rather than independently specialist; needs a genuine academic or institutional second source.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass; gidayū/jōruri's association with puppet theater (bunraku) and kabuki suggests performance-context specificity worth checking, but no ceremonial-restriction claim is confirmed. Flag as open.
- Biggest risk: treating tsugaru-jamisen's percussive soloistic style as representative of "the shamisen" generally, erasing the nagauta/gidayū/min'yō distinctions and their different builds (hosozao vs futozao).

## Erhu

- **Tradition**: Chinese, specifically the 20th-century conservatory-standardized tradition descending from Liu Tianhua's teaching method (1930s), alongside older regional and opera-accompaniment (e.g. Jiangnan sizhu, Cantonese opera) uses not covered by this search. `tradition_scope`: state conservatory-style vs. regional/opera style explicitly; they differ in technique and repertoire.
- No system file for Chinese tradition.
- Best candidates: Nicholas Ng, "Introduction to Erhu" (academic conference/method paper — Ng is a practicing erhu performer and ethnomusicologist, University of Melbourne) — only located via a Scribd upload, not opened in full this pass, so depth is unconfirmed. Second: Vancouver Inter-Cultural Orchestra's erhu resource page — a real performing institution's instrument page, moderate depth per snippet, not independently verified by fetch.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — Ng is the right kind of practitioner-academic source but wasn't actually read; the institution page is plausible but unconfirmed at depth.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass; erhu's older ritual/opera-pit uses were not investigated. Flag as open.
- Biggest risk: presenting the post-1930s conservatory virtuoso style (recital repertoire, standardized pedagogy) as if it were erhu practice across all eras and regional contexts, erasing older folk and opera-accompaniment functions.

## Guzheng

- **Tradition**: Chinese, 21-string conservatory-standardized tradition (from the 1950s) plus older regional schools — Chaozhou, Hakka, Henan, Shandong, etc., none of which were surfaced by name this pass, a real gap. `tradition_scope`: conservatory-standard vs. regional school must be distinguished; not attempted here.
- No system file for Chinese tradition.
- Best candidates: Missouri State University (BearWorks) thesis, "The Development of Guzheng Tuning" — academic, openly hosted full PDF, not read past confirming availability. No practitioner or institution-level second source was found at specialist depth; guzhengalive.com and similar sites are teaching-adjacent commercial content, not verified as specialist.
- Gate verdict: **borderline**, leaning toward fail without a second source — one plausible academic thesis is not enough on its own; needs either a named regional-school practitioner or an ethnomusicology fieldwork source.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass. Flag as open — worth checking whether any regional guzheng repertoire has ritual or Daoist/Buddhist ensemble associations.
- Biggest risk: presenting the D-major-pentatonic 21-string conservatory tuning as "the" guzheng tuning, erasing the regional schools' distinct scales, ornamentation vocabularies, and repertoires entirely.

## Pipa

- **Tradition**: Chinese, pear-shaped plucked lute; modern conservatory tradition (established via nine conservatories 1927-1964) plus older regional/lineage schools (Pudong, Chongming, Pinghu — not surfaced by name this pass). `tradition_scope`: conservatory-standard vs. named lineage school.
- No system file for Chinese tradition.
- Best candidates: UC eScholarship dissertation, "An Exploration of the Pipa..." — academic, openly hosted full PDF, not read past confirming availability. Second: Gao Hong, Hal Leonard *Chinese Pipa Method* — Gao Hong is a genuine practitioner (Central Conservatory-trained, teaches at Carleton College), but this is a commercial print method with online video lessons, not confirmed openly readable in full.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — a plausible open academic dissertation plus a real but non-open practitioner method; needs the dissertation actually read at depth, or an open practitioner interview/article substituted.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass; older pipa repertoire in narrative/programmatic forms (e.g. *Ambush from Ten Sides*) is public concert repertoire, not evidently restricted, but this wasn't specifically checked against ritual-ensemble contexts.
- Biggest risk: presenting modern conservatory technique (five-finger tremolo, standardized 29-31 frets) as timeless pipa practice, without noting it is a 20th-century standardization layered onto older regional lineages.

## Dizi

- **Tradition**: Chinese, transverse bamboo flute; the searched material centers on the post-1949 solo/conservatory tradition (per the "Forever Red" title itself), distinct from dizi's older folk/opera-accompaniment roles. `tradition_scope`: post-1949 solo-concert tradition specifically, not dizi generally.
- No system file for Chinese tradition.
- Best candidates: "Forever Red: The Invention of Solo Dizi Music in Post-1949 China," *British Journal of Ethnomusicology* — the right kind of source (ethnomusicology, historically specific) but paywalled at Taylor & Francis, abstract-only this pass. arXiv paper on dizi resonator acoustics — genuinely open access and specialist, but acoustics-only, and acoustics papers explicitly do not satisfy the practitioner/teacher/institution/fieldwork requirement per the gate.
- Gate verdict: **likely fail** — nothing at practitioner, teacher, or tradition-institution level was found readable at depth this pass; the one ethnomusicology source that would count is paywalled.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way. Flag as open — dizi's older ritual/opera uses were not investigated.
- Biggest risk: writing a page from the dimo-membrane craft blogs and buyer guides alone, which is exactly the "agreement reads as corroboration" trap the protocol warns about — these sites are consistent with each other but none is a specialist source.

## Sarod

- **Tradition**: Hindustani classical (North Indian), with the Maihar gharana (Ustad Allauddin Khan's lineage) dominant in contemporary performance; other gharanas (Gwalior, Senia Shahjahanpur, Lucknow) named but not detailed. `tradition_scope`: state gharana explicitly; sarod construction itself changed under Allauddin Khan (larger teak body, wider fingerboard, expanded sympathetic strings) so pre- and post-reconstruction instruments differ physically.
- System file exists: `RAGA_AND_TALA.md` in `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/` — a page would link there for mode/cycle theory rather than duplicate it.
- Best candidates: ipassio's teacher pages (Mallar Rakshit, Abhishek Borkar, Dr. Troilee Dutta) — genuine practicing teachers offering structured online lessons, openly readable, but the material found is introductory (tuning basics, hand position) rather than section-depth scholarship. No ethnomusicology or conservatory-level academic source on sarod specifically was surfaced this pass.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — practitioner presence is real and plentiful, but nothing yet read at the depth the gate wants; needs either a deeper ipassio lesson series actually read, or an academic source (e.g., work on the Maihar gharana specifically).
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass. Hindustani classical bandishes (compositions) are gharana-protected in a soft-IP sense but this is a different kind of restriction than sacred/ceremonial; worth distinguishing explicitly if written up.
- Biggest risk: presenting Maihar-gharana sarod (the now-dominant style) as sarod practice generally, erasing the older Shahjahanpur/Lucknow/Gwalior lineages' distinct instruments and styles.

## Sarangi

- **Tradition**: Hindustani classical (North Indian); regional/folk sarangi traditions (e.g. Rajasthani sarangi, used differently) exist but are not covered by these sources. `tradition_scope`: Hindustani classical concert sarangi specifically, via Magriel's and Courtney's documented lineages (Magriel studied under Ustad Abdul Latif Khan and Ustad Mohammed Ali Khan).
- System file exists: `RAGA_AND_TALA.md`.
- Best candidates, both confirmed by direct fetch this pass: **Nicolas Magriel**, sarangi.net — a practicing sarangi teacher and ethnomusicologist (SOAS-trained; his own site states decades of teaching sarangi and Hindustani vocal music, plus an alphabetical archive of sarangi performers) — genuine practitioner/scholar depth, openly readable. **David Courtney**, chandrakantha.com — a practitioner-educator (also a tabla teacher/repairer) whose sarangi page was confirmed to carry detailed construction diagrams with Hindi/Urdu nomenclature, four tuning systems compared, and technique/fingering discussion including Ram Narayan's specific method — openly readable, section-to-full depth.
- Gate verdict: **likely pass** — two independent sources, both practitioner-level, both confirmed readable at section depth in this pass (the strongest result of the whole survey).
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass; sarangi's historic association with courtesan (tawaif) culture and its subsequent stigma/decline in status is a documented social-history thread worth surfacing as context, not a repertoire restriction per se.
- Biggest risk: writing sarangi construction/tuning from Courtney's site alone and skipping Magriel's fieldwork-based performer archive, which would miss the instrument's actual performance practice and social history (the stigma and decline are part of "what must not be casually universalised").

## Steelpan (Trinidad and Tobago)

- **Tradition**: Trinidad and Tobago steelband/pan tradition, emerging from Afro-Trinidadian communities in the late 1930s-40s. `tradition_scope`: the national/Carnival steelband tradition specifically; note that individual panyards and named pioneers (e.g. Winston "Spree" Simon) represent distinct lineages within it.
- No system file for Trinidadian steelband.
- Best candidates: William R. Aho (Rhode Island College), academic essay hosted on panonthenet.com — confirmed by fetch: real fieldwork in Trinidad 1972-1985, interviews with panmen and pan women, full text openly readable, but the essay is a social/history analysis (class, race, cultural legitimacy) with only light technical coverage of construction or tuning acoustics. Second: NTA-TT (National Training Agency) and YTEPP steelpan-manufacturing pages — Trinidadian vocational-training institutions, plausible institution-level sources on construction, but not verified at depth by direct fetch this pass.
- Gate verdict: **borderline-to-pass** — Aho clears the fieldwork/practitioner-adjacent bar for social and historical content; the technical/construction side needs the NTA-TT or YTEPP pages (or the University of Trinidad and Tobago's Advanced Steel Pan Tuning course materials) actually read to confirm depth.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way this pass — steelband is a secular Carnival/festival tradition as far as these sources show, not ceremonial in the mbira/dùndún sense. Flag as resolved-likely-open (no restriction found, not confirmed absent).
- Biggest risk: treating pan tuning as a purely technical/acoustic subject and skipping the social history Aho documents — pan's road from criminalized street instrument to national symbol is load-bearing context, not background color.

## Yoruba dùndún (talking drum)

- **Tradition**: Yoruba people, Nigeria; the dùndún hourglass-drum ensemble (iyáàlù lead drum plus supporting drums) used for speech surrogacy, oríkì (praise poetry), and court/community announcement functions. `tradition_scope`: the specific fieldwork community in Lagos documented by the named informants below; dùndún practice elsewhere in Yorubaland may differ.
- No system file for Yoruba tradition.
- Best candidate, confirmed by direct fetch: "The Speech Surrogacy Systems of the Yoruba Dùndún and Bàtá Drums: On the Interface Between Organology and Phonology," *Frontiers in Communication* (2022) — fully open access, peer-reviewed, and built on field recordings made in Lagos in February 2020 with three named master drummers (Ayanlere Alajede on ìyáàlù dùndún, Kangan Bamidele on gángan, and one anonymous bàtá master). This is fieldwork with practitioners at full readable depth — the strongest single source found in this survey. Second: the companion paper, "When Music Speaks: An Acoustic Study of the Speech Surrogacy of the Nigerian Dùndún Talking Drum," same open-access journal, acoustics-focused (specialist but not itself a practitioner source, though it draws on the same fieldwork).
- Gate verdict: **likely pass** — one fieldwork source with named practitioner-informants at confirmed full depth, plus a second open specialist source from the same research effort.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: the task brief names dùndún speech surrogacy and oríkì specifically. The sources confirm oríkì (praise poetry) and òwe (proverbs) as core repertoire transmitted through practitioner lineages, and note the drum's historic role informing kings of arriving guests — a court-announcement function. Whether specific oríkì texts are family/lineage-restricted (as praise poetry naming specific lineages often is in Yoruba practice) was not directly confirmed this pass and should be checked before any page treats oríkì text as freely reusable.
- Biggest risk: extracting the "talking drum" pitch-contour mechanism as a generic melodic-typing trick without carrying forward that its primary traditional content is specific spoken language (Yoruba) and specific praise/proverb texts tied to real lineages and courts — not an abstract "speech-like drum sound."

## Turkish ney

- **Tradition**: Ottoman/Turkish art music (makam) and Mevlevi Sufi ceremonial practice (sema). `tradition_scope`: state whether a claim is about the Mevlevi/tekke context or the concert/art-music context — they overlap but are not identical.
- No system file for Turkish makam (confirmed absent per the task brief; `ADDITIVE_METERS_BALKAN_TURKISH.md` exists but covers meter, not makam or the ney's role in it).
- Best candidates: Karl Signell, *Makam: Modal Practice in Turkish Art Music* (1977) — the canonical English-language academic reference on Turkish makam, but a print book; not confirmed openly readable online this pass. Ozan Yarman's PhD dissertation, "79-Tone Tuning & Theory for Turkish Maqam Music" (Istanbul Technical University Turkish Music State Conservatory) — openly readable on academia.edu, genuinely academic and institution-adjacent (ITU conservatory), but its focus is tuning-system theory generally, not ney construction/technique/repertoire specifically.
- No named ney hoca (teacher) or player-practitioner source with substantial written material was found and verified this pass; salamuzik.com's ney guides are commercial/SEO-adjacent and were not treated as specialist on their own.
- Gate verdict: **borderline** — two real academic candidates exist, but neither is ney-specific pedagogy from a practitioner, and Signell's book wasn't confirmed open. Needs a named ney teacher's written material (many exist in Turkish; English-language open ones were not found this pass) or full access to Signell/Feldman.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: the Mevlevi Sufi sema ceremony and its ney taksim (improvisation) are ceremonial/tekke-lineage practice, adjacent to but distinct from concert Turkish art music; this needs explicit treatment if a page is written, similar to shakuhachi's honkyoku/Fuke distinction.
- Biggest risk: treating ney playing as generic "Middle Eastern flute" technique and missing that its two major contexts — Mevlevi ceremonial practice and Ottoman/Turkish classical concert music — carry different repertoire, aesthetics, and (in the Mevlevi case) ceremonial weight.

## Kemençe (klasik and Black Sea)

- **Tradition**: two distinct instruments sharing a name. **Klasik kemençe** (Armudî/pear-shaped kemençe) is a bowed lute in Ottoman/Turkish classical (fasıl) music, related to the Constantinopolitan lyra and Greek politikí lýra. **Black Sea kemençe** (Karadeniz kemençesi, "kemençe of the Black Sea," also called Pontic lyra) is a distinct folk fiddle of the Trabzon/Rize/Giresun region, central to horon dance music. `tradition_scope`: these must never be merged into one "kemençe" claim — several of the commercial sources found this pass do exactly that.
- No system file for either (Turkish makam covers klasik kemençe's context; Black Sea kemençe would need a distinct regional-folk file).
- Best candidates found were almost entirely for the **Black Sea** instrument: Istanbul Technical University PhD thesis (polen.itu.edu.tr, May 2023) — openly hosted full PDF, genuinely academic and institution-based, not read past confirming hosting. "Organonscape, geography, and aural spaces of kemençe in and around Trabzon," *Ethnomusicology Forum* (2022) — the right kind of source (ethnomusicology, regionally specific, uses fieldwork-adjacent framing of "organonscape") but paywalled at Taylor & Francis, abstract-only this pass.
- For **klasik kemençe** specifically, nothing at specialist depth was surfaced this pass — every source found (including Sala Muzik's "Kemençe, Pontic Lyra & Politiki Lyra Guide") treats it alongside the Pontic/Black Sea instrument rather than on its own terms, which is itself evidence of the "agreement reads as corroboration" trap.
- Gate verdict: **likely fail for klasik kemençe** (no dedicated specialist source found distinguishing it from the Black Sea instrument); **borderline for Black Sea kemençe** (one open PhD thesis not yet read, one paywalled ethnomusicology article — needs the paywall cleared or a substitute practitioner source, e.g. a named Trabzon kemençe player's own material).
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: nothing found either way for either instrument this pass. Flag as open for both.
- Biggest risk: writing one page that silently conflates two different instruments from two different musical systems (Ottoman classical fasıl vs. Black Sea regional folk/horon) because the buyer-guide sources do exactly that — this is close to the "continent as genre" failure the index warns against, at a smaller geographic scale.

## Bağlama/saz

- **Tradition**: Anatolian Turkish folk (âşık/folk minstrel tradition, regional styles across Turkey) and, distinctly, Alevi-Bektashi ritual practice (cem ceremony), where the bağlama is called "the Qur'an with strings" and carries deyiş/nefes repertoire. `tradition_scope`: secular âşık/folk performance vs. Alevi cem ritual use are two different contexts requiring separate treatment.
- No system file for Turkish/regional folk tradition.
- Best candidates: named published teaching methods — Sabri Yener, *Bağlama Öğretim Metodu* ("Bağlama Teaching Method"), and İrfan Kurt, *Bağlamada Düzen ve Pozisyon* ("Bağlama Tuning and Position") — both real, practitioner-authored, standard Turkish pedagogical texts (both credited with establishing the 17-tone-per-octave fret system used today), but neither was found openly readable online in this pass, and both are in Turkish. Archaeopress (an academic archaeology/humanities publisher) blog post, "The Turkish Long-Necked Lute or Bağlama" — plausibly credentialed but the specific author was not confirmed this pass.
- Gate verdict: **likely fail as surveyed** — the genuinely authoritative practitioner sources (Yener, Kurt) exist but are not open/English and were not obtained; the one open blog candidate is unconfirmed for authorship and depth.
- Restricted/ceremonial repertoire: **flag explicitly** — Alevi-Bektashi cem ceremony repertoire (deyiş, nefes) played on bağlama is sacred/ritual to that community, distinct from secular âşık and modern folk-pop use of the same instrument. This needs the same treatment as mbira's bira ceremonies: name it, decline to imitate the ritual use, and point to adjacent secular âşık repertoire instead.
- Biggest risk: presenting bozuk/kara düzen tuning and generic "Turkish folk" ornamentation as the whole of bağlama practice while being silent about the Alevi ritual dimension — an omission that would misrepresent both the instrument's cultural weight and its restricted repertoire.
