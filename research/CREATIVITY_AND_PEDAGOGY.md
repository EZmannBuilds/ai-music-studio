# Research: Creativity, Project Development and Teaching

## Purpose

The research behind `creative-lab/SKILL.md`, `project-guide/SKILL.md`,
`shared/CREATIVE_EXPLORATION_SCHEMA.md`, `shared/INTERACTION_MODES.md` and the listener panel in
`listener-model/SKILL.md`.

The guiding question:

> How do you generate directions that differ by mechanism, help someone develop and finish their own
> work, and teach without taking the work away from them?

# 1. Why most "alternatives" are not alternatives

## Research

- Boden's distinction, formalised by Wiggins (2006), *Knowledge-Based Systems*: **exploratory**
  creativity searches within a conceptual space defined by a style's rules; **transformational**
  creativity alters or drops one of those rules so that previously unreachable results become
  possible; **combinational** creativity juxtaposes familiar ideas. Wiggins showed transformational
  search is exploratory search one level up: you search the space of rule sets.
- Guilford's divergent production is scored on fluency, flexibility, originality and elaboration,
  where **flexibility is spread across categories**, not the number of ideas.
- Osborn's idea-spurring checklist, compressed by Eberle as SCAMPER: substitute, combine, adapt,
  modify, put to other uses, eliminate, reverse.

## Skill translation

Five chord progressions are five points in one space. To differ by mechanism, a candidate must change
the space: a different pitch organisation, a different rhythmic logic, a different form grammar, a
different performance constraint, a different compositional process.

The Creative Lab therefore states the current space as explicit rules before generating anything, and
builds candidates by dropping, inverting, substituting or combining those rules. Its own test counts
**mechanism categories**, after Guilford's flexibility, not candidates.

# 2. Constraints generate only when they forbid the default

## Research

- Stokes (2006), *Creativity from Constraints*: constraints work in pairs. One **precludes** a
  habitual response; the other **promotes** a substitute. Novelty still requires domain competence.
- Eno, in a 1980 radio interview, on Oblique Strategies: the cards came from working situations where
  "the panic of the situation, particularly in studios, tended to make me quickly forget that there
  were other ways of working"; they are a way of remembering that tangential attacks exist. In
  practice he commits to a drawn card for a pass rather than treating it as a suggestion.
- Stravinsky, *Poetics of Music*: "the more constraints one imposes, the more one frees one's self".
  This is a composer's creed, not a finding.
- Amabile's componential model: intrinsic motivation is the component most sensitive to environment,
  and **externally imposed** constraints such as surveillance and expected evaluation can *lower*
  creative output.

## Skill translation

`creative_exploration.constraint` is recorded as a preclude/promote pair, so a constraint that forbids
nothing is visible as a non-constraint. The user chooses or accepts the constraint: the Lab proposes,
the user imposes. That ordering is the difference between Stokes' generative constraint and Amabile's
corrosive one.

# 3. "Temperature" is a preference dial, not a quality axis

## Research

- Berlyne's arousal-potential curve predicts peak preference at intermediate novelty.
- Chmiel & Schubert (2017), *Psychology of Music*, reviewing 57 studies: only 15 showed a clean
  inverted-U; many showed preference for the prototypical and familiar.
- Gold et al. (2019), *Journal of Neuroscience*: pleasure is highest at particular combinations of
  predictability and uncertainty, not at maximum novelty.

## Skill translation

`temperature: safe | exploratory | radical` selects how far from the anchor candidates travel. **Safe
is a legitimate setting**, not a failure, and the Lab never presents radical as better. A user who
wants the familiar done well is asking for a real thing.

# 4. Improvisation as a model for constrained generation

## Research

- Pressing (1988), in Sloboda (ed.), *Generative Processes in Music*: improvisation runs against a
  **referent**, "a set of cognitive, perceptual, or emotional structures that guide and aid in the
  production of musical materials", with a rehearsed knowledge base supplying retrieval cues.
- Berliner (1994), *Thinking in Jazz*: vocabulary is learned aurally, then recombined, transposed and
  modified; it is a language and a tradition rather than spontaneous invention.
- Norgaard (2011), *Journal of Research in Music Education*: artist-level improvisers described
  **sketch plans** for upcoming passages and continuous self-monitoring folded into planning.
- Sawyer's group-creativity work: group flow depends on close listening, equal participation,
  autonomy, and the real possibility of failure; the weakest move is refusing the offer.

## Skill translation

Rule-based improvisation in `shared/EXPERIMENTAL_SYSTEMS.md` declares a referent explicitly, and the
Performance Director plans at phrase level before choosing notes, after Norgaard. Where the studio
works turn by turn with a user, it acknowledges and extends what the user brought rather than
replacing it.

## Caution

These sources are jazz-centred and assume a harmonic referent, a shared repertoire and a live group.
A loop, a timbre, a text or a rhythmic cycle can equally be the referent.

# 5. Developing and finishing a project

## Research

- Getzels & Csikszentmihalyi (1976), *The Creative Vision*: art students who spent longer in
  **problem-finding**, delaying closure and restructuring the problem mid-work, produced work judged
  more original, and this predicted later success.
- Collins (2005), *Psychology of Music*: a three-year case study describes composition as "problem
  proliferation and successive solution implementation" — recursive, not linear.
- Valéry: a work is never finished, only abandoned, in the sense that an accident stops it and hands
  it to the public. Rubin (2023) puts the same thing as a decision: done is when you are ready to let
  go, and work can be finished on the clock even though it is not made on the clock.
- Neto, Hartmann, Luck & Toiviainen (2024), *Journal of New Music Research*, with a large corpus and
  130 professionals: high-energy, high-valence tracks are placed early; consecutive transitions
  alternate direction in energy and valence; professionals agree most about the opening track.
  Neto et al. (2025), *PLOS One*, across roughly 50,000 albums: tempo follows an inverted U while
  loudness and arousal follow the opposite shape, varying by genre and era. The authors note evidence
  that scrambling order may not change felt emotion.

## Skill translation

- The Project Guide writes a **completion definition before production**, not after, and treats an
  empty thesis field as an open question rather than an invitation to invent one.
- Its revision loop logs each pass as problem found, solution tried, new problems. When three passes
  touch the same place, it stops fixing and reopens the question, after Getzels & Csikszentmihalyi.
- Sequencing is offered as **two orders built on different principles**, with the corpus regularities
  described as what professionals do. The studio does not tell a user their order is wrong.

# 6. Teaching without taking over

## Research

- Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976): six scaffolding functions — recruitment, reducing degrees of freedom,
  direction maintenance, marking critical features, frustration control, demonstration.
- The expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al., 2003; meta-analysis 2025, *Learning and Instruction*,
  176 effects across 60 studies): worked examples and heavy guidance help low-prior-knowledge learners
  and **hurt** high-prior-knowledge learners.
- Hattie & Timperley (2007) and Kluger & DeNisi (1996): feedback operates at task, process,
  self-regulation and self levels; feedback directed at the self is least effective, and more than a
  third of feedback interventions in the Kluger & DeNisi meta-analysis **lowered** performance.
- Amabile (1979): expected external evaluation lowered judged creativity, except where criteria were
  explicit.
- Kratus (1991) describes seven levels of improvisation and warns against skipping them.

## Skill translation

`shared/INTERACTION_MODES.md`:

- support fades as demonstrated competence rises, and self-reported expertise is a starting point, not
  a setting to trust;
- feedback stays at task, process and self-regulation level, and ends with a question the user can act
  on;
- criteria are stated before the work is judged, and the user may change them;
- a beginner is not buried in terminology and an expert is not given a worked example they did not ask
  for. Both failures are failures.

## Caution

These are Western school-curriculum models. Kratus' levels are a diagnostic, not a ladder that must be
climbed in order; aural traditions teach in other sequences.

# 7. Listeners differ in what they name, not mainly in what they feel

## Research

- Bigand & Poulin-Charronnat (2006), *Cognition*: untrained listeners perceive tension and relaxation,
  form expectancies, integrate large-scale structure and respond emotionally at levels close to
  trained musicians. Training matters most for explicit labelling and fine discrimination.
- Wolpert (2000), *Music Perception*: all musicians noticed a vocal in a different key from its
  accompaniment; only 40% of non-musicians did, and half of those reported differences that were not
  there.
- De Man et al. (2015), AES: critical listeners' emotional responses varied more with mix quality;
  mixers preferred their own mixes without recognising them.

## Skill translation

The listener panel defines perspectives by **attention channel** (affect and gist, structure and
harmony, production and timing, body and groove) rather than by demographic caricature. The casual
perspective reports feeling, not theory. Every panel report carries the same warning: these are
simulated tendencies drawn from small studies, they over-articulate, and they do not replace playing
the music to a person.
