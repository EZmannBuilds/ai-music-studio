# Harp: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Concert pedal harp, with a short lever-harp note. Six sources were read at section depth: two
public-domain orchestration treatises with substantial harp chapters (Forsyth 1914; Rimsky-Korsakov),
one further public-domain treatise chapter describing the historical single-action harp
(Berlioz/Strauss), one dedicated orchestration-research web page on harp scoring technique, one
harp-pedagogy web page comparing lever and pedal mechanisms, and the American Harp Society's public
"Getting Started" page. This is a better-sourced family than the 2.0 page's own evidence note
claimed: the pedal-mechanism and glissando material that 2.0 labelled `excerpt-derived` from an
unnamed "public orchestration academy" is now traceable to two primary orchestration sources read
directly in this pass.

Salzedo's own method was not opened directly (paywalled/out of print in an accessible digital form);
where Salzedo's technique is mentioned, it is via a secondary description found in web search, not
read at section depth, and is not counted as a source here.

The finger-position-to-tone claim (fleshy pluck warmer, tip pluck brighter) was likewise only found
via a search summary, not opened at section depth for harp specifically. Since it does not reach
section depth it carries no record below; it is stated on the page as `to-verify` only, and is
tracked in "Still to verify."

## Records

### HARP-01 Pedal-foot assignment: left foot B, C, D; right foot E, F, G, A
- claim: the seven transposing pedals are split three to the harpist's left foot (B, C, D) and four
  to the right foot (E, F, G, A), radiating outward from the player.
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: high
- scope: modern (post-1810) double-action concert pedal harp.
- limitations: none material; this is a description of a stable mechanical design that has not
  changed in its pedal-side assignment since Forsyth's time. This directly verifies the spec's stated
  claim (left foot D, C, B; right foot E, F, G, A): Forsyth lists the left side "B-, C-, and
  D-pedals" in that order radiating outward from the player, the same three pedals, ordered from the
  player outward rather than alphabetically.
- inference: none.

### HARP-02 A pedal change takes a small but real amount of time and is not always silent
- claim: depressing a pedal into either notch "requires a small but appreciable fraction of time,
  and is not always free from noise."
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: high
- scope: modern double-action pedal harp mechanism.
- limitations: no measured millisecond figure or a description of what the noise sounds like beyond
  "not always free from noise"; this pack does not invent a number.
- inference: labelling this mechanical noise "pedal buzz" (a term used in modern harp discussion) is
  this pack's naming of Forsyth's described phenomenon, not a term found in the source itself.

### HARP-03 Repeated notes are produced by re-plucking a different, enharmonically-tuned string, because quick repetition on one string is poor technique
- claim: quickly repeating the same string is technically awkward and produces a weak, damped tone,
  because each pluck damps the previous one before the string's vibration (which needs a visible
  amplitude in the lower/middle registers) has developed; harpists instead achieve rapid repeated
  *sounds* by alternating between two adjacent strings tuned to the same pitch via an
  enharmonic-unison pedal setting (e.g. one string set to C-sharp, the adjacent string left as
  D-flat).
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: high
- scope: modern double-action pedal harp.
- limitations: describes orchestral-writing practice (Debussy is cited as a worked example) rather
  than a controlled measurement of repeated-note technique.
- inference: none.

### HARP-04 The thumb is held up, and plays the highest note of a hand's chord, not the lowest
- claim: unlike a pianist's thumbs, which oppose each other and sit at the bottom of each hand's
  span, a harpist's thumbs are both held up (above the fingers); because of this, the thumb-to-index
  span is the largest interval within a hand's reach, and the thumb naturally takes the topmost note
  of a hand's chord or arpeggio group, not the lowest.
- source: HARP-THUMB-HANDPOSITION (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: medium
- scope: modern pedal harp hand position (Salzedo/French-school hand position, the standard taught
  today).
- limitations: the hand-position fact itself is stated plainly and is standard, widely-taught harp
  technique, corroborated loosely by an unread web-search summary of Salzedo-method discussion
  describing "thumbs kept up." The source describes hand position and interval reach, not a loudness
  measurement; "the thumb plays the highest note" is a position fact, and "therefore it is often
  played loudest" is this pack's application of general plucking-dynamics knowledge (a firmer pluck
  near the fingertip is louder), not separately measured for harp specifically, and is kept as
  inference on the page rather than folded into this record's confidence.
- inference: the loudness implication (see corrections below) is flagged as inference building on a
  sourced position fact.

### HARP-05 The pedal harp is essentially diatonic; only seven pitch classes sound at once; a chromatic scale across an octave requires rapid multi-pedal changes and is effectively unplayable at speed
- claim: with all pedals set, the harp produces a diatonic scale (or a reconfigured "scale" of some
  other seven-note collection via enharmonic-unison tuning); executing a full chromatic scale
  requires setting and re-setting up to five pedals within a single octave and is, in Berlioz's own
  words for the older single-action instrument, "impossible for any harp" at any but the slowest
  tempo. The modern double-action harp is more flexible (it can be "set" to more keys) but remains
  bound by the same one-pedal-per-letter-name mechanism once set.
- source: RIMSKY-1913 (section); FORSYTH-1914 (section); BERLIOZ-1855 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: medium
- scope: modern pedal harp for Rimsky-Korsakov and Forsyth; the older single-action Eb harp for the
  Berlioz passage specifically, used here for historical context on *why* the instrument is described
  as "anti-chromatic," not as a claim about the modern instrument's exact limits.
- limitations: the general diatonic/pitch-class-limit claim is stated independently and consistently
  by all three primary sources, which is high confidence; but exactly how much more flexible the
  modern double-action harp is versus the single-action harp Berlioz describes is medium confidence,
  since Berlioz's chapter predates Erard's invention becoming universal. Berlioz's specific "eight
  keys" limitation describes the obsolete single-action harp, not the modern double-action instrument
  (which Forsyth describes as settable "almost as one chooses" before then being bound to that
  setting); this pack does not carry Berlioz's specific eight-key list forward as a modern
  constraint.
- inference: none for the core claim; care taken not to conflate the historical and modern
  instruments' exact numeric limits.

### HARP-06 Glissando pitches are set by the pedal configuration, including enharmonic-doubling tricks to produce non-diatonic collections
- claim: a glissando sweeps all seven string classes in whatever the pedals are currently set to; by
  setting some string pairs to enharmonic unison (e.g. both members of a pair sounding the same
  pitch), a subset of pitches can be made to repeat within the sweep, producing recognisable
  sonorities (dominant sevenths, diminished sevenths, and similar) rather than a plain diatonic
  scale. No chromatic (twelve-pitch) glissando is possible, because there are only seven string
  classes per octave.
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section); RIMSKY-1913 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: high
- scope: modern pedal harp.
- limitations: none material.
- inference: none.

### HARP-07 Lever harps change pitch one half-step at a time, by hand, string by string, and cannot manage the mid-phrase pedal changes a pedal harp can
- claim: a lever harp has one hand-operated lever per string, each raising that single string by one
  half step; there is no double-action equivalent (no single mechanism reaches flat, natural and
  sharp on one string the way a pedal does), so achieving an enharmonic change on several strings of
  one letter name requires flipping several levers by hand. Because levers are hand-operated, a
  lever-harp player must have a free hand and a beat or two of time to make a change, unlike a pedal
  harp player, whose feet are free while both hands keep playing. This is why certain classical
  repertoire written for pedal harp cannot be played on a lever harp without alteration, while
  Celtic and folk repertoire, which was generally written with lever harp's constraints in mind, can.
- source: LEVERHARP-PEDAL-PEDAGOGY (section)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: lever (folk/Celtic) harp versus concert pedal harp.
- limitations: a single pedagogy web source, not cross-checked against a lever-harp method book,
  though consistent with the American Harp Society's own basic pedal/lever distinction
  (AHS-GETTINGSTARTED).
- inference: none for the mechanism.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

- **"The thumb is stronger, so the lowest note of a hand's group is usually the loudest" was wrong.**
  Harpists pluck with the thumb held up, above the fingers, so the thumb naturally takes the
  **highest**, not lowest, note of a hand's group (HARP-THUMB-HANDPOSITION). The loudness implication
  is kept as inference, since it depends on plucking dynamics as well as position, but the position
  claim itself — the load-bearing part of the error — is now sourced and corrected.
- **Pedal and glissando material relabelled from `excerpt-derived`/unnamed source to sourced.** The
  2.0 page attributed this to "a public orchestration academy's harp articles, read as search
  excerpts only." This pass opened two orchestration-treatise chapters (FORSYTH-1914, RIMSKY-1913)
  that state the same mechanisms directly and in more mechanical detail (the enharmonic-unison table,
  the pedal-foot assignment, the pedal-change-timing description), so the material is now sourced
  rather than a weaker label.
- **Pedal-foot assignment verified**, not merely asserted: left foot B, C, D; right foot E, F, G, A,
  confirmed word-for-word in Forsyth's description of the modern instrument's layout.
- **Pedal buzz** (audit finding: not previously named) is now sourced as a real, if unquantified,
  phenomenon: Forsyth's own words are that a pedal change "is not always free from noise."
- **Repeated-note behaviour** (audit finding) is filled by HARP-03: enharmonic-string repetition, and
  the mechanical reason quick single-string repetition is avoided (each pluck damps the last before
  it has spoken).
- **Lever harp** is added as a short new section, sourced to a dedicated pedagogy page plus the
  American Harp Society's own pedal-vs-lever description.

## Still to verify

- **Exact full range** (2.0 gave C1-G7, `orchestration-text`, Adler, not opened): Rimsky-Korsakov's
  chapter states the full compass as C-flat1 to F-sharp7, with the practically-used range being the
  first to fourth octave of that span. This is now sourced rather than not-opened, and the 2.0 figure
  was close but not exact; corrected on the page.
- **Finger-position-to-tone claim** (fleshy pluck = warm, tip pluck = bright/loud): only found via
  search summary, not opened at section depth for harp specifically; kept as to-verify on the page
  rather than promoted to sourced, and carries no record above (see "Scope and method").
- **Salzedo's own method**: not opened directly; hand-position claims corroborated instead through
  HARP-THUMB-HANDPOSITION and general web-search consensus. A direct read of Salzedo and Lawrence's
  *Method for the Harp* would strengthen HARP-04 further.
- **A measured pedal-change time in milliseconds**: not available in any source opened; Forsyth's
  "small but appreciable fraction of time" is the strongest available statement and is kept
  qualitative on the page rather than assigned an invented number.
