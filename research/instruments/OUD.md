# Oud: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Arabic oud practice (Egyptian, Levantine, Iraqi), as distinguished in
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MAQAM.md`. Nine sources were tried; five were read and used at full depth:
MaqamWorld's own oud page and its instrument-family overview page (both written and maintained by a
practitioner of the tradition, Johnny Farraj, co-author of the standard reference *Inside Arabic
Music*), a University of California Santa Barbara Middle East Ensemble teaching page (directed by
ethnomusicologist Scott Marcus), and two Michael Frishkopf ethnomusicology papers on Egyptian
Sufi/devotional performance, read for the secular-versus-devotional ensemble question rather than for
oud construction. A performer-teacher's blog (Brian Prunka, a working oud performer and teacher) was
reachable only as a page of post titles, read at excerpt depth; its content is used once, cautiously,
to corroborate that position-based fingering pedagogy exists, not for any specific technical claim.
The two standard texts named in the research spec, Farraj & Abu Shumays (2019) and Racy (2003), were
attempted (a table-of-contents PDF and several retailer/preview pages) but no readable section-depth
content was reachable in this pass; both are registered `not-read`.

Not reachable: an oud-specific acoustics paper (soundboard resonance, plucking-point spectrum); an
oud-specific tremolo-regularity study; region-specific (Egyptian v. Levantine v. Iraqi) technical
pedagogy distinguishing construction or technique rather than only intonation and repertoire (which
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MAQAM.md` already covers at the system level). Commercial/SEO pages on oud
lutherie and a Turkish-vs-Arabic mandal comparison were found during search but are not used as
sources anywhere on this page, per the research spec's rule that such pages may lead to a primary
source but are never themselves one.

## Records

### OUD-01 Construction: fretless short-necked lute, paired courses, bowl back
- claim: the oud is a fretless, short-necked lute with a deep pear-shaped bowl back of bent wood
  ribs, a thin wooden soundboard, and five or six courses of strings (normally paired in unison) plus
  a single bass string, played with a plectrum (risha).
- source: MAQAMWORLD-OUD (full); UCSB-MEE (full)
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: high
- scope: Arabic oud generally, as described by two independent institutional sources; not a claim
  about Turkish or Persian construction, which the sources themselves distinguish.
- limitations: neither source gives acoustic measurements (wood species properties, plate thickness,
  bracing); both are descriptive rather than technical-acoustic accounts.
- inference: none for the construction facts themselves; the page's framing of the fretless neck as
  the instrument's defining programming-relevant fact is this pack's synthesis.
- tradition_scope: Arabic oud practice generally; both sources describe general/pan-regional Arab
  ensemble use rather than one school.

### OUD-02 Tuning is named and regionally/individually variable, not singular
- claim: at least two differently-spelled reference tunings are in documented use (MaqamWorld: C F A
  D G C low to high; UCSB: GG AA D G c, with a variable lowest course and an optional sixth string),
  and the lowest course is explicitly recorded as varying by player.
- source: MAQAMWORLD-OUD (full); UCSB-MEE (full)
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: low
- scope: two named instances, one from a practitioner-run reference site, one from a university
  ensemble's teaching page; not a survey of tunings across the tradition.
- limitations: high confidence for the existence of at least two named, disagreeing tuning instances;
  low confidence for any claim about which is more common or region-specific, since neither source
  attributes its tuning to a specific region, school or era beyond "commonly used" language. This page
  does not claim either tuning is Egyptian, Levantine or Iraqi specifically, because neither source
  says so.
- inference: the instruction to "state which named tuning a part uses" is this pack's own
  methodological conclusion from the disagreement, not a statement either source makes.
- tradition_scope: Arabic oud practice generally; the specific regional attribution of either named
  tuning is unresolved and left to-verify on the page.

### OUD-03 Ensemble role: naqr family, heterophonic takht texture
- claim: the oud belongs to the naqr ("plucking or hammering") instrument family alongside the
  qanun, set against the sahb ("pulling or stretching") family (nay, violin); the takht ensemble
  texture is heterophonic, with no chordal accompaniment section.
- source: MAQAMWORLD-INSTR (full)
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: high
- scope: the standard Arabic takht ensemble (oud, nay, qanun, violin, riqq) as described by
  MaqamWorld; does not cover larger 20th-century orchestral Arab ensembles, which the same source
  notes as a separate later development.
- limitations: MAQAMWORLD-INSTR names the sahb/naqr grouping but does not go further into how the
  two families interact moment to moment beyond "meant to complement each other."
- inference: the programming consequence (do not write the oud chordally) is this pack's synthesis
  from the stated heterophonic, non-chordal ensemble shape.
- tradition_scope: Arabic takht practice generally.

### OUD-04 The oud is a documented secular-tarab instrument, distinct from the Sufi/devotional wind role
- claim: in at least one documented Egyptian Sufi-chant (inshad) ensemble tradition, the oud (with
  the violin) is explicitly an instrument of secular tarab music and "not traditional in Sufi music,"
  where the wind role is instead carried by reed flutes (nay, kawala).
- source: FRISHKOPF-TARAB-2001 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology (fieldwork-based)
- confidence: medium
- scope: one ethnomusicologist's account of one named Egyptian Sufi ensemble (Shaykh Yasin's group)
  that borrowed secular-tarab instrumentation; not a claim about every Sufi order or every region.
- limitations: a single documented case, explicitly described by the source as unusual/borrowed
  instrumentation for that context, not a general law that the oud never appears in devotional
  Arabic-tradition music anywhere.
- inference: none beyond restating the source's own framing; the page is careful to scope this as
  one documented tradition, not a universal claim.
- tradition_scope: Egyptian Sufi/devotional performance practice, specifically the ensemble Frishkopf
  documents; not generalised to Arabic maqam practice as a whole.
