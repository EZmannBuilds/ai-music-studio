# Javanese Gamelan Instruments: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Ten Balungan (American Gamelan Institute journal) articles were located and downloaded from the
journal's public archive (gamelan.org); seven were read in full (two, Cengkok for the Gender and
An introduction to the slendro gender panerus, required OCR from page images because their PDFs
carried no extractable text layer) and three (Hastanto's interview, Susilo's memoir, Supanggah's
"Gatra") were read at section depth, sampling the sections relevant to instrument construction,
tuning and performance role rather than the full running text of interviews and theoretical essays
whose remainder concerns institutional history, aesthetics and analytical argument outside this
page's scope. One additional Balungan article, on the bonang barung (Lysloff), returned a 404 "page
does not exist" response and was not read; the gap is recorded honestly on the bonang card and in
"Still to verify" below rather than filled by inference. No manufacturer manual, acoustics paper or
dedicated technical source specific to the rebab, suling or siter/celempung was located in this
pass; read depth was judged by whether the passages actually cited were read in the session, not by
skimming a table of contents.

## Records

### JAV-01 Gong, kenong, kempul, kethuk and kempyang: construction, tuning method and named individual-pitch quirks

- claim: Hanging gongs (gong ageng, siyem, suwukan, kempul) and gong-kettles (kenong, kethuk,
  kempyang) are tuned primarily by hammering rather than by removing material, "so that the overall
  mass is not reduced"; a maker/tuner scrapes (files or grinds) a metallophone key's middle to lower
  its pitch and either end to raise it, but treats gongs differently, hammering them and only rarely
  filing; kempul specifically may be either scraped (lowers pitch) or hammered (raises pitch); a
  cracked or damaged gong-kettle can be restored to clearer resonance by packing a towel or sponge
  inside it; two kethuk in a set are conventionally a kempyung (Javanese fifth) apart; some
  individual pitches (kempul 3 generically, gong suwukan 2 less severely) are reported as
  characteristically hard to tune, moving the "wrong" direction under the hammer.
- source: SUKARNO-1998 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: One documented tuner's (Panggiyo Resowiguna, a professional Solonese gamelan maker and
  STSI teacher) account, given in an interview about retuning two specific New Zealand university
  gamelan sets (one Central Javanese, one Cirebon pelog); the kempul-3/suwukan-2 difficulty is stated
  by him as general ("everywhere," not just that set).
- limitations: A single named tuner's practice and testimony, not a survey of Javanese gamelan-making
  workshops generally; no acoustic measurement accompanies the account, and no frequency figures are
  given for any of these instruments in a specific set.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese gamelan making/tuning practice, as taught and practised at STSI
  Surakarta; the Cirebon-set portion of the interview describes a different, related pelog tuning
  the tuner was asked to reconcile toward Solonese practice, not treated here as representative of
  Cirebon gamelan on its own terms.

### JAV-02 Colotomic register hierarchy and the seleh (cadence) as the structurally decisive part of a gatra

- claim: Kenong marks phrase-internal terminals and is treated by musicians as marking "which
  kenong unit" a performance has reached; kempul, kethuk and kempyang mark finer subdivisions inside
  that; the final (fourth) beat of a gatra (the seleh) is described by named Javanese theorists as
  the structurally decisive part, carrying most of the weight for how the phrase is realised by the
  elaborating instruments.
- source: SUPANGGAH-GATRA (section); SUKARNO-1998 (full, for the kenong/kempul/kethuk-kempyang
  register-and-role facts specifically)
- source_type: ethnomusicology (Supanggah); performer (Sukarno/Resowiguna)
- confidence: medium
- scope: Supanggah's essay reports named Javanese theorists' (Sindusawarno, Martopangrawit)
  terminology for gatra structure and the significance of its final beat, as an insider theoretical
  account; Sukarno's interview gives register/role facts for kenong, kempul, kethuk and kempyang
  from one tuner's working knowledge.
- limitations: The Gatra essay was read at section depth (excerpts on gatra terminology and the
  seleh's significance), not start to finish; its fuller argument about competing theoretical
  formulations of gatra (citing Sumarsam and Perlman's own separate dissertations) was not read in
  this pass and is not represented on the guide page beyond the seleh-significance claim.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese karawitan theory as formulated by named Surakarta-tradition
  theorists (Sindusawarno, Martopangrawit) and reported by Supanggah, a Surakarta-based
  scholar-practitioner.

### JAV-03 Saron family and slenthem: construction, regional-style tone, and the balungan/saron range distinction

- claim: The saron family (demung, saron barung, saron panerus/peking) is built from metal keys over
  a shared trough resonator (not individual tubes); slenthem shares the balungan-instrument function
  but is constructed with individual tube resonators like the gendèr family; Yogyakarta-style saron
  use larger, thicker keys and harder-wood mallets, producing a louder, clearer, showier tone,
  while Surakarta style favours a softer balungan that leaves interpretive room for the elaborating
  (garap) instruments; the slendro saron family's playable range (one octave plus two tones) is
  narrower than the balungan gendhing's own conceptual range (about two octaves plus two or three
  tones), a fact several named Javanese and Western scholars (Sumarsam, Supanggah, Hastanto, cited
  within the essay; Kunst, Hood, Becker as the Western view being contested) use to argue the notated
  balungan is not simply "what the saron plays."
- source: SUSILO-1999 (section, for saron/slenthem construction and function); SUPANGGAH-1988 (full,
  for the range distinction and the Surakarta/Yogyakarta contrast)
- source_type: performer (Susilo); ethnomusicology (Supanggah, translated by Perlman)
- confidence: medium
- scope: Supanggah's essay draws its regional comparison from his own observation of Surakarta and
  Yogyakarta karawitan practice as a Surakarta-trained scholar-performer; Susilo's account is a
  general memoir description of gamelan instrument roles for a mixed pedagogical/festival audience.
- limitations: Supanggah states he does not intend a detailed comparison along every dimension of
  contrast, only illustrative examples; the range figures given (one octave plus two tones slendro,
  one octave pelog) are for "the saron family" generally, not measured for a specific named set.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese karawitan, specifically the Surakarta and Yogyakarta court/
  conservatory styles Supanggah names; not extended to village or regional styles outside those two.

### JAV-04 Balungan formats, irama and density: mlaku, nibani, nggantung, and their relationship to garap latitude

- claim: Balungan is played in named formats (mlaku/"walking," one note per beat; nibani, tones
  only on even beats; nggantung, "hanging"; and others) that determine how much interpretive
  latitude the elaborating (garap) instruments have — sparser formats (nibani, nggantung) give more
  latitude; changing irama level (e.g. dados to wilet) increases the space between balungan strokes,
  offering the same increased latitude; denser balungan and louder playing are associated with
  Yogyakarta style, sparser/softer balungan with Surakarta style; some gendhing genres (palaran)
  dispense with a full balungan, using only cadence-tone indications.
- source: SUPANGGAH-1988 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: Supanggah's own theoretical account and worked notated examples (gendhing Cucurbawuk,
  ketawang Langen Gita, gendhing Pancatnyana, gendhing Lambang Sari, lancaran Tropongan), addressed
  to readers already familiar with Javanese gamelan notation and terminology.
- limitations: Illustrated with specific named gendhing rather than a systematic survey of every
  named balungan format; the essay itself argues the written/notated balungan is a secondary,
  "adjusted" (dibesut) version of an "essential" balungan the ensemble as a whole produces, a
  theoretical position this page reports as Supanggah's argument, not as settled consensus (the
  translator's preface notes competing formulations by Sumarsam and Sutton that use different terms
  for related ideas).
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese karawitan, Surakarta-centred (Supanggah's own training and
  primary reference point), with explicit Surakarta/Yogyakarta comparison.

### JAV-05 Gendèr and gendèr panerus: construction, key/resonator dimensions, and the visually-symmetric unplayed-key layout

- claim: A documented slendro gendèr panerus is built with a teak case (plangkan, ~91 cm long,
  ~47 cm high), bronze keys suspended by cord (pluntur, ~2 m) through end pieces (denda, ~20 cm) held
  by pegs (sanggan, ~5.5 cm, of bronze, buffalo horn or bamboo), over zinc or bamboo tube resonators
  (bumbungan) individually tuned to each key (lowest-tone resonator ~7.6 cm diameter, highest-tone
  ~4.2 cm, both ~37 cm long), with a partial-cap modification (tumbengan) available when a resonator
  cannot be shortened further; the instrument has 13 or 14 total keys but only 9 or 10 are actually
  played, the rest kept for visual symmetry since Javanese gamelan, including the gendèr panerus,
  "is not only meant to be heard but to be seen."
- source: MARSUDI-1988 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: high
- scope: One documented gendèr panerus, owned by the Pusat Pengembangan Kesenian Jawa Tengah
  (Central Javanese Cultural Center) at Sasono Mulyo, Surakarta, as measured and described in a
  named student's (Marsudi's) ASKI final-exam paper.
- limitations: Dimensions and key counts are for this one named instrument; the paper itself notes a
  documented but never-encountered alternative 13-key layout attempted by a named maker (Projo
  Wiguno) specifically to balance the player's hand positions, which did not become standard —
  evidence that layout is a construction choice, not a physical necessity, but not itself generalised
  here beyond that one anecdote.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese gendèr panerus as built and taught at ASKI Surakarta in the
  1980s; gendèr barung is stated to conventionally share the same key count, but its own dimensions
  are not separately measured in this source.

### JAV-06 Gendèr playing technique: two-handed independence, and the timed-damping rule

- claim: Gendèr is struck with a padded disc-mallet in each hand; most melodic activity is carried
  by the left hand, with the right hand playing a complementary (often octave) part; every stroke's
  ring is damped by the heel of the same hand that struck it, timed to land at or just after the
  next attack, except that a repeated tone following a rest is damped during that intervening rest
  instead of at the next stroke; this damping technique is described as "an extremely important
  aspect of gendèr playing."
- source: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: high
- scope: A named Surakarta gamelan teacher's (Wignyosaputro's) beginner-exercise method, collected
  and published by Jody Diamond, intended to introduce the hand-motion/damping vocabulary before a
  student attempts real cengkok.
- limitations: The exercises themselves are explicitly not performance material ("not used in actual
  performance"), designed to isolate the damping/hand-independence technique for teaching purposes;
  the source does not measure timing in milliseconds or give acoustic confirmation of the damping
  rule, only the teacher's verbal/notational account of it.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: One named Surakarta teacher's method for beginning Western/foreign gamelan
  students specifically (the article notes the gendèr is unusually hard for students without a
  lifetime of exposure to gamelan sound); presented as broadly representative gendèr technique, not
  scoped to a single named lineage the way the Balinese Sinti/Vitale sources are.

### JAV-07 Gendèr panerus laku density system: a directly measured instance of density scaling with irama

- claim: Gendèr panerus playing technique is organised into four named densities (laku): laku 2 (8
  strokes per gatra, irama lancar), laku 4 (16 strokes per gatra, irama I/tanggung), laku 8 (32
  strokes per gatra, irama II dados or III wilet), and laku 16/rangkep (64 strokes per gatra, irama
  IV rangkep and sometimes wilet); each level exactly doubles the stroke count of the level before
  it, giving a directly counted instance of irama's density-doubling relationship.
- source: MARSUDI-1988 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: high
- scope: The same ASKI final-exam paper as JAV-05, giving notated example figures (Figures 1-4) for
  each laku level on the documented slendro gendèr panerus.
- limitations: Stroke counts are given per gatra (four balungan beats) for this one instrument; the
  paper does not separately confirm the same doubling pattern for gendèr barung, though it implies
  a parallel relationship exists across the elaborating instruments generally, consistent with
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`'s independently stated (differently sourced) irama rule.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese gendèr panerus technique as taught at ASKI Surakarta.

### JAV-08 Gendèr and gendèr panerus cengkok: seleh-oriented realisation, and documented inter-player variation

- claim: Cengkok (melodic patterns) on gendèr and gendèr panerus are aimed at a seleh (cadence)
  tone and realised idiomatically, not doubling the balungan literally; gendèr follows the rebab and
  vocal (pesindhèn) melody rather than tracking the balungan closely; individual players' realisation
  of the same named cengkok differs in detail: three named Solonese gendèr panerus players'
  (Martopangrawit, Ngasinah, Sarno) transcribed realisations of the same cengkok (e.g. Puthut Gelut,
  Debyang-debyung, Ayu Kuning, Kacaryan, Duduk, Rujak-rujakan) share the same seleh target but differ
  in their specific note-by-note path to it.
- source: BRINNER-1985 (full); MARSUDI-1988 (full)
- source_type: performer (Brinner); pedagogy (Marsudi/Devereaux)
- confidence: high
- scope: Brinner's article discusses gendèr barung cengkok generally, drawing on a named student's
  (Suprapto's) ASKI exam transcription and the named-teacher lineage Brinner himself studied under
  (K.R.T. Wasitodipuro, R.Ng. Martopangrawit, R.Ng. Mloyowidodo, L.M. Harjito); Marsudi's paper
  documents the same inter-player-variation pattern specifically for gendèr panerus, transcribing
  three named players' versions of each named cengkok side by side.
- limitations: Both sources describe Solonese (Surakarta-style) practice specifically; neither
  claims the same named cengkok are realised, or even named, identically in Yogyakarta style, and
  this page does not extend the claim there.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Surakarta-style Central Javanese karawitan, documented through named ASKI-trained
  and ASKI-affiliated musicians.

### JAV-09 Gambang cengkok logic (gantung/seleh) and its density scaling with irama: a second, independent instance of the irama density rule

- claim: Gambang elaboration is built from two cengkok types: gantung ("hanging," circling a pitch
  without resolving) and seleh ("goal," moving toward and arriving at a balungan pitch on its final
  beat, chosen to open from above or below that goal tone for the smoothest join with the preceding
  cengkok); gambang density scales with irama level, documented for one lesson piece (Lancaran
  Ricik-ricik) at 4:1 gambang-to-balungan density in irama I (tanggung), doubling to 8:1 when the
  same piece is played in irama II (dados).
- source: WIDIYANTO-2010 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: A named teacher's (Widiyanto S. Putro's) "two cengkok" beginner method, compiled from
  lessons given at U.C. Berkeley and later email correspondence, explicitly presented by its own
  author as a simplified first step ("Gambang 101/102"), not the full complexity of real gambang
  playing.
- limitations: The teacher's own framing states that real gambang playing is "much more complicated
  and melodically extended" than this teaching method represents, and that learning the "authentic
  garap" requires listening to recordings rather than reading notation; the 4:1/8:1 density figures
  are for one specific documented lesson piece, not a general survey across repertoire.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: One named teacher's (Widiyanto's) beginner-pedagogy method for teaching Central
  Javanese gambang to Western students at U.C. Berkeley.

### JAV-10 Ensemble dynamic leadership: bonang barung in loud sections, rebab in soft sections; instrument construction facts for bonang, rebab, suling, siter/celempung, kendhang and voices

- claim: In soft (quieter) sections of a piece, musicians listen to and follow the rebab's (a
  two-string spiked fiddle) melodic leadership; in loud sections, they follow the bonang barung (a
  rack of 10-14 small bronze kettle gongs in two rows, played with two beaters reaching across the
  rack); celempung (zither, described as larger than siter), suling (bamboo flute) and gendèr also
  belong to the "elaborating" instrument group; kendhang (laced double-headed drums) function as
  tempo/dynamic leader at one moment and dance accompaniment the next; kendhangan (drum patterns)
  and genderan (gendèr patterns) are more codified/fixed in conservatory teaching than the patterns
  of gambang, celempung or rebab; pesindhèn (solo female singer) and gérong (male chorus) are the
  ensemble's two named vocal roles.
- source: SUSILO-1999 (section)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: A veteran teaching pedagogue's (Hardja Susilo's) memoir/opening-address account of gamelan
  instrument functions and roles, aimed at an international festival/teaching audience rather than
  written as a dedicated organological or technical source.
- limitations: This is the only source read in this pass that discusses rebab, suling, siter/
  celempung, bonang barung/panerus, kendhang and pesindhèn/gérong at all; it gives function and role,
  not construction dimensions, tuning, bowing/plucking/embouchure technique, or acoustic detail for
  any of these instruments, which is why their cards on the guide page carry many `unresolved:` rows
  rather than the depth available for gendèr, gambang and saron.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: General Central Javanese gamelan practice as the author experienced and taught it
  across decades in the United States, drawing on his own Javanese training; not scoped to one named
  regional style the way the Supanggah/Perlman source explicitly compares Surakarta and Yogyakarta.

### JAV-11 Tuning order across the ensemble, starting from the gendèr as reference

- claim: Tuning proceeds outward from the gendèr (barung and panerus), checked against itself
  (lower and upper gembyang/octave of each pitch, for comfort); then the slenthem, tuned against the
  gendèr barung's lowest keys; then the demung, an octave above the slenthem; then the saron barung,
  against the gendèr panerus; the saron panerus's highest pitches (5, 6, high 1/7) lie above the
  gendèr panerus's own range and must be tuned "by ear" in octaves rather than checked against it.
- source: SUKARNO-1998 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: One named tuner's (Panggiyo Resowiguna's) account of his own working method, given in an
  interview about a specific retuning job.
- limitations: A single tuner's reported practice, not confirmed against a second maker's account or
  a written tuning manual; the "why gendèr first" rationale is reported as the tuner's own framing
  ("it acts as a mother"), not independently explained acoustically.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese gamelan tuning practice as taught and practised at STSI Surakarta
  by this one named maker/tuner.

### JAV-12 Regional tuning-interval differences (Cirebon, Solo, Yogyakarta, East Java)

- claim: One tuner reports that the interval between pitches 1 and 2 is wide in Cirebon pelog,
  narrower in Solonese tuning, and narrower again in East Javanese tuning; the interval between 6
  and 7 is wide in Cirebon and narrow in Solo; pitch 4 sits mid-way between 3 and 5 in Cirebon
  tuning but closer to 5 in Solo; the interval between 5 and 6 is wide in Yogyanese tuning.
- source: SUKARNO-1998 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: low
- scope: Impressionistic, comparative statements by one named tuner, given conversationally in an
  interview, not measured in cents or Hz for any specific instrument.
- limitations: No frequency or cents figures accompany any of these comparisons; they describe the
  tuner's general sense of regional style differences, not a specific measured set, and should not be
  read as a table of interval sizes.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Regional Javanese gamelan tuning practice (Cirebon, Solo/Surakarta, Yogyakarta,
  East Java) as characterised by one named Solonese tuner.

### JAV-13 The ceremonial weight of gamelan/the gong, and honorific treatment of named sets

- claim: A Javanese saying, "yen durung nggantung gong kuwi ora resmi" ("it's not official until the
  gong is hung"), reports that music, and specifically the gong, was traditionally considered
  necessary to make an occasion (the interview gives a child's birth as an example) formal; gamelan
  sets are still addressed by honorific names ("Kiyai" for some, "Nyai" for others) and treated with
  deference, particularly in keraton (palace) contexts, to the point that a composer's notated
  demand to strike a gong in an unconventional way ("kick the gongs") is described as something
  players will not simply do.
- source: HASTANTO-1989 (section, for the saying and its stated context); SUKARNO-1998 (full, for
  the honorific-naming and deference practice)
- source_type: tradition institution (Hastanto, then-director of ASKI); performer (Sukarno/
  Resowiguna)
- confidence: medium
- scope: Hastanto's account is given as a general cultural observation about the historical role of
  the arts (including music) in Javanese daily and court life, in an interview primarily about ASKI's
  institutional direction; Sukarno's account is a working tuner's direct experience of how gamelan
  sets are treated, given in the context of a specific retuning job that included a Cirebon set with
  its own keraton history.
- limitations: Neither source describes a specific restricted or declined repertoire for Central
  Javanese court/village gamelan of the kind this page otherwise covers; both establish general
  ceremonial weight and reverence rather than naming which pieces, if any, are off-limits, which is
  why the guide page's "Restricted and ceremonial repertoire" section leaves that specific question
  open.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Central Javanese gamelan generally, including keraton (palace) context
  specifically for the honorific-naming/deference claim.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

There is no 2.0 page for Javanese gamelan instruments in the pack; `CULTURALLY_SPECIFIC_INSTRUMENTS.md`
was the prior state for this instrument family, as for every tradition-specific instrument without
its own page. Nothing is being corrected; everything on the guide page is new coverage.
