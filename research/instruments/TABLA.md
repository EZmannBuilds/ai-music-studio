# Tabla: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries the
conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Nine David Courtney articles (chandrakantha.com) were read in full: strokes/bols, tabla making, the
pudi's construction, tuning (psychoacoustic method), repair and maintenance, the cyclic form, the
cadenza, the psychoacoustics of pitch, and the mridangam/tabla contrast piece. Several originally
appeared in the Percussive Arts Society's *Percussive Notes*, a peer-reviewed practitioner venue, and
Courtney is himself a performing and teaching tabla player (a practitioner/teacher source under
`CULTURALLY_SPECIFIC_INSTRUMENTS.md` step 1's hierarchy), which is what clears this page's gate
together with the academic acoustics sources below. C V Raman's 1920 Nature note and 1934/35 Proc.
Indian Acad. Sci. paper were both read in full; four later academic papers that re-measure or model
the loaded tabla membrane (Siddharthan, Chatterjee and Tripathi 1994; Malu and Siddharthan 2000;
Sathej and Adhikari 2008; Patranabis et al. 2015) were read at section depth (introduction, method and
relevant results; not every page of statistical detail). One NIME engineering paper (Kapur, Essl,
Davidson and Cook 2002) on an electronic tabla controller was read at section depth for its account of
the physical mechanism of hand technique, particularly the bāyāṅ's pitch bend. One essay on solo tabla
listening (Chordia, undated in the copy read) was read in full. James Kippen's *The Tabla of Lucknow*
and Thomas Rossing's *Acoustics of Drums* (Physics Today, 1992) were not opened; both are cited
secondhand, from their appearance in Courtney's own bibliographies, and are entered as
`standard-reference`, `not-read`.

## Records

### TABLA-01 Construction: dāyāṅ and bāyāṅ shells, and the multi-layer pudi
- claim: the dāyāṅ is a single carved wooden shell (teak, rosewood/shisham, occasionally jackwood);
  the bāyāṅ is a beaten and hammered metal bowl (most often brass); each head is a laminate of a
  reinforcing inner layer (bharti), a main membrane (maidan), and an outer annular ring (chat), bound
  into a woven hoop (gajara), with a permanent loaded black patch (syahi) applied in many thin,
  stone-polished, crack-networked layers.
- source: COURTNEY-MAKING (full); COURTNEY-PUDI (full); RAMAN-1935 (full)
- source_type: performer; academic acoustics
- confidence: high
- scope: general Hindustani tabla construction as documented by one performer/craftsman's own
  published account, corroborated in its broad outline (loaded membrane, sixteen-strap tensioning) by
  Raman's independent acoustic description of the same drum family
- limitations: Courtney documents specifically the Hyderabad/Deccan trade dialect and craft community;
  he states explicitly that terminology and some construction details vary regionally across India,
  and this page does not claim uniformity beyond what he documents.
- inference: none for the core construction facts; the claim that the syahi's crack network is what
  lets a rigid material flex with the membrane is stated directly by Courtney, not inferred.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani tabla, documented primarily from Deccan (Hyderabad-area) craft practice
  per Courtney; Raman's acoustic description is general to the drum family and not region-specific.

### TABLA-02 The five-harmonic model, and its correction
- claim: the dāyāṅ's loaded, damped circular membrane produces the first several vibrational modes
  grouped into an approximately harmonic series of five tones (fundamental plus four overtones); later
  re-measurement finds this only approximate, with the fundamental itself shifted out of exact integer
  ratio with the higher partials.
- source: RAMAN-1920 (full); RAMAN-1935 (full); SIDDHARTHAN-1994 (section); MALU-SIDDHARTHAN-2000
  (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: high
- scope: the dāyāṅ specifically (Raman's paper covers the Mridanga/right-hand drum family generally,
  including both the South Indian Mridanga and the Thabla as he calls it); the correction (shifted
  fundamental) is from a specific 1994 remeasurement study and a 2000 theoretical model built to
  reproduce it
- limitations: Raman's original figures came from period equipment and a small number of drums;
  Siddharthan et al. 1994 explicitly could not reproduce Raman's claim of eight suppressed higher
  overtones under their own loading calculations, and found only two, so the mechanism Raman proposed
  for *why* nine modes converge to five is itself not fully settled by the sources read here.
- inference: none; both the original claim and its correction are stated directly in the sources.
- tradition_scope: general to the loaded-membrane drum family (dāyāṅ, and, per Raman, the South Indian
  Mridanga); not specific to one gharana or player.

### TABLA-03 Tuning method: Naa-stroke tuning, and the extrapolated fundamental
- claim: the dāyāṅ is tuned by ear to the "Naa" stroke rather than the more clearly-pitched "Tun",
  because "Naa" is the stroke actually used in performance; the fundamental is often suppressed or
  absent in "Naa", so the perceived pitch is the ear's extrapolation from strong upper harmonics, which
  is a genuinely difficult skill; fine tuning is done primarily by striking the gajara hoop with a
  hammer rather than by adjusting lacing tension.
- source: COURTNEY-TUNING (full); COURTNEY-PSYCHO (full); COURTNEY-REPAIR (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: Courtney's own teaching and psychoacoustic-measurement experience, reported over roughly a
  ten-year teaching period at the time of the original 1991 publication; a general account, not scoped
  to one gharana
- limitations: Courtney's spectral measurements were made with period equipment (a Nakamichi
  cassette-era transducer and Hewlett-Packard spectrum analyser for the original 1991 work, a
  Macintosh-based digital setup for the later psychoacoustics piece); he notes himself that the
  interval between "Tun" and "Naa"'s apparent pitch varies drum to drum rather than being fixed; no
  general numeric interval is given.
- inference: none stated as fact beyond what Courtney reports directly.
- tradition_scope: general Hindustani tabla tuning practice as documented by one performer/teacher; not
  gharana-specific.

### TABLA-04 The bāyāṅ's continuous pitch bend mechanism
- claim: the bāyāṅ's open "Ga"/"Ghe" stroke can be pitch-bent in real time, after the attack, by the
  heel and wrist of the left hand: greater downward force on the head raises pitch, and moving the
  heel's contact point closer to the centre of the syahi also raises pitch; a player can glide pitch
  continuously and play melodic shapes this way.
- source: COURTNEY-PSYCHO (full); KAPUR-NIME-2002 (section)
- source_type: performer; academic (engineering/physical-modelling conference paper, NIME)
- confidence: medium
- scope: general Hindustani tabla technique, corroborated independently by a performer's account
  (Courtney, who names Zakir Hussain's melodic use of this technique as illustration) and by an
  engineering paper built specifically to model and reproduce the same mechanism for a physical
  controller
- limitations: neither source gives a measured cents-per-newton or cents-per-millimetre figure; both
  describe the mechanism qualitatively (force up, pitch up; contact point toward centre, pitch up).
  No number is available to state as a magnitude in the guide page, and none is invented there.
- inference: the guide page's recommendation to drive this as a continuous controller across a note's
  sustain, rather than only at onset, is this page's own extrapolation from the mechanism as described;
  neither source is itself a virtual-instrument programming guide.
- tradition_scope: general Hindustani tabla bāyāṅ technique; not gharana-specific in either source.

### TABLA-05 Bol vocabulary and stroke technique, including regional (Dilli/Purbi) variants
- claim: the tabla's stroke vocabulary is named at the level of the bol (Dhaa, Dhin, Ga, Ka, Kat, Naa,
  Na, Taa, Tak, Tee, TiRaKiTa, TiTa, Tin, Too, and more), each a physically specific hand action; several
  common bols have genuinely different Dilli (Delhi) and Purbi (Lucknow/Farukhabad/Benares) execution
  techniques producing different sounds, and performers commonly mix both within one composition.
- source: COURTNEY-BOLS (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: Courtney's own pedagogical material, explicitly presented as an introductory subset ("there
  are many more bols than are practical to give in this web site"); the Dilli/Purbi distinction is
  general across the gharanas he names
- limitations: Courtney states directly that the list is not exhaustive and that Roman-script spelling
  of bols is ambiguous; this page follows his transliteration and does not attempt Devanagari.
  Gharana-level technique differences beyond Dilli/Purbi and the one named Lucknow variant (on Taa) are
  not detailed in this source.
- inference: this page's summary that "each named bol is a physically distinct strike position... not
  a velocity variant of a smaller set" is a direct restatement of Courtney's own framing, not an
  extrapolation beyond it.
- tradition_scope: general Hindustani tabla technique across gharanas, with two named exceptions
  (Dilli-style vs Purbi-style technique; a specific Lucknow-gharana release variant on Taa), both
  attributed to their named school in the source.

### TABLA-06 Cyclic and cadential compositional forms
- claim: tabla composition falls into two mutually exclusive classes, cyclic (theka and its variants,
  kaida, rela, peshkar, gat, laggi and related minor forms) and cadential (mukhada, tihai, chakradar,
  mohara, paran, tukra/tukada, tipali, amad), distinguished by whether the material rolls along without
  resolving or moves toward resolution on the returning sam; forms are defined by independent, often
  overlapping criteria (bol, structure, function), which is why the same composition is often called
  different things by different musicians.
- source: COURTNEY-CYCLIC (full); COURTNEY-CADENZA (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: high
- scope: general Hindustani tabla compositional theory, explicitly synthesised by Courtney from
  multiple named prior sources (Stewart 1974, Kippen 1988, Shepherd 1976 and several Hindi-language
  treatises he cites), and cross-checked against his own decades of teaching; the multi-criteria
  account of nomenclature is presented as resolving genuine, long-standing confusion in the secondary
  literature, not as Courtney's own invention
- limitations: both articles are themselves secondary syntheses; the underlying ethnomusicological
  monograph most directly on this ground (Kippen 1988, on the Lucknow gharana specifically) was not
  read this pass, so gharana-specific nuance beyond what Courtney states explicitly (e.g. the
  Delhi/Benares kaida-vs-bant naming dispute, the Punjab gharana's shorter "B" mini-theme convention)
  is taken on his authority alone.
- inference: none beyond what is stated; the guide page's account of the tihai's formula-level
  structure follows Courtney's worked examples directly.
- tradition_scope: general Hindustani tabla, with specific named gharana attributions (Dilli origin of
  kaida; Benares's "bant"/"banti"; purbi origin of gat in Lucknow, Farukhabad and Benares practice;
  Punjab gharana's shorter mini-theme convention) kept as attributed rather than generalised.

### TABLA-07 Ensemble role: theka in accompaniment versus the nagma in a tabla solo
- claim: in accompaniment the tabla's central responsibility is holding the theka so the soloist and
  drummer can locate the cycle against each other, historically with little tolerated deviation; in a
  tabla solo, the same function is instead served by a nagma, a short repeated melody (usually on a
  secondary instrument) chosen to mirror the tāl's own subdivision.
- source: COURTNEY-CONTRAST (full); CHORDIA-SOLO (full)
- source_type: performer; performer/researcher essay
- confidence: medium
- scope: general Hindustani practice, contrasted explicitly against Carnatic mridangam practice (where
  the audience's own clapping, not a drummer's fixed pattern, carries the cyclic reference)
- limitations: Chordia's essay is a listener's guide rather than a peer-reviewed ethnomusicological
  source, and the specific example given (Ramesh Mishra's choice of nagma for a slow tintal piece) is
  one named performer's practice, not a claim about all performers; treated here as illustrative, not
  as a general rule beyond "a nagma is usually chosen to mirror the tāl's subdivision."
- inference: none stated as fact beyond the sources' own claims.
- tradition_scope: general Hindustani tabla accompaniment and solo practice; the contrast with Carnatic
  practice is Courtney's own comparative claim, corroborated independently by
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`'s existing sourcing on Carnatic tala's separate structure.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

This is a new page for 2.1; there was no 2.0 `TABLA.md` in the guide (the instrument was listed in
`HAND_PERCUSSION.md`'s routing table as "page in preparation in 2.1"). There is accordingly no prior
page's claims to correct. The one relevant correction this page makes to adjacent material is to
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`'s treatment of sam/khali/theka: that file already states, as
of this pass, that these are Hindustani terms and that Carnatic mridangam accompaniment is not built on
a theka; this page's ensemble_behavior row and "Performer interaction and ensemble role" section are
written to agree with that framing rather than to reintroduce a conflation.
