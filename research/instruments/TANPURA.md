# Tanpura: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Two academic-acoustics lines were read in full: C. V. Raman's 1921 paper, which is the founding
description of the jivari/jawari bridge mechanism shared by the tanpura, sitar and veena, and two
later lines that build on it directly: a high-speed-video experimental study of a single tanpura
string at IIT Kanpur (Pisharody and Gupta, 2018), and a pair of real-time physical-modelling papers
from Queen's University Belfast (van Walstijn, Bridges and Mehes, 2016; Bridges and van Walstijn,
2017) aimed at synthesis rather than measurement, read at section depth (the derivations of the
finite-difference and modal schemes were skimmed rather than worked through; the introduction,
qualitative results and conclusions were read closely). An ethnomusicological source, Weisser,
Demoucron and Leman (2012), was read in full: it reports fieldwork and player interviews at the ITC
Sangeet Research Academy, Kolkata, and gives the practice side the acoustics papers do not.

Two practitioner sources were read. Ravi Shankar's autobiography and sitar manual, *My Music, My
Life*, was read at section depth: the tamboura gets one page in the instruments chapter, which was
read in full, and the surrounding chapters on the sitar and Maihar training were read for context
but not exhaustively. Martin Spaink's practical tanpura-tuning article, written for a print journal
(*Samakalikam Sangeetham*) by a self-identified professional tanpura player and jivari-maker who
names his training under Ustad Sayeeduddin Dagar, was read in full; it is the only source here that
describes tuning practice, plucking rhythm and jiva placement from a working player's own hand.

No Carnatic-specific practitioner or institutional source was reached. Everything below that names
a tuning order, a term (jora, karaj, pancham), or a plucking convention is Hindustani and is scoped
as such; the underlying bridge physics (Raman, Pisharody and Gupta, the modelling papers) was
studied on instruments identified only as "tanpura," not attributed to either system, and is treated
as shared.

## Records

### TANPURA-01 The jivari/jawari bridge produces an overtone-rich sound by grazing contact, not ordinary string physics

- claim: the strings pass over a curved bridge surface (with a cotton or silk thread, jiva, inserted
  to fine-tune the grazing point) rather than leaving a sharp edge; this grazing contact suspends the
  ordinary rule that plucking a string at a node of a harmonic silences that harmonic (the
  Young-Helmholtz law), and instead produces a slow transfer of energy from the fundamental into a
  rich, evolving series of overtones.
- source: RAMAN-1921 (full); PISHARODY-GUPTA-2018 (full)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: high
- scope: the physical mechanism of the tanpura's bridge and jiva, as instrumented and photographed;
  the instruments studied are not identified by tradition.
- limitations: both sources study one string removed from a specific instrument in a laboratory
  setup, not a full instrument in playing context; Raman's 1921 account is qualitative and precedes
  modern spectral analysis, though Pisharody and Gupta's 2018 high-speed-video study confirms the
  same mechanism with instrumentation.
- inference: none for the mechanism itself; the claim that this is what listeners hear as the
  tanpura's characteristic "buzz" is the studies' own framing, not this page's addition.
- tradition_scope: general to the tanpura/tambura family; not attributed to Hindustani or Carnatic
  practice specifically by these sources.

### TANPURA-02 The jiva must sit within a narrow range of positions, and players find that range by ear

- claim: the cotton thread's position between string and bridge is sensitive: the jivari effect only
  occurs within a certain range of thread placement, and moving it within that range changes the
  character of the sound; setting it is a manual, by-ear process, not a fixed measurement.
- source: BRIDGES-2017 (section); VANWALSTIJN-2016 (section); SPAINK-2012 (full)
- source_type: academic acoustics (Bridges, van Walstijn); performer (Spaink)
- confidence: high
- scope: Bridges and van Walstijn model the sensitivity mathematically; Spaink describes the same
  sensitivity as a working player's tuning procedure, pulling the thread "ever so slowly" while
  listening for the harmonics to "open up."
- limitations: the modelling papers work from an idealised two-point or distributed contact model
  and do not claim to reproduce every maker's or player's practice; Spaink's account is one
  practitioner's method, stated as his own, not surveyed across other jivari-makers.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani (Spaink names Hindustani and Carnatic musicians he has tuned for, but
  the tuning description itself is his own practice).

### TANPURA-03 The characteristic drone sound builds in richness after the pluck rather than simply decaying, and is destroyed by plucking too hard

- claim: unlike a plainly decaying plucked string, a well-set tanpura's overtone content grows over
  roughly the first half-second after the pluck (a "precursor" effect whose spectral centre glides
  downward over time); this only happens within a narrow range of pluck amplitude, and the desired
  drone is lost if the string is plucked too hard.
- source: PISHARODY-GUPTA-2018 (full); VANWALSTIJN-2016 (section); SPAINK-2012 (full)
- source_type: academic acoustics (Pisharody and Gupta; van Walstijn et al.); performer (Spaink)
- confidence: high
- scope: Pisharody and Gupta measure this directly (spectrograms at 0.5 cm, 1 cm and 2.5 cm initial
  displacement, all on the same string); van Walstijn et al. describe the same precursor behaviour in
  their model and note it is "contrary to our normal experience of plucked strings."
- limitations: the amplitude figures (0.5 cm producing the desired pattern, degrading above that) are
  measurements of one string on one instrument in one lab setup, not a general playing-technique
  threshold that would carry the same centimetre figure to a different instrument.
- inference: connecting the acoustic finding to Spaink's practitioner language ("the strings should
  not know they have been plucked," a phrase he reports as common among musicians) is this page's
  synthesis of an acoustic measurement and a player's saying about the same effect; the two sources
  are independent and consistent, but the connection is drawn here, not stated jointly by either
  source.
- tradition_scope: the acoustic measurement is not tradition-attributed; Spaink's account is
  Hindustani.

### TANPURA-04 No frets; four (or five) metal strings, tuned by large pegs and fine-tuned by sliding beads, plucked continuously in rotation with no plectrum

- claim: the tanpura/tambura is an unfretted long-necked lute with (commonly) four playing strings,
  tuned with large pegs for coarse adjustment and sliding beads for fine adjustment, plucked one
  after another in a continuous cycle by the fingers of the right hand, without a plectrum.
- source: RAMAN-1921 (full); SHANKAR-1968 (section); SPAINK-2012 (full)
- source_type: academic acoustics (Raman); performer (Shankar; Spaink)
- confidence: high
- scope: Raman describes the four-string form; Shankar's one-page description of the tamboura in the
  Hindustani instrument family; Spaink describes four- and five-string instruments and the peg/bead
  tuning mechanism from a maker's perspective.
- limitations: Raman does not describe playing technique, only construction; Shankar's description is
  one paragraph in a book whose sitar chapters are far more detailed, and does not cover five-string
  instruments (which Spaink does).
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani for the playing description (Shankar, Spaink); the four-string
  construction Raman documents is not tradition-attributed.

### TANPURA-05 Hindustani tuning order pa-sa-sa-Sa, with a practitioner's stated variants and cautions

- claim: on a four-string Hindustani tanpura, the strings (in playing order) are the pancham (fifth,
  or another chosen svara depending on the raga), the paired jora tuned to the upper octave Sa, and
  the karaj or mandra, the low Sa; a five-string instrument allows more options; tuning a string to
  the third (gandhar) is specifically discouraged because the harmonics of the three Sa-strings
  already sound that pitch in several octaves.
- source: SPAINK-2012 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: one practitioner's stated convention and reasoning, offered as general Hindustani practice
  by a player who describes tuning tanpuras for "visiting master-musicians from Hindustani and
  Carnatic traditions," but documented here only in his own words.
- limitations: this is a single named source for the tuning order and its exceptions; it is not
  cross-checked against a second Hindustani practitioner or teacher, and `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/
  DRONE_TRADITIONS.md` already flags that tanpura tunings beyond Sa-Pa and Sa-Ma are to verify
  against a practitioner source. This record is that verification for the pa-sa-sa-Sa case and the
  gandhar caution; it does not extend to every raga-specific variant Spaink alludes to but does not
  enumerate.
- inference: none stated as fact beyond Spaink's own claims; the page carries the raga-by-raga
  variants (beyond the general order) as to-verify.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani, one practitioner (Martin Spaink).

### TANPURA-06 The drone's resonance and buzz serve a named Hindustani aesthetic of continuity and textural saturation, reported by players themselves

- claim: Hindustani musicians describe the taraf/jawari-style resonance (on the tanpura and on
  related chordophones) as bringing "beauty and richness" that would otherwise be "dry and lifeless";
  the tanpura's continuous sounding of the tonic is part of a broader aesthetic of continuity and
  textural saturation documented across Hindustani instrumental practice.
- source: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology (fieldwork and player interviews, ITC Sangeet Research Academy)
- confidence: medium
- scope: fieldwork conducted with named institutional grounding (ITC-SRA, Kolkata) and acknowledged
  informants (including Pandit Dhruba Ghosh); the quoted player language is reported by the
  researchers, not verified here against a second transcript.
- limitations: the paper's own analysis concentrates on the sarangi and sitar, with the tanpura's
  role discussed as context (the "essential drone" and the aesthetic argument) rather than as its own
  measured case; it is ethnomusicology correlated with acoustic analysis, not a tanpura-specific
  fieldwork study on its own.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani classical instrumental practice, as studied by Weisser, Demoucron and
  Leman at ITC-SRA and in Belgium.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

There is no 2.0 page for this instrument in the pack; `CULTURALLY_SPECIFIC_INSTRUMENTS.md` is the
prior state for every tradition-specific instrument without its own file, tanpura included. Nothing
is being corrected; everything here is new coverage.

- The internal construction and control model of the electronic tanpura or sruti box: its existence
  and use as a tuning reference is attested in passing (Pisharody and Gupta tuned their experimental
  string against "an electronic tanpura drone"), but no source describing how such a device is built
  or voiced was read.
- Recording practice for the tanpura (microphone placement, its interaction with the buzz spectrum)
  was not reached by any source read in this pass.
- Raman (1921) predates modern spectral-analysis instrumentation; his qualitative account is
  corroborated by Pisharody and Gupta (2018) using high-speed video and spectrograms, but the
  precise magnitude of "overtone dominance over the fundamental" he reports is descriptive, not a
  measured figure.
