# Ewe Dance Drums: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Sources attempted: David Locke's critical edition of Agbadza (reached and read at section depth
across its construction, ensemble-overview, bell, rattle, kagan, kidi, and sogo sections); Locke's
Music Theory Online article on Yewevu, including its "Learn More" excerpt page (reached, read at
section depth, deliberately restricted to general ensemble-instrumentation content rather than
Yewevu repertoire, per rule 5 of `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/INDEX.md`); C. K. Ladzekpo's "Drums and
Drumming" and two-part "Atsimevu" pages (reached, read in full; both are short, single-topic pages);
Jeryl Johnston's comparative variation study (reached, read at section depth, restricted to its
agbadza ("Miwua Agbo Mayi") and Drum Gahu material, since its other two examples, a Yoruba dùndún
piece and an Akan adowa performance, are outside this page's tradition and were not used); Kofi
Agawu's 2006 JAMS article, attempted and blocked by a Cloudflare bot-check page rather than the
article text, so it is cited only as `standard-reference`, not read.

Read depth was judged by whether the passage that supports a specific claim was actually read
(`section`) versus only a title, abstract, or blocked landing page being available (`not-read`).
Ladzekpo's two short pages were short enough to read in full rather than by section.

## Records

### EWE-01 Ensemble instrumentation and general construction

- claim: The Anlo-Ewe ensemble covered here consists of the double bell gankogui, the gourd rattle
  axatse, the support drum kagan, the response drum kidi, and one or two lead drums (sogo and/or
  atsimevu); drums are traditionally a skin membrane over a carved or coopered cylindrical body.
- source: LADZEKPO-AMD (full); LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010 (section)
- source_type: pedagogy; ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: Anlo-Ewe dance-drumming generally, as described by a practising Anlo-Ewe teacher (Ladzekpo)
  and by Locke's general ensemble description; not specific to one named piece for this record.
- limitations: Ladzekpo's site material predates 2026 by decades (internally copyrighted 1995) and
  is not dated as current practice; exact instrument counts and names vary by piece and ensemble, as
  the page itself notes for the high-pitched drum pair.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: Anlo-Ewe dance-drumming generally

### EWE-02 Gankogui: double bell, one stick, timeline role

- claim: Gankogui is a double bell of two joined, differently pitched lobes, held in the weak hand
  and struck with a single straight wooden stick; it states the ensemble's reference timeline
  pattern and is not documented as varying within a piece.
- source: LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section); LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: agbadza specifically for the timeline/unvarying-part claim; general Ewe ensemble
  instrumentation for the bell's construction.
- limitations: no source read describes bell muting technique, dynamic timbre change, or
  conventional recording practice; bell material (forging method, metal) was not stated in any
  source read.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-03 Axatse: construction and two-stroke technique

- claim: Axatse is a gourd covered with an external net of beads or seeds; it is played with a
  repeating downstroke (against the thigh, in unison with the bell) and upstroke (against the
  weak-hand palm, between bell strokes) pattern, normally by several players at once.
- source: LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010 (section); LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section); LADZEKPO-AMD (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology; pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: agbadza for the specific kinesthetic pattern and its relation to the bell and to a
  three-then-two handclap pattern; general Ewe instrumentation for construction.
- limitations: dynamic and timbre change with strike force is inferred by analogy to other struck
  idiophones, not separately documented; no source gives a stroke count or player count for a
  specific performance.
- inference: dynamic_timbre_change is generalised from struck-idiophone physics, not measured for
  axatse specifically.
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-04 Kagan and kidi: construction, bounce/press strokes

- claim: Kagan and kidi are single-headed barrel drums played with two wooden sticks; kidi's
  vocabulary is built from bounce (resonant, open) and press (muted) strokes, and kagan's part is a
  comparatively simple, dry, offbeat-marking figure.
- source: LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: agbadza specifically; Locke's book documents this drum pair in the most technical detail
  of any source read for this page (an "Inventory of Response Drum Themes" and per-stroke
  accentuation analysis).
- limitations: head-tensioning/tuning mechanism, exact head material, and conventional recording
  practice were not found; kagan's stroke vocabulary is documented in less depth than kidi's.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-05 Kidi answers the lead drum's calls: correction to the 2.0 page

- claim: Kidi does not play one unvarying, non-responsive ostinato; it selects among a memorised
  inventory of response themes in relation to the lead drum's specific calls and to the bell phrase.
  A prior (2.0) version of this pack's material stated that supporting Ewe parts do not improvise;
  that overstated the case for kidi specifically.
- source: LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: agbadza's kidi part specifically, as documented through Locke's "Inventory of Response Drum
  Themes" and "Relationship of Response Drum Themes to Bell Phrase" analysis.
- limitations: this is bounded, repertoire-based selection among known themes, not free
  improvisation; the corrected claim should not be overstated in the other direction. Bell, rattle
  and kagan are not shown to have this same responsive quality in the sources read.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-06 Sogo: bare-hand four-stroke vocabulary, lead role

- claim: In agbadza, sogo is played with two bare hands using four named strokes (ga, de, dzi, tsa),
  each a distinct hand/finger technique, contact point, pitch, and typical loudness; sogo is the
  lead drum and ensemble leader, with the ensemble's greatest freedom for musical invention, and
  closes a piece with a fixed ending-signal phrase.
- source: LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: agbadza specifically, including Locke's Table 14 stroke inventory and his discussion of
  sogo's ending signal and dancer-cueing rolls.
- limitations: this bare-hand technique is not universal to every "sogo"-named drum; see EWE-07.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-07 Sogo/agbivu technique varies by repertoire: stick versus bare hand

- claim: The Yewevu ensemble's comparable lead drum (called agbivu and/or sogo there) is described
  as played with two straight sticks, or one stick and one bare hand, in contrast to agbadza's
  bare-hand sogo technique.
- source: LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: the specific instrumentation description on the Yewevu project's "Learn More" page, cited
  here only for this general organological fact, not for Yewevu repertoire content.
- limitations: only instrumentation-level description was read for this ensemble, not a stroke
  vocabulary comparable to agbadza's Table 14; the source does not explain why the technique differs
  by repertoire.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: Yewevu ensemble instrumentation only (repertoire itself excluded, per rule 5)

### EWE-08 Atsimevu: construction and multi-technique pitch vocabulary

- claim: Atsimevu is a roughly four-and-a-half-foot carved cylindrical drum tilted on a stand
  (vudetsi), played with bare hand, fingers, and stick at the head's centre and periphery, and with
  a stick against the body itself (vukogo), producing a documented series of eight-plus named
  pitches/timbres plus two combined ones, taught as vocables and read as "drum speech" (vugbe).
- source: LADZEKPO-AMD (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: high
- scope: atsimevu specifically, as documented by C. K. Ladzekpo, a practising Anlo-Ewe master
  drummer, writing in his own voice about his own instrument and technique.
- limitations: a single-author, self-published source; no independent second source corroborates the
  exact vocable set or technique names, though it is internally detailed and consistent. No absolute
  pitch, head-tuning mechanism, or recording-practice information is given.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: Anlo-Ewe atsimevu practice as taught by C. K. Ladzekpo

### EWE-09 Standard pattern and bell-phrase framing (weak support)

- claim: The bell's seven-stroke pattern across a twelve-pulse cycle functions as the ensemble's
  reference timeline.
- source: LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section); AGAWU-2006 (not-read)
- source_type: ethnomusicology; standard reference
- confidence: low
- scope: agbadza's bell phrase specifically, per Locke's own transcription and metric-matrix
  analysis (Table 8, Figure 21); the wider "standard pattern" literature (Agawu) could not be read
  for this pass.
- limitations: Agawu 2006 is cited by attribution only; its content is not independently confirmed
  here, and this claim should not be strengthened past standard-reference on Agawu's account until
  it is read. The theory of the pattern (its four-and-six matrix reading) belongs to
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/EWE_DANCE_DRUMMING.md`, not to this instrument page, and is not repeated
  here beyond what is needed to say the bell does not vary.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: agbadza (Anlo-Ewe)

### EWE-10 Repertoire distinction: agbadza/Gahu (secular) versus Yewevu (restricted)

- claim: Agbadza is public social and funerary dance-drumming and Drum Gahu is purely recreational;
  both are openly taught and published. Yewevu is the music of the Yeve religious order, described
  by its own documenter as shrouded in secrecy, withheld from the uninitiated, and guarded by
  practitioners; this page treats it as restricted and does not use its repertoire content as
  material to imitate.
- source: LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010 (section); LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: as documented by Locke, whose own account of Yewevu is itself a staged, folkloric-troupe
  arrangement several removes from shrine practice, offered by agreement with his teacher without
  divulging shrine-restricted content.
- limitations: this page's judgement that agbadza and Gahu are not similarly restricted rests on
  Locke's own framing of them as public, taught repertoire; no separate source was read that assesses
  agbadza's or Gahu's restriction status directly, though nothing in either source suggests
  restriction.
- inference: the general policy line ("this page declines Yewevu repertoire and offers agbadza/Gahu
  instead") is this page's application of `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/INDEX.md` rule 5 to what the
  sources describe, not a claim from the sources themselves.
- tradition_scope: Yewevu (Yeve religious order) versus agbadza/Gahu (Anlo-Ewe secular repertoire)

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

- **Supporting Ewe parts do not improvise (2.0 claim) → corrected.** Locke's Agbadza documents kidi
  choosing among a memorised inventory of response themes in relation to the lead drum's calls and
  the bell phrase; this is bounded, repertoire-based response, not free improvisation, but it is not
  inert non-improvising repetition either. See EWE-05. [LOCKE-AGBADZA-2012]
- **"West African polyrhythm" as one undifferentiated bucket → decomposed.** This page, together with
  `JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md` and the two new `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/` files, replaces the single
  `WEST_AFRICAN_POLYRHYTHM.md` file's treatment of Ewe and Mande material as one entry; the two
  traditions have different instruments, ensembles, construction, and technique, documented on their
  own pages.
- **A single "sogo" technique → repertoire-dependent.** The 2.0 material did not distinguish
  agbadza's bare-hand sogo technique from the stick or stick-and-hand technique documented for the
  Yewevu ensemble's comparable drum. See EWE-07. [LOCKE-YEWEVU-2010]
