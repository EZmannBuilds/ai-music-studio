# Great Highland bagpipe: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries the
conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Four sources were read at section depth or more. Ewan MacPherson's 1998 pitch-and-scale article,
published in the pipe-band magazine New Zealand Pipeband, was read in full and is the strongest
single source for this family: it reports chanter and drone pitch measurements across a century,
gives a cents table for the chanter scale, and names competing tuning schools for two notes (D and
high G) with their sources. `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/DRONE_TRADITIONS.md` already cites this same
article for the just-intonation-Mixolydian framing and the pitch-rise figures; this page's citations
were independently verified by reading the article directly in this pass, not copied from that file.
A Bagpipe News trade-publication article on drone and chanter acoustics was read at section depth (its
acoustic-mechanism content; its speculative unsourced prehistory narrative was set aside, see below).
A self-published piping tutor by a named instructor (Peter L. Heineman) was read at section depth for
its embellishment chapters (birl, grip/leumluath, taorluath, D throw). Piping Press's "Analysis: More
on Fingering Old and New," edited by piping journalist and historian Robert Wallace, was read in full;
it is built almost entirely from direct quotations and testimony from named teachers and players
(Dale Brown, RG Hardie, Duncan Watson relaying Seumas MacNeill -- then editor of the Piping Times
magazine and a former Principal of the College of Piping -- Patrick Molard naming his own teachers
Robert Brown and Robert Nicol, and an account of Donald MacPherson's competition performance), and is
this family's strongest practitioner/institution-tier source for embellishment technique
specifically, since it documents real, named disagreement about exact execution rather than
presenting one invented standard.

One caution about the Bagpipe News article: its opening sections narrate a speculative, unsourced
prehistory of how bagpipes might have evolved from a "primitive man" blowing across a reed by a
stream. This is not treated as fact anywhere on the page; only the article's acoustic-mechanism
content (how a drone behaves as a stopped pipe, how the chanter's conical bore differs) and its
historical pitch-standard figures (which read as a compressed, uncited survey rather than invention)
are cited, and even the latter are treated cautiously given the source cites no primary reference for
them.

## Records

### BP-01 Chanter and drone pitch have risen over the past century, and are far sharper than A440
- claim: the Great Highland bagpipe's chanter and drones are tuned to a pitch called "A" that is
  substantially sharper than concert A440, and that pitch has risen over roughly the past century:
  one measured chanter in 1885 at about 441 Hz; an average of several chanters at about 459 Hz in the
  mid-1950s; modern (1990s) chanters typically tuning between 470 and 480 Hz for low A.
- source: MACPHERSON-1998
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: medium
- scope: reported as a trend across named measurement studies MacPherson cites (1885, mid-1950s,
  1990s); not this page's own measurement.
- limitations: each figure is one dated measurement or survey average, not a current, live figure;
  MacPherson's own 1990s figure is now roughly three decades old, so a 2026 chanter's actual pitch is
  unverified by this page. A second source read this pass (Bagpipe News, 2020) separately states "low
  A pitched at about 459 cps" as the "current" figure at that time of writing, which is lower than
  MacPherson's 1990s figure and is not itself cited to a specific measurement study; the two sources
  disagree on what "current" pitch is, which itself supports treating any single number as dated
  rather than definitive.
- inference: none for the reported figures; noting that the two sources disagree, and drawing "treat
  any figure as a date's measurement" from that disagreement, is this page's own conclusion.
- tradition_scope: general Great Highland bagpipe practice, drawn from named measurement studies of
  multiple chanters/pipers rather than one named player's instrument.

### BP-02 The chanter scale is a nine-note Mixolydian-like scale, with "C" and "F" sharp of their piano equivalents
- claim: the chanter's nine notes (low G, low A, B, C, D, E, F, high G, high A) sound, against a
  piano, closest to a major scale with a flattened seventh (the Mixolydian mode starting from the
  chanter's low A), except that the "C" and "F" are themselves closer to C-sharp and F-sharp than to
  natural C and F -- traditional pipe notation does not write these sharps, because the instrument
  never plays the natural versions.
- source: MACPHERSON-1998
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: high
- scope: general to the instrument's fixed nine-note chanter.
- limitations: none of substance; this is a description of the chanter's fixed note set, not a
  disputed or varying claim.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: general.

### BP-03 D and high G are tuned by more than one named scheme, and current practice has shifted
- claim: not all chanter intervals are equally settled. A simple whole-number-ratio (just-intonation)
  Mixolydian scheme tunes D to a 4:3 ratio above low A and high G to 16:9. A 1954 study by J. Lenihan
  and Seumas MacNeill of eighteen pipe chanters found Ds tuned sharper, to 27:20, and high G to 9:5 --
  also whole-number ratios, so also "just" in the strict sense, but a different scheme. MacPherson
  reports that analysis of more recent recordings by named soloists shows a shift back toward the
  simple just D (4:3) and toward a flatter high G, around a 7:4 ratio he calls "Harmonic" because its
  harmonics coincide with the seventh harmonic of the bass drone; low G is often tuned an octave below
  at 7:8. High A is reported as typically tuned 10 to 30 cents flat of a true octave above the drones,
  which MacPherson attributes to convention or taste (possibly to keep the note audible against the
  drones, or as insurance against reed sharpening during a performance) rather than to a physical
  necessity.
- source: MACPHERSON-1998
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: medium
- scope: the 1954 study is named and scoped to eighteen chanters measured by Lenihan and MacNeill; the
  "recent recordings" shift is MacPherson's own analysis of named soloists' recordings, not a
  separately published study MacPherson cites by name.
- limitations: MacPherson's own "Harmonic" high-G naming and 7:4 figure is presented as his own
  synthesis from listening to recordings, not as a separately peer-reviewed or published figure; the
  underlying 1954 study was not independently read this pass, only MacPherson's summary of it.
- inference: none for what MacPherson states; this page does not independently verify the 1954 study.
- tradition_scope: general Great Highland bagpipe tuning practice as surveyed by MacPherson, who
  explicitly frames this as a matter of school and era rather than one settled answer.

### BP-04 The drone behaves acoustically as a stopped pipe; the chanter's conical bore gives it both odd and even harmonics
- claim: a Highland bagpipe drone, driven by a single reed at one end and closed at that end while
  open at the other, behaves acoustically as a stopped pipe, whose harmonic content is dominated by
  odd-numbered harmonics (with even harmonics present only because the stopped condition is not
  perfectly ideal in practice). The chanter's conical bore is the acoustic reason it produces both odd
  and even harmonics, unlike the drone; because both odd and even harmonics from the chanter's note
  can coincide with the drone's own (largely odd) harmonic series, the chanter and drones can either
  reinforce each other as a tuned chord or clash, which is what a piper is listening for when tuning
  by ear.
- source: BAGPIPENEWS-TUNING-2020
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: general acoustic description of the instrument's two sound sources (chanter, drone), stated
  as general acoustics rather than scoped to one named chanter or drone reed.
- limitations: the source is a trade-publication "hints and tips" article by a named author, not a
  peer-reviewed acoustics paper; the underlying physics (stopped-pipe odd-harmonic dominance, conical
  bore giving a fuller harmonic series) is standard wind-instrument acoustics, but this page has not
  independently verified it against a dedicated acoustics text this pass (`FLETCHER-ROSSING-1998` is
  in the register as `not-read`, cited only where the guide page marks a claim standard-reference).
- inference: none for the mechanism as stated; the framing "this is what a piper is listening for" is
  the source's own explanation, not this page's addition.
- tradition_scope: general.

### BP-05 Because the chanter's sound is continuous, gracenote figures are how notes are articulated and separated, and their exact execution genuinely varies by teacher and era
- claim: the chanter cannot be silenced by the player's breath the way a wind instrument with a
  free-standing reed can simply stop blowing (the bag maintains pressure), so a repeated note or a
  clean note-start needs a gracenote figure rather than a breath articulation. Several such figures are
  named and taught with specific finger sequences: doublings (a family of movements, one per note, each
  a specific short gracenote figure); the birl (several alternative execution methods exist -- tap,
  down-up, tap-and-pull, tap-and-push -- all producing a Low A, Low G, Low A figure); the "great birl"
  (a birl with an added high-G gracenote); the leumluath, also called the grip (Low G, D gracenote,
  Low G, then the melody note); the taorluath (a leumluath/grip with an added E gracenote on Low A);
  and the D throw (Low A, Low G, then a D-C-D figure ending on D). Beyond these agreed-on basic shapes,
  real, named disagreement exists among top teachers and players about exact execution -- for example,
  whether a taorluath or a D throw on the note D should include an additional D gracenote (as Donald
  MacPherson famously played, and was controversially marked down for, in a competition judged by
  Evan MacRae and Donald Morrison but defended by Andrew Pitkeathly) or a B gracenote instead (as
  Patrick Molard's own teachers, Robert Brown and Robert Nicol, taught him), and whether a "throw" on D
  is better understood as its own named movement or as a species of doubling (as RG Hardie taught).
- source: HEINEMAN-TUTOR (figure definitions); PIPINGPRESS-FINGERING-2020 (named variation)
- source_type: pedagogy; tradition institution
- confidence: medium
- scope: Heineman's tutor gives one consistent, internally coherent teaching sequence for a beginner;
  Piping Press's article is explicitly about documented disagreement among named, prominent teachers
  and competition judges over decades of practice, not a single standard.
- limitations: Heineman's tutor is self-published rather than issued by a piping institution as such,
  though it is a structured, named-author pedagogical text; this page treats it as a good source for
  the basic shape of each figure while treating Piping Press's testimony as the better source for the
  real range of practice, per this addendum's rule to say whose practice a claim describes.
- inference: connecting "the chanter's continuous sound" to "gracenotes function as articulation" is
  this page's own synthesis of the mechanism (bag-driven continuous reed) with the technique
  (gracenote figures at repeated or accented notes); no single source read this pass states that
  causal link in those exact terms, though both halves are separately stated by sources read this
  pass.
- tradition_scope: Heineman's definitions are general pedagogy for a beginner; the named-teacher
  variation in Piping Press is scoped explicitly to the individual teachers and players named (Robert
  Brown, Robert Nicol, RG Hardie, Seumas MacNeill, Donald MacPherson, Patrick Molard), not stated as
  representative of all piping practice.

### BP-06 Ceòl mòr/piobaireachd and canntaireachd are named, but their history is not independently verified this pass
- claim: piobaireachd (also called ceòl mòr, "great music") is the Highland bagpipe's extended,
  formal solo repertoire, historically taught and memorised through canntaireachd, a system of sung
  vocables representing the tune and its gracenotes, used in the absence of a dedicated written
  notation for the style.
- source: BAGPIPENEWS-TUNING-2020
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: low
- scope: general naming and description of the repertoire and its transmission method.
- limitations: the source's account of this repertoire's history (including a specific causal claim
  that competitive piping's Culloden-era suppression narrative explains later technical developments)
  is presented without citation within the article itself, and reads as a compressed popular account
  rather than a documented history; this page repeats only the naming (piobaireachd/ceòl mòr,
  canntaireachd as a vocalised mnemonic system) and not the causal historical narrative, which is
  marked to-verify.
- inference: none for the terminology itself; declining to repeat the uncited causal historical
  narrative is this page's own editorial caution, not itself sourced.
- tradition_scope: general terminology, not scoped to a named period, region or teacher beyond what
  the source itself (thinly) states.
