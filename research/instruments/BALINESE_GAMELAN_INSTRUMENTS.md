# Balinese Gamelan Instruments: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Seven sources were read: two full-length practitioner-scholar articles by Wayne Vitale (one solo,
one co-authored with Marc Paelinck), a full-length interview with Balinese composer I Wayan Sinti
(conducted with Michael Tenzer, another named practitioner, participating), a full-length player's
handbook prepared for a New Zealand university course, a full-length set of ethnomusicological notes
on Balinese ensembles presented by a named ethnomusicologist, and two academic acoustics sources
(one read in full, one at section depth) measuring paired-tuning ("ombak") beat rates. All were
located as already-downloaded files in the assigned scratch folder; none required a new web fetch in
this pass. A second Vitale-authored interview (with I Wayan Dibia, on music and dance) and two
further Balungan articles in the same folder (Devereaux's Hastanto interview and Widiyanto's gambang
method, both cited on the Javanese page instead) were not used as sources for this page because their
content is Javanese or not directly about the instruments this page covers.

## Records

### BAL-01 Kotekan: structure, named figuration types, and the polos/sangsih relationship

- claim: Kotekan (fast interlocking figuration) divides melodic elaboration between two named parts,
  polos and sangsih, commonly but only approximately described as the on-beat and off-beat parts;
  in nyog cag figuration this is straightforwardly true (polos on the beat, sangsih filling the
  offbeats); in nyok cok figuration the two parts share anticipatory tones just before the pokok
  (core melody) moves; in kotekan telu ("three") figuration a three-tone cell shares a pivot tone
  between the parts, and either part may land on the beat depending on the cell's position relative
  to the pokok; in kotekan empat ("four") figuration the two parts share no tone, but their outer
  tones (roughly a fifth apart) sound together, producing an emergent third rhythm from their
  coincidence; kotekan is elaboration around the pokok, not the primary melody, meeting it in unison
  or at the octave at structurally important points and departing from it between them; the Balinese
  describe kotekan as the "flowers" (bunga) of the pokok's "branches."
- source: VITALE-1990 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: high
- scope: Wayne Vitale's own technical analysis, illustrated with notated examples from named gong
  kebyar repertoire (Teruna Jaya, Jauk, Gabor, Legong Keraton, Genta Anyar by I Wayan Sujana, and
  others), written from his training and performance experience in both Balinese and Western music.
- limitations: Vitale's article explicitly scopes its detailed analysis to gong kebyar, noting
  interlocking technique is used "to a lesser degree" in semar pegulingan and appears, in related
  but structurally different form, in gamelan gambang; the article does not claim the same figuration
  taxonomy for those other genres, and this page does not extend it there either.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gamelan gong kebyar, the modern (turn-of-the-20th-century-onward) Balinese
  style; explicitly not claimed for older or other Balinese genres beyond the qualified exception
  noted above.

### BAL-02 Gangsa construction, resonator behaviour, and damping technique

- claim: Gangsa (pemade, kantilan) are ten-keyed bronze metallophones, each key suspended by leather
  strap over an individually tuned bamboo resonator; a properly tuned resonator enormously lengthens
  a key's sustain (over a minute undamped), demonstrated by decoupling the resonator with a paper
  shim, which leaves a strong attack but a fast-fading, barely audible fundamental; playing technique
  uses a hard wooden mallet in one hand and precise damping with the other, timed so a note neither
  bleeds into the next (damped too late) nor leaves a gap (damped too early); this damping precision
  underlies the ability to play kotekan at its documented tempos.
- source: VITALE-1990 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: high
- scope: Vitale's own construction and technique description, given as background for the kotekan
  analysis; the ten-key, two-octave count is stated as standard for "most modern gong kebyar gamelan."
- limitations: Construction dimensions (case size, key size, resonator diameter) are not given, only
  key count, octave span and the resonator-decoupling demonstration; the source does not measure
  sustain time or damping-precision timing acoustically, only describes it qualitatively.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gong kebyar gangsa (pemade, kantilan) specifically, as generalised across "most
  modern" sets by a practitioner with broad Balinese gamelan experience, not scoped to one named set.

### BAL-03 Paired tuning (ombak): mechanism, typical ranges by ensemble type, and octave-treatment methods

- claim: Nearly every Balinese metallophone is tuned in pairs, a lower pengumbang and a higher
  pengisep, the frequency difference producing the beating called ombak; typical documented ombak
  ranges by ensemble type: gender wayang 3-6 Hz, semar pagulingan 6-8 Hz, gong kebyar 7-10 Hz,
  angklung 6-10 Hz; reyong is conventionally tuned to pengumbang and trompong to pengisep when paired
  (some tuners reverse this); a single ugal is tuned to pengumbang, and where two exist, the lead
  instrument is the pengumbang; keeping ombak consistent across octaves is not automatic under naive
  exact-octave tuning (which would double the beat rate each octave up), so tuners instead compress
  the pengisep's octave, stretch the pengumbang's octave (the more traditional method), or stretch
  both (a more "aggressive," South-Bali-associated sound).
- source: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: high
- scope: A workshop report from a Bali Summer School course taught by Wayne Vitale (director of a
  named American Balinese gamelan group), co-written with Marc Paelinck, covering gong kebyar and
  semar pagulingan tuning specifically; the typical-ombak-range table cites Michael Tenzer's
  monograph on gong kebyar for further detail not itself read in this pass.
- limitations: The ombak-range table gives typical values "according to the tuner's taste," not a
  fixed standard; the workshop covered only gong kebyar and semar pagulingan in the tuning-order
  portion, so the reference-tuning process itself (setting the initial interval structure) is
  described as outside the report's scope and left to Tenzer's book, not read here.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Balinese gamelan tuning practice as taught in one named workshop (Codarts
  Rotterdam's Bali Summer School, 2006) by a practitioner with extensive Balinese gamelan tuning
  experience; the ombak-range table's angklung and gender wayang figures are given as general typical
  values, not verified against a named angklung or gender wayang set in this source.

### BAL-04 Measured ombak beat rates on named gamelan sets

- claim: Measured beat rates on one University of Hawaii gamelan set (Balinese, gong-kebyar-type)
  ran approximately 6.3-7.2 Hz on jegogan, 6.3-8.1 Hz on calung, and 5.6-8.95 Hz across sampled
  gangsa pitches (both low and high registers measured), with no paired beat rate recorded for that
  set's reyong (its pitches were measured but not reported as a pengumbang/pengisep pair); a larger
  survey measuring eleven named Balinese gong kebyar sets in Bali and Japan (owned by conservatories,
  universities and named individual performers, made by named Balinese makers including I Wayan
  Beratha and I Wayan Sukarta) reports interference beat frequencies "generally tuned between 5 Hz
  and 10 Hz," with absolute pitch and interval size differing by region and period.
- source: HEARON-VANTROYER-2008 (full); SHIOKAWA-2016 (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: medium
- scope: Hearon and Van Troyer directly measured one specific University of Hawaii gamelan set for
  an electroacoustic composition project, recording, sampling and analysing frequencies with the
  Max/MSP fiddle~ object; Shiokawa, Umeda, Minagawa and Kartawan measured eleven named gong kebyar
  sets across Bali and Japan for an acoustics survey published by Nihon University, giving fundamental
  frequencies and citing the 5-10 Hz range as consistent with earlier literature they reference (not
  independently re-derived here from their own eleven-set data table, which was not read in the
  section-depth portion of this pass).
- limitations: The Hawaii-set measurement is one instrument, not a survey, and its reyong result
  (no clear paired beat rate) may reflect that particular reyong's own tuning rather than a general
  fact about reyong tuning practice; the Nihon University report's eleven-set survey was read at
  section depth (methodology, set descriptions, and the summary 5-10 Hz claim), not at the depth of
  its full per-set pitch tables, so this page does not cite specific per-set frequency figures from
  it beyond the general range.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gong kebyar-type Balinese gamelan specifically; the Hawaii set and the eleven
  surveyed sets are all gong kebyar or gong-kebyar-descended instruments, not older genres.

### BAL-05 Jegogan and calung/jublag: construction, legato-damping technique, and register/function

- claim: Jegogan (bass, five keys) and calung/jublag (tenor, five keys, an octave above jegogan) are
  played with a two-handed legato technique: the playing hand's mallet starts a note and the other
  hand grasps the key to stop it exactly as the next note starts; calung is struck with a rubber-
  faced (soft) mallet giving a sustained, near-attackless, humming tone, audibly different from
  gangsa's bright hard-mallet attack even where their ranges overlap; jegogan plays very sparsely,
  reinforcing the metre; calung/jublag plays the unadorned pokok (core melody) the rest of the
  ensemble elaborates around.
- source: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 (full); VITALE-1990 (full, for the soft-mallet/attackless-tone
  contrast with gangsa specifically)
- source_type: pedagogy (handbook); performer (Vitale)
- confidence: medium
- scope: The handbook is prepared teaching material for a specific continuing-education course
  (Victoria University of Wellington, supported by the New Zealand School of Music and Gareth Farr);
  Vitale's tonal-contrast observation is given as background to his kotekan analysis, not as a
  dedicated study of jegogan/calung construction.
- limitations: Neither source gives resonator dimensions or measured decay times for jegogan/calung
  specifically (unlike the detailed dimensions Marsudi's source gives for a Javanese gendèr panerus);
  the "very sparsely" and "unadorned" characterisations are qualitative, not quantified against a
  specific notated piece in these sources.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gong kebyar jegogan and calung/jublag, as taught in one named New Zealand
  teaching context (handbook) and described generally by a practitioner (Vitale) with broad Balinese
  gamelan experience.

### BAL-06 Reyong, trompong and the gong/ketuk/ceng-ceng punctuation-and-timekeeping group: construction and technique

- claim: Reyong is a horizontal row of small tuned bronze gong-kettles (twelve pots in one documented
  ensemble listing), played by four musicians with two cord-wrapped mallets each, striking pots on
  their boss for melody and on their shoulder for a percussive sound (kecek), with careful soundless-
  mallet damping between melodic strikes; trompong is a larger, lower-pitched version of reyong,
  played by one musician, and is not always included in a gong kebyar ensemble (one documented
  teaching set excludes it, grouping it with rebab and suling as "not used in this class"); the
  hanging-gong hierarchy (gong wadon/lanang, kempur, kemong, klentong) and ketuk (also kempli/kajar)
  mark the ensemble's metre and pulse, with klentong characteristically struck with the mallet's
  wooden handle for its "tong" sound and ketuk continuously hand-damped for a dry, non-resonant,
  metronomic tone; ceng-ceng (cymbals) fill faster subdivisions (documented as roughly four strokes
  per beat) alongside ketuk's slower pulse.
- source: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 (full)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: One prepared teaching handbook's description of the instruments used in its own course,
  presented as general gong kebyar practice.
- limitations: Pot counts, mallet materials and specific technique details are given for the
  instruments as taught in this one course; the handbook itself notes trompong, rebab and suling are
  not used in that particular class, so their inclusion or omission in gong kebyar generally is not
  settled by this source alone (the omission is corroborated for trompong specifically by
  PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010's association of trompong with the semar pegulingan ensemble instead).
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gong kebyar as taught in one named New Zealand continuing-education course; the
  twelve-pot reyong count is corroborated separately by SINTI-1985's ensemble listing.

### BAL-07 Named colotomic metric scheme for one eight-beat gong kebyar phrase

- claim: In a documented eight-beat measure, the petuk and gangsa giing sound every beat; the
  djublag sounds on beats 2, 4, 6 and 8; the kempul on beats 2 and 6; the kenong (a middle-register
  instrument in this scheme) on beat 4; the djegog on beats 4 and 8; and the gong on beat 8 — giving
  a concrete, named instance of how Balinese colotomic instruments are disposed across a metric cycle.
- source: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: Presented as a general illustrative example ("this gives some idea of the metric
  disposition of the gamelan instruments") by the source, not tied to one named piece.
- limitations: This is one documented scheme for one metric length (an eight-beat "tabuh"), not a
  survey of all possible colotomic dispositions; the source states beat counts in a tabuh are always
  even (2, 4, 8, 16) but never 3, 6 or 7, which is reported here as the source's own generalisation
  and not independently verified against a second source.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Balinese gamelan generally, per this source's own framing, illustrated with a
  gong-kebyar-consistent instrument list; not attributed to one named piece or region.

### BAL-08 Kendang: named stroke vocabulary, roles, and ensemble leadership

- claim: Wadon ("female") is the lower-pitched of the kendang pair, lanang ("male") the higher;
  named strokes include pak (a loud finger slap), de (a resonant bass palm stroke), cung (a partially
  damped harmonic stroke) and dag (a bass stroke using a mallet); the two kendang sometimes play
  rhythmically interlocking patterns, sometimes combine for rhythmic accents, and sometimes the lead
  player plays alone; together with ketuk, the kendang set the ensemble's tempo, and in dance
  accompaniment the lead player watches the dancer and translates cues into signals for the rest of
  the ensemble; a separate notated system (developed by Michael Tenzer) records each drum's part on
  three simultaneous lines (left hand's non-pitched sound "pek," left hand's pitched sound "peng,"
  right hand's stroke "tut" for lanang or "dag" for wadon); there is no conductor in Balinese
  gamelan, and wherever a kendang (or related drum) is present it functions as the ensemble's leader.
- source: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 (full, for the stroke vocabulary and general leadership role);
  SINTI-1985 (full, for the notated three-line system and the wadon/lanang naming in that system);
  PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010 (full, for the no-conductor/drum-as-leader generalisation)
- source_type: pedagogy (handbook); performer (Sinti/Diamond/Tenzer); ethnomusicology (Pandji/
  Ornstein)
- confidence: medium
- scope: Three independent sources converge on the kendang's leadership role and basic wadon/lanang
  naming; the specific four-stroke vocabulary (pak/de/cung/dag) is from the handbook alone, and the
  three-line notation system is Tenzer's own, documented in the Sinti interview's transcription notes.
- limitations: The handbook's stroke names and Sinti/Tenzer's notation-system stroke names ("pek,"
  "peng," "tut," "dag") do not fully overlap in the excerpts read, and this page does not attempt to
  reconcile them into one unified vocabulary; whether they name the same physical strokes differently
  or describe genuinely different technical inventories was not established in this pass.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Gong kebyar kendang technique as taught in one named New Zealand course
  (handbook) and as notated by one named composer/practitioner (Tenzer) for teaching purposes in the
  Sinti interview; the no-conductor/drum-leadership generalisation is stated broadly for Balinese
  gamelan by Pandji/Ornstein.

### BAL-09 Balinese practitioner training sequence, and the obstacle paired tuning poses for voice with gamelan

- claim: One documented Balinese practitioner's training began on a small gangsa (kantilan) for about
  a month, then moved to the larger pemade, then after about six months began leading as ugal
  ("conductor," leading by playing the melody with visible mallet movements), then after about a
  year progressed to reyong and trompong, then kendang; because of paired (ngumbang-ngisep) tuning,
  a singer has comparatively few gamelan pitches to align cleanly with, which the same practitioner
  reports as a real practical obstacle to combining voice with gamelan, and a reason voice
  historically appeared with only some genres (Arja, Janger) rather than the large orchestral forms
  (Pelegongan, Gong Gede, Semar Pegulingan, Kebyar).
- source: SINTI-1985 (full)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: One named practitioner's (I Wayan Sinti's) own account of his personal training history and
  compositional experience, given in an interview with a second named practitioner (Michael Tenzer)
  participating and corroborating parts of the discussion.
- limitations: A single practitioner's account of his own training sequence and his own view of the
  voice/tuning obstacle, not a survey of Balinese gamelan pedagogy generally or of other musicians'
  experience combining voice with gamelan.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: One named Balinese practitioner's (I Wayan Sinti's) own training in the Denpasar/
  Ubung area of Bali, beginning in the 1940s-50s; not generalised to Balinese gamelan pedagogy as a
  whole.

### BAL-10 Named Balinese ensembles outside gong kebyar, and their ceremonial associations

- claim: A pelegandjur ensemble (kendang, bonang, bende, kempul, tjeng-tjeng, gong) is used at
  temples for "melis"/"mekiis" (fetching holy water) and for calling on the spirits of the dead; a
  gambang-and-saron ensemble (bronze saron plus bamboo gambang) is used only at cremation ceremonies,
  to guide spirits to their resting place, and is "never performed as a music piece" outside that
  context; selunding (iron-keyed, six instruments, no kendang) is found in only a few remote villages
  (Tenganan-Karangasem named specifically) for the Mekare war dance and other ceremonial dances;
  gambelan luang (close to selunding, seven tones, bronze and wood/bamboo instruments) is similarly
  used for ritual ceremonies and temple-dances, with "only a few" such ensembles existing in Bali;
  gender wayang (four genders in two octave-related pairs) accompanies Wayang Kulit and other named
  ceremonies (teeth-filing, a newborn's three-month ceremony, cremation, temple ceremonies).
- source: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: A survey-style overview of named Balinese ensembles and their documented uses, presented by
  a named ethnomusicologist (Ruby Ornstein) with fieldwork, performance and teaching experience in
  Balinese gamelan specifically.
- limitations: A brief overview covering many ensembles rather than a deep study of any one; it does
  not itself use the word "sacred" or state a formal restriction, only documented ceremonial use and,
  for the gambang/saron case, an explicit "never performed as a music piece" statement; whether
  selunding or gambelan luang are formally restricted to lineage-holders, as opposed to simply rare
  and geographically localised, is not addressed by this source and is treated as an open question
  on the guide page, read alongside `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`'s separate, already-declined-
  by-the-studio statement about sacred iron selonding gamelan.
- inference: This page treats the studio's decline of selonding/selunding repertoire, stated in
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`, as continuing to apply, and reads this source's independent,
  differently spelled ("selunding") documentation of the same instrument's village- and ceremony-
  specific use as corroborating rather than contradicting that declination; this bridging inference
  is the guide page's own, not a claim made by Pandji/Ornstein directly.
- tradition_scope: Named Balinese ceremonial ensembles and repertoire (pelegandjur, gambang/saron,
  selunding, gambelan luang, gender wayang) as documented by this one source; not a claim about gong
  kebyar's own repertoire, which this source does not describe as restricted.
