# Mallets: research records

## Scope and method

The 2.0 page's central factual error (xylophone and glockenspiel sounding ranges printed as if they
were written ranges) was checked against three independent pedagogy sources (BYU's four instrument
pages, a percussionist's dedicated pitch-and-notation reference page, and a general web search
cross-check)
that agree on direction and approximate magnitude, though not on exact octave boundaries (a
2.5-octave glockenspiel and a 3-octave one differ). The bar-tuning correction (fourth-partial versus
twelfth relationships) rests on one acoustics-textbook page, read at section depth, that gives actual
measured-style ratio figures for both instruments. The four-mallet reach correction rests on one
university project page that explicitly names a tenth as the outer, difficult limit. Fletcher and
Rossing was not reachable in full and is cited only as `standard-reference`/`to-verify` for bar decay
times and resonator-tube tolerance. Tubular bells and crotales have the thinnest sourcing on this
page: only the pitch-range reference covers them at section depth, so their behaviour cards are
mostly `inference`.

## Records

### MAL-01 Xylophone written versus sounding range
- claim: the xylophone is written roughly F3 to C7 and sounds one octave higher, roughly F4 to C8.
- source: BYU-XYLOPHONE (section); RONNEFARTH-PITCH-RANGE (section)
- source_type: pedagogy (both)
- confidence: high
- scope: standard 3.5-octave orchestral/concert xylophone
- limitations: two independent university/performer pedagogy sources state the same direction and
  near-identical numbers; neither source is a peer-reviewed or tradition-institution source, and both
  are practical teaching references, sufficient for a range fact of this kind but not for anything
  more contested
- inference: none

### MAL-02 Glockenspiel written versus sounding range
- claim: the glockenspiel is written roughly F3 to F6 (or a comparable 2.5-to-3-octave span in a
  comfortable staff register) and sounds two octaves higher, roughly F5 to F8.
- source: BYU-GLOCKENSPIEL (section); RONNEFARTH-PITCH-RANGE (section)
- source_type: pedagogy (both)
- confidence: medium
- scope: standard orchestral/concert glockenspiel, sizes varying by instrument
- limitations: high confidence for the direction and magnitude (two octaves) of the transposition;
  lower (hence medium overall) for the exact written boundary notes, which the two sources do not
  state identically (BYU gives "2.5 octave instrument most common" without exact notes;
  RONNEFARTH-PITCH-RANGE gives F3-F6 written specifically). Exact range varies by specific instrument
  size; the page states a typical range rather than a fixed specification, consistent with the
  surrounding guide's own rule that ranges are typical, not constants
- inference: none for the transposition direction; the specific written boundary notes given in the
  page follow RONNEFARTH-PITCH-RANGE as the more specific of the two sources

### MAL-03 Marimba and xylophone bar tuning ratios
- claim: an ideal uniform beam's first two natural frequencies are in a ratio of about 2.76:1;
  instrument makers undercut bars to pull this toward a small integer, landing xylophone bars near a
  ratio of 3 (roughly a twelfth) and marimba bars near a ratio of 4 (roughly two octaves); a
  resonator tube under each bar is tuned to reinforce the fundamental specifically.
- source: WOODHOUSE-MARIMBA (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: high
- scope: general marimba and xylophone bar construction; the source does not distinguish by
  manufacturer or region
- limitations: the source gives specific measured-style ratio sequences for both instruments
  (xylophone: 1.00, 3.00, 6.16, 10.29...; marimba: 1.00, 3.92, 9.24, 16.27...) and states plainly that
  "well-tuned marimba and xylophone bars have this ratio adjusted to be close to 3 or, much more
  commonly, 4." The source itself notes that "measured frequency ratios do not seem to be deliberately
  tuned beyond the second mode," meaning only the first overtone is reliably tuned this precisely;
  higher partials are not claimed to follow a clean integer series
- inference: connecting the tuning ratio to the instrument's *perceived character* (marimba's rounder
  tone versus xylophone's harder, more clattering one) is this page's own reasonable extrapolation
  from the ratio data, not a claim the source states explicitly in those terms; the source's own
  framing is acoustic (frequency ratios), not descriptive (timbral character)

### MAL-04 Four-mallet interval span per hand
- claim: the interval held between two mallets in one hand ranges from a 2nd up to an octave
  comfortably; a 9th or a 10th is reachable by some players but is difficult, not routine, and the
  Stevens grip is specifically noted for enabling large intervals such as a tenth.
- source: UH-FOUR-MALLET (section)
- source_type: pedagogy (university percussion project page)
- confidence: medium
- scope: marimba four-mallet technique generally, Stevens grip specifically named as enabling the
  widest spreads
- limitations: a single, fairly informal source (a student/faculty project overview page rather than
  a PAS or conservatory-level resource), though its claims are consistent with general four-mallet
  pedagogy knowledge and with the Percussive Arts Society's own blog on four-mallet grips (fetched
  but found not to state numeric spans, so not cited as a confirming source). A single source for the
  exact numeric claim; the PAS blog on four-mallet grips was
  checked as a second source and did not corroborate or contradict with its own numbers, so this
  remains a single-source claim rather than a doubly-confirmed one
- inference: none for the core claim; extending the same reach figures to vibraphone four-mallet
  technique in the page is this page's own inference, flagged there as a generalisation from marimba
  data

### MAL-05 Vibraphone motor as amplitude tremolo, not vibrato
- claim: the vibraphone's motor rotates discs inside the resonator tubes, producing an amplitude
  tremolo at a selectable speed; it is not pitch vibrato, can be switched off, and is a per-passage
  character choice rather than a per-note one.
- source: BYU-VIBRAPHONE (section)
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: standard modern vibraphone
- limitations: consistent with the widely known mechanical description of the instrument, though the
  specific source consulted is brief and does not give a numeric speed range for the motor
- inference: the "per-passage, not per-note" framing is carried over from the prior version of this
  file's own reasoning, not independently re-verified in the BYU source this pass

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

- **Xylophone and glockenspiel written/sounding ranges were mislabelled in the 2.0 page** (given as
  "written," when the figures were the sounding ranges). This page states both explicitly, in the
  correct direction, for both instruments, and extends the same discipline to crotales, which share
  the glockenspiel's two-octave transposition. See MAL-01, MAL-02.
- **Four-mallet span corrected from "an octave and a half" to "a 2nd to an octave comfortably, a 9th
  or 10th as a genuine stretch."** See MAL-04. An octave and a half (a 12th) is not supported by any
  source found this pass and considerably exceeds what even an advanced Stevens-grip player is
  documented as reaching.
- **Bar-tuning ratios added**, correcting the prior page's silence on *why* marimba and xylophone
  sound different beyond "wood versus a different density of wood." See MAL-03.
- **Motor and pedal detail retained and lightly extended** from the prior version, which already had
  these mostly correct; the vibraphone pedal is now explicitly analogised to the piano's continuous
  curve (already the convention named in `PIANO_AND_KEYBOARDS.md`) rather than a binary switch.
