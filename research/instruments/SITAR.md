# Sitar: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Two acoustics papers by the same author (Siddiq, the 2010 Vienna Talk conference version and the
expanded 2012 Archives of Acoustics journal version, which overlap heavily but were both read in
full) give a physical-modelling account of the sitar's jawari bridge and dispersion, building
directly on Raman (1921, read in full), which is the founding source for the bridge mechanism itself.
Weisser, Demoucron and Leman (2012, read in full) is ethnomusicology correlated with acoustic
analysis, with named fieldwork at the ITC Sangeet Research Academy, Kolkata, and gives the sitar's
taraf (sympathetic string) settings, the meend ornament's measured pitch range, and players' own
account of controlling the taraf's resonance by plucking direction.

The practitioner source is Ravi Shankar's *My Music, My Life*, which contains a full sitar
self-instruction manual (construction, tuning, playing position, notation and a graded set of
exercises) in addition to the autobiography. The construction and instrument-family chapters, the
tuning and playing-position sections, the notation-symbol glossary, and the krintan/zamzama/meend/
gamak exercise section were all read at section depth; the manual runs to many pages and was sampled
rather than read start to finish, so it is recorded as section, not full, depth throughout. Shankar
trained under Ustad Allauddin Khan and represents the Maihar gharana; his manual teaches his own
method, developed for teaching Western students, and is not presented here as gharana-neutral. No
second gharana's pedagogy (Imdadkhani/Etawah in particular) was reached by a comparable source in
this pass, so claims that might differ by gharana are flagged as such rather than generalised.

## Records

### SITAR-01 The jawari bridge produces descending formants and defeats the Young-Helmholtz law, and dispersion is essential to the effect

- claim: the sitar's curved ("flat") bridge causes the vibrating string's effective length to vary
  periodically as it collides with the bridge surface, which (a) produces spectrograms with
  descending formants not found in Western plucked strings, (b) produces complex, non-monotonic
  envelopes on individual partials from energy flow between them, and (c) defeats the classical
  Young-Helmholtz law, under which plucking at a node should silence the corresponding harmonic.
  Dispersion (frequency-dependent wave speed in the real, stiff string) is necessary for the model to
  reproduce the descending formants; without it the bridge's effect on the spectrum is much weaker.
- source: RAMAN-1921 (full); SIDDIQ-2012 (full); SIDDIQ-2010 (full)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: high
- scope: Siddiq's physical model is built and verified against a recording of a real sitar string
  plucked at one fifth of its length in an anechoic room; Raman's account is the original qualitative
  identification of the mechanism in the tanpura and veena, extended to the sitar's "flat" bridges in
  Siddiq's discussion.
- limitations: Siddiq's model deliberately neglects the sympathetic (taraf) strings and the resonating
  body, so it isolates the single-string mechanism and does not model the instrument's full sound;
  it also neglects torsional and in-plane-parallel-to-bridge motion, which the paper itself flags as
  potentially important and unmodelled.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: instrument physics, not attributed to a school or gharana.

### SITAR-02 Sitar taraf strings: eleven to thirteen, tuned to the raga, sharing the jawari bridge with the playing strings

- claim: the sitar carries eleven to thirteen sympathetic strings (taraf), grouped as a single set,
  positioned in the closed handle below the playing strings, tuned according to the raga in a range
  of roughly 280 to 750 Hz; both the playing strings and the taraf pass over jawari bridges (two
  bridges on the instrument, one for each string group).
- source: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012 (full)
- source_type: ethnomusicology (with acoustic measurement)
- confidence: medium
- scope: the paper's Table 1 gives this as a general description of "the sitar" built from its field
  recordings and interviews with named players (a virtuoso sitar player recorded at ITC-SRA), not a
  survey across many instruments or gharanas.
- limitations: taraf count and tuning are stated by the paper to vary with the raga and, implicitly,
  with the instrument and player; the 280-750 Hz range is a reported range, not a fixed scheme, and
  should not be read as a table of which taraf plays which raga.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani sitar practice, ITC Sangeet Research Academy fieldwork.

### SITAR-03 Meend (pulled bends) can reach more than a sixth on a single fret, and splits the sound into a shifting note and a lingering sympathetic resonance

- claim: pulling the string sideways at a fretted position (meend) can shift its pitch by more than a
  sixth without moving the fretting finger to a new fret; because a taraf string tuned near the
  original pitch keeps ringing after the pull, the resulting sound spectrographically splits into two
  components, the sliding pitch of the main string and the decaying resonance of the taraf string at
  the note the pull departed from.
- source: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012 (full); SHANKAR-1968 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology, with acoustic measurement (Weisser, Demoucron, Leman); performer
  (Shankar, describing the physical execution: pulling the string with the second finger)
- confidence: medium
- scope: the "more than a sixth" figure is given by Weisser, Demoucron and Leman as the maximum pitch
  variation reachable on one fret via meend, in the context of discussing melody-tracking algorithms,
  not as a survey of typical meend intervals in performance; Shankar's manual describes the technique
  (pulling the string, maintaining pressure until the target pitch is heard) without giving an
  interval figure.
- limitations: neither source gives a distribution of how far meend is typically pulled in practice
  versus the maximum reachable; the taraf-splitting description is illustrated with one recorded
  example (a sitar meend in a gat), not a general survey.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani sitar; Shankar's execution description is Maihar-gharana pedagogy.

### SITAR-04 Mizrab strokes are named and directional (da outward, ra inward), and plucking direction is one of a sitar player's few controls over taraf resonance

- claim: the plectrum stroke worn on the right index finger is played in two directions, named da
  (outward, away from the thumb) and ra (a following inward stroke); besides the choice of plucking
  technique and the strength of the pluck, the direction of plucking is one of the few means a sitar
  player has to influence how strongly the sympathetic strings respond.
- source: SHANKAR-1968 (section, notation-symbol glossary and exercises); WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012
  (full, players' own report on controlling taraf response)
- source_type: performer (Shankar); ethnomusicology with player interviews (Weisser, Demoucron,
  Leman)
- confidence: high
- scope: Shankar's da/ra naming is standard notation in his own teaching method; Weisser, Demoucron
  and Leman separately confirm, from player interviews, that pluck direction is one of the taraf
  controls sitarists report having.
- limitations: Weisser, Demoucron and Leman note sitarists report "fewer possibilities" for
  controlling taraf than sarangi players do; the mechanism by which plucking direction changes taraf
  excitation is not explained acoustically by either source.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: da/ra terminology is Shankar's (Maihar-lineage) teaching vocabulary; the taraf-
  control finding is from ITC-SRA fieldwork and not attributed to a single gharana.

### SITAR-05 Chikari strings are a drone/rhythmic layer, played most densely in jhala, functioning as ground against the melodic figure

- claim: the chikari (drone strings played open, without fretting) are plucked intermittently
  throughout a performance, most frequently and at the fastest tempo in the jhala section, where a
  single melodic pluck may be followed by several chikari strokes; across the performance the chikari
  functions as a rhythmic and droning "ground" against which the melodic line is the "figure," though
  the two can appear to blur.
- source: SHANKAR-1968 (section, notation and bol-phrase examples); WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012 (full)
- source_type: performer (Shankar); ethnomusicology (Weisser, Demoucron, Leman)
- confidence: medium
- scope: Shankar's manual notates chikari strokes (symbol and the syllable "ya" in bol recitation)
  throughout the graded exercises; Weisser, Demoucron and Leman's ground/figure framing is their own
  analytic description, attributed to Napier's concept of "filling the void" in Hindustani aesthetic
  saturation.
- limitations: the ground/figure account is an analytic framing by the researchers, not a term
  reported directly from player interviews in the excerpt read.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Hindustani sitar performance generally, per both sources; not scoped to one
  gharana.

### SITAR-06 Named left-hand ornament techniques: cut, slip, hammer, krintan, zamzama, gamak, taught as a graded vocabulary in one gharana's pedagogy

- claim: Shankar's manual teaches a set of left-hand techniques with specific names and executions:
  a "cut" (lifting a stopping finger off a fretted note to sound a lower, already-fretted note without
  a new pluck), a "slip" (sliding a finger to a new fret while maintaining string pressure, sounding
  the new note without a new pluck), a "hammer" (bringing a finger down onto a fret firmly enough to
  sound the note without plucking), krintan (a family of such left-hand-only figures), zamzama (rapid
  repeated alternation between two notes, two or three times), and gamak (pulling the string rapidly
  from one note to the next and back).
- source: SHANKAR-1968 (section, notational glossary and the krintan/zamzama/meend/gamak exercise
  section)
- source_type: performer
- confidence: medium
- scope: this is Shankar's own pedagogical vocabulary and set of definitions, built for teaching
  sitar outside India where "the scarcity of competent sitar teachers" (his words) was a stated
  problem; it represents Maihar-gharana-descended practice as he taught it.
- limitations: this page does not have a second gharana's pedagogy to compare against; whether
  Imdadkhani/Etawah-style teaching uses the same terms for the same techniques, different terms, or
  a different technical emphasis (that tradition is often described as closer to vocal, gayaki-style
  ornamentation) was not reached by a source in this pass and is left to-verify.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: Maihar gharana, as taught by Ravi Shankar.

## Still to verify

- Whether Imdadkhani/Etawah (or other) gharana pedagogy names, executes or emphasises krintan,
  zamzama, gamak, meend and mizrab stroke technique the same way Shankar's Maihar-descended manual
  does. This page states Shankar's account as Shankar's account and does not extend it to "the
  sitar" in general.
- The traditional attribution (repeated in Shankar's own book) of the sitar's movable frets and
  three-to-seven-string reconfiguration to the 13th/14th-century poet-musician Amir Khusru: this is a
  widely repeated origin story within the tradition itself, carried here as a traditional attribution
  reported by a practitioner source, not as verified organological history. A specialist
  organological or historical-musicology source was not reached in this pass to confirm or contest it.
- The precise range and count of frets and playing strings in current practice beyond what Shankar's
  own manual specifies for his own instrument (19 standard frets, 20 recommended by him, 6 or 7
  playing strings): these are his stated preferences and standard figures, not a survey of
  contemporary makers.
- Recording practice for the sitar (microphone placement and its interaction with the taraf/jawari
  spectrum) was not reached by any source read in this pass.
