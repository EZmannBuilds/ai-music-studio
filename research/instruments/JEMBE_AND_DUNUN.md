# Jembe and Dunun: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Two sources were reached and read at section depth: Rainer Polak's "Rhythmic Feel as Meter"
(fieldwork in Bamako and rural areas to its south, Mali, 2006-2007, and the author's own
apprenticeship as second jembe under the late Jaraba Jakite, 1997-1998), and Polak and London's
"Timing and Meter in Mande Drumming from Mali" (field recordings made by Polak in Mali in 2012,
with named professional drummers in two regions). Both are read closely for construction, technique,
and ensemble-configuration content, in addition to their primary timing-analysis purpose. Eric
Charry's *Mande Music* (2000) was not independently opened; it is cited only where Polak's own text
attributes a specific claim to it (the jembe's regional origin and page range), and is otherwise
used only to flag, honestly, where the wider jembe-scene literature says more than this page's
fieldwork sources documented (the three-drum dunun family). Jeryl Johnston's comparative variation
study, read for the Ewe family's page, mentions Polak and Jakubowski's jembe-perception work only in
passing and was not used as a source for this page.

## Records

### JD-01 Jembe: construction, three-stroke/six-articulation vocabulary

- claim: The jembe is a goblet-shaped, single-headed hand drum of the Mande peoples of southern Mali
  and northeastern Guinea, played with two bare hands using three named strokes (bass, tone, slap),
  each playable open or muted.
- source: POLAK-JEMBE-2010 (section); CHARRY-2000 (not-read, cited by Polak for regional origin)
- source_type: ethnomusicology; standard reference
- confidence: high
- scope: jembe construction and stroke terminology generally, as documented across Polak's Bamako
  fieldwork; the six-way stroke/mute legend is given explicitly in the article's transcription key.
- limitations: head-tensioning/tuning mechanism and absolute pitch are not given; the claim rests on
  one author's fieldwork corpus (extensive but geographically centred on Bamako and its rural south).
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: Mande jembe music, Bamako and rural areas to its south, Mali

### JD-02 Jembe ensemble configuration: duo, trio, quartet; lead and accompaniment roles

- claim: The smallest typical jembe-dunun ensemble is a duo (one jembe, one dunun); classical Bamako
  style also uses trios (two jembes, one dunun) and quartets (two jembes, two dununs); larger
  ensembles are common today. Lead jembe improvises within a bounded phrase vocabulary; a second
  jembe, where present, plays a short fixed two-beat ostinato.
- source: POLAK-JEMBE-2010 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: classical Bamako jembe-ensemble style specifically, documented through the Manjanin
  recording analysed in the article (a quartet) and the article's general statement about typical
  ensemble sizes.
- limitations: does not cover Khasonka or Bamana ensemble sizing, which follows different
  conventions (see JD-05); does not give a maximum size for "larger ensembles."
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: classical Bamako jembe ensemble style, Mali

### JD-03 Dunun: identity phrase, largely fixed, danced/sung as the piece's name

- claim: The first (or lead) dunun's basic phrase marks the identity of the composition, functions as
  a timeline and phrasing referent, and is sung or shouted by dancers to call up the piece; a
  documented standard variation simply omits one stroke.
- source: POLAK-JEMBE-2010 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: classical Bamako jembe-ensemble style, documented through the Manjanin analysis specifically
  (the phrase appears identically in 164 of 218 cycles in the analysed recording, with the named
  variation occurring about 40 times).
- limitations: one piece, one recording set; whether this generalises to every piece in the standard
  repertoire is not established here.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: classical Bamako jembe ensemble style, Manjanin specifically for the measured
  figures

### JD-04 Khasonka dundunba: construction and simultaneous drum-and-bell technique

- claim: The Khasonka dundunba is a double-headed cylindrical drum with goatskin membranes, about
  33-37 cm in diameter and 55-70 cm long, played with a curved wooden stick in the stronger hand; the
  same performer simultaneously plays a small bell (an iron ring on the weak hand's thumb striking a
  bell fixed to that hand's fingers) with the weak hand.
- source: POLAK-LONDON-2014 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: high
- scope: Khasonka dundunba tradition, Khaso region, western Mali, as documented through named
  fieldwork with two professional dundunba players (the Sacko brothers) in Mahina.
- limitations: one regional tradition, two named performers; the article itself notes the demanding,
  bimanual nature of this technique produces measurably higher timing variability than single-task
  drumming, which should not be read back as a general property of "dunun" playing outside Khasonka
  style.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: Khasonka dundunba tradition, Khaso region, western Mali

### JD-05 Regional variant resolved: whether one performer plays drum and bell

- claim: Whether the dunun's player also plays an integrated bell is a documented regional/stylistic
  variable, not a constant: Khasonka dundunba style pairs drum and bell on one performer; the
  classical Bamako jembe-ensemble material documents dunun (first and second) as stick-only, with no
  bell described in that context.
- source: POLAK-JEMBE-2010 (section); POLAK-LONDON-2014 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: comparison between the two specific ensembles and pieces these two articles document
  (Manjanin, classical Bamako style; Bire, Khasonka dundunba style).
- limitations: the Bamako-style article does not explicitly state that dunun *never* carries a bell
  in that style; it simply does not describe one for the piece and ensemble it analyses. The
  claim is a documented contrast between what two specific fieldwork accounts describe, not an
  exhaustive survey of Mande dunun practice region by region.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: contrast between classical Bamako jembe-ensemble style and Khasonka dundunba
  style, Mali

### JD-06 Tempo acceleration as a structural feature

- claim: Performances of both documented pieces accelerate substantially across their course:
  Manjanin from about 135 bpm to 205 bpm in the analysed recording; Bire from about 85 bpm to 125 bpm
  in one recording, with the mean long-short bell timing ratio shifting slightly (from about 59:41 to
  61:39) across that acceleration.
- source: POLAK-JEMBE-2010 (section); POLAK-LONDON-2014 (section)
- source_type: ethnomusicology
- confidence: medium
- scope: the specific recordings analysed in each article; both articles frame acceleration as
  characteristic of Malian dance drumming generally, but the precise figures are per-recording
  measurements, not a general rate.
- limitations: figures come from one or a few recordings per piece; they should be treated as
  documented instances, not universal tempo curves, per `shared/HUMAN_PERFORMANCE_SCHEMA.md`
  section 3's treatment of corpus-specific figures.
- inference: none
- tradition_scope: the specific Manjanin and Bire recordings analysed in each article

### JD-07 Three-drum dunun family (dundunba/sangban/kenkeni): standard-reference only

- claim: A three-drum dunun family, graded largest/lowest (dundunba) to smallest/highest (kenkeni),
  with a middle drum (sangban), each sometimes carrying its own kenken bell, is a widely cited
  convention in commercial and pedagogical jembe literature.
- source: CHARRY-2000 (not-read)
- source_type: standard reference
- confidence: low
- scope: general jembe-scene convention, attributed to Charry as the field's standard reference; not
  independently confirmed.
- limitations: neither Polak article, read at section depth, describes this three-drum naming scheme
  in the regional styles (classical Bamako, Khasonka) it documents; this page explicitly does not
  claim the two are the same thing, and marks this row `to-verify` on the guide page rather than
  presenting it as established by the sources this pass actually read.
- inference: this page infers, but does not assert as sourced, that the convention may still apply to
  other Mande regional styles (for instance Guinea-based ensembles) that neither Polak article covers.
- tradition_scope: general/commercial jembe-scene convention (region and style not specified in the
  attribution available here)

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

- **"West African polyrhythm" as one undifferentiated bucket → decomposed.** This page, together
  with `EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md` and the two new `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/` files, replaces the single
  `WEST_AFRICAN_POLYRHYTHM.md` file's treatment of Ewe and Mande material as one entry.
- **A "verify" question from the earlier pass (how many players, and whether one plays drum and
  bell) → answered, region by region, rather than left open.** See JD-05. [POLAK-JEMBE-2010;
  POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- **The commercial three-drum dunun family is not silently presented as fact.** Where earlier,
  thinner material might have named dundunba/sangban/kenkeni as simply "the" dunun family, this page
  separates what the fieldwork sources document (a first and second dunun, or a Khasonka dundunba
  with its own bell) from what only the standard-reference literature documents (the three-drum
  scheme), per JD-07.
