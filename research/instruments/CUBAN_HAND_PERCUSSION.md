# Cuban hand percussion: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries
the conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Five sources were read at section depth or more. Alex Pertout's "The Conga Drum, Part II," an
excerpt from his Australian National University MPhil thesis published by Drumscene Magazine, was
read in full and is this family's practitioner-tier source: Pertout is himself a professional
percussionist, and the article cites named Cuban master drummers directly (Tata Guines, Jose Luis
"Changuito" Quintana, Carlos "Patato" Valdez, Ramon "Mongo" Santamaria) along with his own thesis
interviews with several of them. Three articles by Kevin Zahner (Rhythm Notes), a percussionist who
names his own teachers (Norm Bergeron, who learned from Changuito; a private lesson with Michael
Spiro) were read in full. Brendan Bache's salsa-drumming pedagogy article (Liberty Park Music), by a
named Latin-percussion teacher, was read at section depth (the substantive percussion-instrument
sections; the drum-kit-adaptation sections at the end were skimmed rather than read closely, and the
page's midsection carries an unrelated block of injected casino-affiliate text that was skipped
entirely and is not cited). One further source, the Percussive Arts Society's "FUNdamentals: The
Conga Marcha," was reached but is paywalled to non-members and contributes nothing; it stays in the
register at `not-read` so the next pass does not re-attempt it expecting free access.

The clave and timeline logic this page depends on is not re-derived here: it lives in
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/CLAVE_AND_TIMELINES.md`, whose own confidence note already states that its
citation of Penalosa's *The Clave Matrix* was not read in full by that file's own research pass.
This family's sources independently corroborate the practical consequence of clave orientation (the
2:3 versus 3:2 decision) without needing to re-read Penalosa directly, since Bache's pedagogy article
states the same orientation logic in its own words for a working salsa arranger.

## Records

### CU-01 The conga's stroke vocabulary, and that its naming is genuinely unsettled
- claim: a fully developed conga (tumbadora) technique uses eight standard hand sounds: palm,
  fingers, bass, open tone, muffled tone, slap, open slap and muffled slap. The exact naming of these
  sounds is not standardised across the pedagogical literature: the same sound the article calls
  "palm" is elsewhere called "heel"; "fingers" is elsewhere called "toe," "tip" or "touch"; "muffled
  tone" is elsewhere called "mute" or "muff." Spanish names for the sounds are also in use. This lack
  of a single standard naming is stated by the source itself as an unresolved state of the field, not
  as an oversight this page can silently correct.
- source: PERTOUT-CONGA-II
- source_type: performer
- confidence: high
- scope: general conga (tumbadora) technique, synthesised by Pertout from earlier ethnographic
  literature on Afro-American drumming (he cites Joseph Howard, *Drums in the Americas*, 1967) plus
  his own thesis interviews with Cuban master players.
- limitations: one author's synthesis; the underlying naming variation is real and documented by the
  source itself, but the specific eight-sound list is Pertout's own organisation of the field rather
  than an official standard from a governing body, since the source states no such standard exists.
- inference: none for the stroke list itself; treating "palm/heel" and "fingers/toe" as the same
  strokes under different names follows directly from Pertout's own text.
- tradition_scope: Afro-Cuban conga technique broadly, not attributed to one named school; the
  historical figures named (Tata Guines, Changuito, Patato Valdez, Mongo Santamaria) span the
  mid-20th-century development of the instrument in Cuba and in the New York-based Latin jazz scene.

### CU-02 Heel/toe/open/closed technique, and a named teaching lineage
- claim: a widely taught modern conga technique organises the hand strokes as heel (a dropped,
  relaxed palm stroke near centre), toe (a wrist stroke near the edge), open and closed (muted) tones,
  and open and closed slaps, developed through structured heel-toe and chop exercises. This specific
  technique framework is traceable, through the source's own account, to Changuito, via a teacher
  (Norm Bergeron) who learned it from him, and independently from a private lesson with Michael Spiro.
- source: ZAHNER-CONGA-TECHNIQUE
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: one contemporary teacher's own technique, explicitly presented as inherited from a named
  lineage rather than invented independently.
- limitations: a single teacher's account of his own lineage; not cross-checked here against a
  method book by Changuito or Spiro directly.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: this specific heel/toe/open/closed framework and its named lineage (Changuito, by
  way of Norm Bergeron; Michael Spiro directly), not claimed as the only correct technique.

### CU-03 Guaguanco's three drum roles, named for the Havana style specifically
- claim: in Havana-style guaguanco (rumba), three conga-family drums have named, distinct roles: the
  tumba (the lowest drum) lands its conversational phrases on beat four on both sides of the clave;
  the segunda (the middle drum) plays at least beat one on the "two" side of the clave; the quinto
  (the highest drum) solos in conversation with the other two drums and with the dancers. The tumbao
  pattern (a repeated conga groove, in mambo and cha-cha-cha as well as folkloric contexts) is
  described as incorporating a "marcha" feel of alternating heel and toe strokes under a slap-and-tone
  backbeat.
- source: ZAHNER-CONGA-TECHNIQUE
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: explicitly scoped by the source itself to the Havana style of guaguanco, as distinct from
  the Matanzas style, which the source names but does not describe.
- limitations: a single teacher's summary, presented as simplified ("it's more complicated than that,
  but let's keep it simple," in the source's own words); not cross-checked against a folkloric
  percussion method text.
- inference: none for the role assignments as stated; describing this as one of at least two named
  regional styles (Havana versus Matanzas) follows the source's own framing.
- tradition_scope: Havana-style guaguanco specifically; the source explicitly withholds the Matanzas
  style's differences as out of scope for that article.

### CU-04 Conga tuning is relative and by ear, commonly to a fourth or fifth between drums
- claim: contemporary conga players commonly tune by ear rather than to fixed absolute pitches,
  adjusting the tension of each drum until the open tone, slap and bass strokes all sound clear, and
  tuning multiple drums to a perfect fourth or perfect fifth interval between them (with a minor third
  or major second also named as workable) rather than to specific pitches.
- source: ZAHNER-CONGA-TUNING
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: one contemporary teacher's own stated practice and preference ("I never tune my drums to
  specific pitches").
- limitations: presented as this player's own working method, not as a documented historical or
  folkloric standard; other conga players may tune differently, and the source does not claim
  otherwise.
- inference: none.
- tradition_scope: general contemporary conga practice as one teacher states it; not attributed to a
  specific historical or regional school.

### CU-05 Bongo construction, finger-pad strokes, and the martillo pattern's heel-toe muted technique
- claim: the bongo pairs a smaller, higher-pitched drum (macho) with a larger, lower-pitched drum
  (hembra), traditionally held between the seated player's legs and played primarily with the finger
  pads rather than the whole hand; striking with one finger pad versus two or more produces
  differently pitched accents, and a finger left resting on the head produces a muted tone. The
  standard bongo pattern, martillo, incorporates a heel-and-toe muted-stroke technique on the macho
  (the left hand typically muting with the thumb while the right hand's pads play the pattern) under
  the melodic open strokes.
- source: ZAHNER-BONGO-TECHNIQUE
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: one contemporary teacher's technique account, itself framed as a synthesis from a high-school
  Afro-Cuban music workshop and subsequent college-level salsa performance experience, not from a
  named traditional-lineage teacher the way CU-02's conga account is.
- limitations: the article's claim that the bongo derives from West African paired-drum designs
  brought to Cuba, and its account of changui preceding son cubano's use of the instrument, are
  historical assertions presented without citation in the source itself; they are recorded here as
  the source's claim, not verified against a historical or ethnomusicological source, and are labelled
  accordingly on the page (standard-reference at best, since the source itself cites nothing for these
  specific historical claims).
- inference: none for the technique description; the historical-origin claims are the source's own
  unsourced assertion, not this page's inference, and are flagged as such.
- tradition_scope: bongo technique generally, as one contemporary player-teacher states it; the
  historical claims are general and not scoped to a named region or period beyond "late 19th century
  Cuba."

### CU-06 The bongocero's switch to handheld cencerro in montuno sections, and clave orientation as a real arranging decision
- claim: in son and salsa performance practice, the bongo player (bongocero) commonly switches from
  playing the bongos (typically the martillo pattern) to playing a handheld cowbell (cencerro,
  called campana de bongo) during the more intense, call-and-response-driven montuno or coro section
  of a tune, and once that switch to bell is made it is unlikely, in practice, that the player
  switches back to the drums for the remainder of that performance. In a salsa ensemble that also has
  a timbalero, the timbales player's own cowbell or cascara part may take over this role instead,
  reducing the need for the bongocero to switch. Separately, clave orientation (whether a section is
  felt as starting on the "2" side or the "3" side of son clave) is treated by this source as a real,
  audible arranging decision that a working performer names explicitly, consistent with
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/CLAVE_AND_TIMELINES.md`'s account of orientation.
- source: LIBERTYPARK-BACHE-SALSA
- source_type: pedagogy
- confidence: medium
- scope: son montuno and salsa performance practice, as taught by one named Latin-percussion and
  drum-kit teacher; the source itself distinguishes strict son practice from salsa-ensemble practice
  (where a timbalero may cover the bell role instead).
- limitations: a single pedagogy article aimed at drum-kit players learning to adapt salsa
  percussion parts, not a specialist bongo or clave text; it corroborates rather than substitutes for
  a dedicated source on either topic.
- inference: none for the described practice; treating this as consistent with (not a substitute for)
  `CLAVE_AND_TIMELINES.md`'s clave-orientation account is this page's own framing, not a claim the
  source itself makes about that file.
- tradition_scope: son montuno and contemporary salsa ensemble practice specifically, not folkloric
  rumba or son performance outside a bongo/timbales-based ensemble.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

No 2.0 `CUBAN_HAND_PERCUSSION.md` page exists; `HAND_PERCUSSION.md` (the frame page) names this page
as "in preparation in 2.1" and explicitly declines to state any Cuban-specific stroke vocabulary
itself, routing to this page instead. There is accordingly nothing on this specific instrument pair
to correct. One thing worth flagging: `HAND_PERCUSSION.md` also corrects an older version of
`PERCUSSION.md` that had presented "bass, open, slap" as a universal hand-drum vocabulary when it is
specifically conga/jembe terminology; this page's own eight-stroke conga list (CU-01) is consistent
with that correction, since it presents the fuller vocabulary as conga-specific, not as a universal
hand-drum standard.
