# Drum Kit: research records

What was read, what was not, and the limits of each claim the guide page makes. The page carries the
conclusions; this file carries the evidence.

## Scope and method

Six sources were read at section or full depth this pass: Forsyth's 1914 *Orchestration* (the side
drum, cymbal, and kettle-drum chapters), the Percussive Arts Society's 40 International Drum
Rudiments sheet (read in full, it is one page), Sofia Dahl's 2003 licentiate thesis and 2004 journal
article on drumstick movement and accent, one peer-reviewed study of a drummer's hi-hat microtiming
(Räsänen et al. 2015), and one page of Jim Woodhouse's *Euphonics* acoustics text on tuned-drum
membrane modes. Fletcher and Rossing's *The Physics of Musical Instruments* was not reachable in full
this pass and is cited only where a specific claim is widely attributed to it, labelled
`standard-reference` or `to-verify`. A ResearchGate-hosted paper on drummer movement (working title
noted as Fujii in scratch notes) returned only a "temporarily unavailable" placeholder page and was
dropped; no claim in the page rests on it.

Forsyth's and Rimsky-Korsakov's material is written for the orchestral snare and suspended cymbals,
not a drum kit. Where this file cites them for a kit instrument (the snare's wire mechanism, cymbal
tapering and choke technique, roll technique), the underlying claim is about the physical object
(a snare drum, a cymbal) rather than the ensemble it usually appears in, and the research records
below say so explicitly rather than treating the transfer as automatic.

## Records

### DK-01 Snare wire mechanism and the "octave-higher" buzz
- claim: the batter head's vibration reaches the snare wires through the shell's air and the snare
  head, and the wires buzzing against the head is what gives a snare drum its bright, dry identity;
  disengaging the wires leaves a small, unsnared drum.
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: medium
- scope: describes the orchestral side drum's construction, which is mechanically the same
  instrument used on a kit (batter head, snare head, wires against the snare head); Forsyth does not
  discuss a drum-kit context.
- limitations: written in 1914 about instruments and heads (calfskin) that have since been mostly
  replaced by synthetic heads; the wire-mechanism physics is not claimed to be affected by that
  change, but the claim was not re-verified against a modern acoustics source.
- inference: the sympathetic-buzz claim (wires buzzing when a nearby drum or loud instrument excites
  a matching frequency, not only when the snare itself is struck) is stated in the page as inference,
  generalised from the same physical mechanism, not itself read in a source this pass.

### DK-02 Cymbal construction, choke, and roll technique
- claim: cymbals are tapered bronze-alloy discs that contact a striking implement at the edge first;
  a choke is a physical grab that stops the ring, implemented virtually as a note-off or aftertouch;
  a cymbal roll is a distinct technique (rapid alternation, or a suspended plate rolled with two soft
  sticks), not a fast repeated single stroke.
- source: FORSYTH-1914 (section)
- source_type: orchestration
- confidence: medium
- scope: orchestral suspended cymbal and clashed pair technique; the construction and choke physics
  transfer directly to a kit's ride and crash cymbals because they are the same object, but the
  specific playing vocabulary (bow/edge/bell terminology used on a kit) is drum-kit pedagogy, not
  Forsyth's, and is labelled `inference` in the page rather than sourced to him.
- limitations: Forsyth does not discuss a drum kit or stick technique on a ride cymbal specifically.
- inference: bow/edge/bell attack-colour distinctions, and the ride cymbal's role as a continuous
  timekeeping voice, are common kit-percussion pedagogy not opened at section depth in a named source
  this pass, and are labelled `inference` in the page.

### DK-03 Rudiment-based sticking as the cause of real dynamic and timing texture
- claim: the Percussive Arts Society's standard 40 rudiments (roll, diddle, flam, and drag families)
  name and drill the specific hand-to-hand sticking patterns real players use for repeated notes,
  fills, and accents; a paradiddle or similar pattern produces a reproducible accent shape purely
  from which hand lands where.
- source: PAS-RUDIMENTS-1984 (full)
- source_type: tradition institution
- confidence: medium
- scope: Western concert and kit-percussion sticking pedagogy, standardised across the US and widely
  adopted internationally; does not cover hand-drum or non-stick traditions.
- limitations: high confidence for the existence and naming of the rudiments; lower (hence the
  medium rating overall) for the claim that programming from named rudiments produces a more
  idiomatic-sounding result than uniform alternation, which is this file's inference from the
  rudiment list rather than a claim PAS itself makes. The source is a one-page reference sheet
  (rudiment names and notated patterns), not a pedagogical explanation of why rudiment-based sticking
  sounds more played; that inferential step is the page's own reasoning, not PAS's stated claim.
- inference: that programming fills and repeated notes from named rudiments, rather than arbitrary
  alternation, is what "reads as played" — reasonable from drumming pedagogy generally, but not a
  claim PAS's rudiment sheet itself makes.

### DK-04 Rebound physics and named stroke types (tap, upstroke, downstroke, full stroke)
- claim: a drumhead is elastic and returns the stick's energy; players are taught to plan strokes by
  named height/preparation states (tap, upstroke/pull-out, downstroke/control stroke, full/free
  stroke) that determine how much of that rebound is used, caught, or released before the next
  stroke.
- source: DAHL-2003-THESIS (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics/perception (percussion movement science), citing pedagogical
  sources (Shivas 1957, Moeller 1956, Famularo 1999) within the thesis
- confidence: high
- scope: snare-drum and general stick-percussion technique, studied via motion capture of four
  percussion players (three professional, one amateur).
- limitations: high confidence covers the physical description and the existence of the named stroke
  vocabulary; the thesis itself cites these as established pedagogy rather than as its own new
  finding. Small sample (four players); the pedagogical stroke-type vocabulary (tap, upstroke,
  downstroke, full stroke) is quoted from within the thesis as established teaching, not derived from
  the thesis's own data.
- inference: none beyond generalising the described technique to kit snare and tom playing broadly,
  which the thesis's own framing (percussion playing in general, across differing drumhead
  stiffness) supports.

### DK-05 Preparatory height, striking velocity, and accent timing
- claim: all four studied players raised the stick to a greater height and struck with higher
  velocity before an accented stroke than before unaccented strokes, with height and velocity both
  increasing with intended dynamic level; the interval beginning with the accented stroke was
  prolonged, with the amount of lengthening decreasing as dynamic level increased.
- source: DAHL-2004-ACCENT (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics/perception
- confidence: medium
- scope: an ostinato rhythm with an accent every fourth stroke, played by four percussionists (three
  professional, one amateur) at three dynamic levels, three tempi, and on three striking surfaces.
  Not tested on a full drum kit, a groove pattern, or a wider range of players.
- limitations: the four-player finding is consistent in direction (the authors note individual
  movement strategies differed considerably between players even though higher preparation and
  velocity for accents, and a prolonged interval before them, held for all four); confidence is
  lowered to medium because the sample is small and controlled, the pattern is a single
  interleaved-accent ostinato rather than naturalistic drumming, and this page uses the finding to
  justify treating `metrical_accent` as a timing model as well as a velocity one for kit programming,
  a reasonable extrapolation not itself tested on a kit groove.
- inference: applying the accent-timing finding to kit snare/tom accents generally, beyond the
  specific ostinato pattern studied.

### DK-06 A drummer's hi-hat microtiming as the groove, not noise around a grid
- claim: a peer-reviewed analysis of Jeff Porcaro's one-handed sixteenth-note hi-hat part in "I Keep
  Forgettin'" (1982, recorded without a metronome) found long-range correlated fluctuations in both
  timing and amplitude, short-range anticorrelation between successive intervals, and a two-bar
  periodic amplitude pattern; the authors treat this structured variation as the source of the part's
  musicality rather than deviation from an otherwise-followed grid.
- source: RASANEN-2015 (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics/perception
- confidence: medium
- scope: one drummer, one recorded track, one instrument (hi-hat) within the kit. The authors'
  broader claim (that this is characteristic of skilled, un-clicked drumming generally) is an
  interpretive claim within the paper, not established across a corpus in this reading.
- limitations: high confidence for this one recording and player, since this is a single-track case
  study; confidence is rated medium overall because a single case study cannot establish that *most*
  live-feel kit parts behave this way,
  only that at least one celebrated example does, with a specific, measurable structure (not white
  noise). The page uses this to narrow, not eliminate, the "kit as marker part" default; it does not
  claim every groove-based style behaves identically to this one recording.
- inference: generalising from this one case to "funk, soul, classic rock, R&B, and any style built
  on a felt pocket" is this page's own extrapolation, consistent with the broader microtiming
  literature summarised in `research/PERFORMANCE_AND_EXPRESSION.md` (which cites the same paper
  alongside others reaching compatible conclusions about structured, non-random deviation), but not
  itself proven for every named style in this single reading.

### DK-07 Off-centre striking excites the pitched modes of a tuned drum head
- claim: striking a tuned drum head (the source discusses timpani specifically) near the centre
  maximises axisymmetric modes that have no definite pitch and gives little sound from the modes that
  actually carry the tuned pitch (which all have at least one nodal line through the centre); this is
  why a player strikes off-centre.
- source: WOODHOUSE-TUNED-DRUMS (section)
- source_type: academic acoustics
- confidence: medium
- scope: the source page is written about kettledrums/timpani specifically.
- limitations: high confidence as stated for timpani, since the underlying physics (mode shape versus
  strike position) is general to any circular tuned membrane; rated medium overall because the source
  does not discuss drum-kit toms, snares, or kicks directly. The claim about *why* this matters
  perceptually for an unpitched or semi-pitched kit drum (as opposed to a tuned timpano) is weaker
  than for timpani, where a definite pitch is the explicit goal.
- inference: extending the claim to a kit tom is this page's inference that the physics (mode shape
  versus strike position on a circular membrane) does not depend on the ensemble the drum appears in;
  the source itself discusses only timpani.

## Corrections to the 2.0 page

- **"Ghost notes are a different sample, not a quieter one" (old line 40) is corrected.** In most
  multi-layer kit instruments, a ghost note *is* reached by low velocity into a genuinely recorded
  soft layer; that is the system working as intended, not a failure. The real, narrower failure is a
  library without enough recorded layers substituting a gain-scaled loud sample, or a programmer
  manually lowering a normal stroke's velocity instead of using the instrument's real soft layer. This
  page states both the correct general case and the narrower actual failure, rather than treating all
  velocity-based ghost notes as suspect. No single source drove this correction; it follows from how
  multi-layer sampled instruments are built (a matter of virtual-instrument programming practice, not
  a citable acoustics fact) and from the SPEC's framing of the error.
- **The "40 to 70" ghost-note velocity band (old lines 42, 112) is removed.** A MIDI velocity zone is a
  property of one installed instrument's velocity mapping, not a fact about drumming, and belongs in a
  calibration profile per `shared/INSTRUMENT_BEHAVIOR_SCHEMA.md`'s own three-layer split. No range
  number appears anywhere in the new page.
- **"The kit is usually a marker part" (old lines 86-89) is corrected to a narrower claim.** For most
  live-feel genres, the kit's own microtiming (especially the hi-hat or ride) is documented, in at
  least one peer-reviewed case study, to carry structured, non-random deviation that is the source of
  the part's musicality, not noise the arrangement is measured against. The new page keeps marker
  treatment as a legitimate, named choice (`realism_target`, `marker_parts_excluded`) for genuinely
  mechanical styles, but removes the default assumption that a kit, or its pulse element, is normally
  a marker. See DK-06.
- **Chokes, kettle-drum-style side-drum rolls, and cymbal construction gained real sourcing** where
  the old page had only the 2.0 labels excerpt-derived or musicianship (now standard-reference and
  inference respectively) for claims Forsyth's 1914 text actually supports at section depth
  (side-drum roll as a pressed, hand-alternating stroke; cymbal choke as a physical grab; cymbal
  tapering).
- **Stick click, pedal squeak, sympathetic snare buzz, drum tuning and muffling, brushes and hot
  rods, and rudiment-based sticking** were added as named rows/prose per the brief; rudiment-based
  sticking and rebound physics are the two additions with direct section-depth sourcing (DK-03,
  DK-04, DK-05); the others remain `inference`.

## Still to verify

- Cymbal and drumhead decay spectra, and the general physics of why a struck membrane's overtones are
  inharmonic: Fletcher and Rossing, *The Physics of Musical Instruments*, not opened this pass.
- Bow/edge/bell attack-colour differences on a ride or crash cymbal, as taught in kit-percussion
  pedagogy specifically (as opposed to the orchestral suspended-cymbal technique Forsyth documents):
  would need a kit-percussion method book or a PAS resource on drum-set technique specifically, not
  opened this pass.
- Whether the hi-hat-as-groove finding (DK-06) generalises beyond the single recording studied, across
  a named corpus of funk/soul/R&B drumming, rather than resting on one celebrated example.
- Exact timpani/tom off-centre strike position (fraction of head radius) that best balances pitched
  and unpitched mode content: Woodhouse's page states the direction of the effect, not a measured
  optimum; Fletcher and Rossing likely carries this.
