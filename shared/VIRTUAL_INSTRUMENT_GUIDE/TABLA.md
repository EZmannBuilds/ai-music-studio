# Tabla

Traditions: Hindustani (North Indian) classical and light-classical music. Context:
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`.

The tabla is a pair of small hand drums, the dāyāṅ (also dahina, or, loosely, "tabla") and the
bāyāṅ (also dagga), played together by one seated player as the principal percussion of Hindustani
music: accompanying khyal, thumri and instrumental performance, and carrying its own solo
repertoire. This page does not cover the pakhawaj, the older barrel drum from which tabla technique
and vocabulary substantially descend, or the Carnatic mridangam, covered on its own page; the two
are related in construction and history but are separate instruments in separate traditions and must
not be mixed (see `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/MRIDANGAM.md` for the contrast).

> Evidence: read at `full` or `section` depth this pass: nine articles by the tabla performer and
> scholar David Courtney (chandrakantha.com, several originally published in the Percussive Arts
> Society's *Percussive Notes*), covering construction, tuning, repair, strokes, and the cyclic and
> cadential solo repertoire; C V Raman's two founding acoustics papers (1920, 1934/35); four later
> academic papers that re-measure or model the tabla's loaded membrane (Siddharthan et al. 1994,
> Malu and Siddharthan 2000, Sathej and Adhikari 2008, Patranabis et al. 2015); one NIME engineering
> paper on an electronic tabla controller that documents the physical mechanism of the bāyāṅ's pitch
> bend (Kapur et al. 2002); and one listener's-guide essay on tabla solo structure (Chordia, not
> dated in the copy read). James Kippen's *The Tabla of Lucknow*, the standard ethnomusicological
> monograph, and Thomas Rossing's *Acoustics of Drums* were not opened this pass and are cited only
> as `standard-reference`, from their appearance in the works above. Source IDs resolve in
> `research/sources/INSTRUMENT_SOURCES.md`; claims and their limits are in `research/instruments/TABLA.md`.

## Tradition and context

### Construction

The dāyāṅ is carved from a single block of wood (teak, rosewood/shisham, or, in southern India,
jackwood; mango and similarly soft woods are avoided for poor tone and insect vulnerability), roughly
6 to 8 inches in diameter and 10 to 12 inches long, seasoned for up to two years before use, because a
heavier, better-seasoned shell resonates more fully than a light one [sourced: COURTNEY-MAKING]. The
bāyāṅ shell is most commonly brass (also copper, steel, rarely clay or aluminium), beaten from a flat
disc and a rolled cylinder joined and hammered into a bowl, then chrome-plated [sourced: COURTNEY-MAKING].

Each head (pudi/puddi) is a laminate, not a single skin: a reinforcing inner layer (bharti, itself
built from small overlapping trapezoidal goatskin pieces) sits beneath the main resonating membrane
(maidan), which is in turn overlaid at its outer edge by an annular ring (chat) that both strengthens
and damps the head; the whole assembly is bound into a woven leather hoop (gajara) that transfers and
buffers tuning tension from the lacing to the membrane [sourced: COURTNEY-PUDI]. The syahi, the black
tuning patch, is built up in many thin layers of a soot/iron-filings/gum paste, widest at the centre
and stepped down toward the edge, each layer polished with a stone until it dries into a network of
fine, mutually unconnected cracks; those cracks are what let a rigid material still flex with the
membrane, and their density is a marker of a well-made syahi [sourced: COURTNEY-PUDI + academic: RAMAN-1935]. The bāyāṅ's black patch is loaded off-centre (eccentrically), unlike the dāyāṅ's
concentric loading, for a different acoustic purpose (bass colour rather than a harmonic overtone
series) [academic: SATHEJ-ADHIKARI-2008]. Sixteen leather thongs (tasma) lace each head to the shell
through the gajara, tightened over wooden dowels (gatta); this sixteen-strap arrangement is, per
Raman, essentially universal across the drum family [sourced: COURTNEY-REPAIR + academic: RAMAN-1935].

### Tuning

The dāyāṅ is tuned to Sa, the tonic of whichever soloist it is accompanying, chosen per performance
rather than fixed to an instrument-independent pitch [sourced: COURTNEY-TUNING]. Coarse tuning moves
the gatta (wooden dowels) along the lacing; fine tuning strikes the gajara (the woven hoop) directly
with a small hammer, downward to raise tension and pitch, from underneath to lower it — in practice
most fine tuning happens this way, without touching the lacing tension itself [sourced: COURTNEY-REPAIR]. Players tune by ear to the "Naa" stroke rather than the more purely pitched "Tun",
because "Naa" is the stroke actually used in performance and, on a well-made drum, the two should
agree; the fundamental itself is commonly absent or ambiguous in "Naa" and the ear's perceived pitch
is extrapolated from strong upper harmonics, a genuinely difficult skill to develop, and one Courtney
argues could usefully train Western percussionists' ear-tuning more broadly [sourced: COURTNEY-TUNING; COURTNEY-PSYCHO]. An unevenly tensioned head shows a "cross" pattern of alternating high and low pitch
around the rim, which the same "Naa"-by-position technique is used to locate and correct [sourced: COURTNEY-REPAIR]. The bāyāṅ's baseline pitch is set by the puddi's tension and, in performance, is
then actively bent by the playing hand rather than held to one setting; see pitch_instability above
[sourced: COURTNEY-PSYCHO + academic: KAPUR-NIME-2002].

### Note production and technique

Bols are named, physically specific strokes, not a generic loud/soft/long/short vocabulary: see
articulation_logic above for the core set. Several common bols exist in more than one regional
technique — a Dilli (Delhi) and a Purbi ("eastern": Lucknow, Farukhabad, Benares) version of the same
named stroke are genuinely different hand actions with a different resulting sound, and a single
performer will often mix both within one composition for a balance of speed and clarity [sourced: COURTNEY-BOLS]. The bāyāṅ's pitch bend is produced by the heel and wrist of the left hand: pressing
harder into the head raises pitch, and moving the contact point toward the centre of the syahi also
raises pitch, and a player can glide continuously between these while the stroke is still sounding
[sourced: COURTNEY-PSYCHO + academic: KAPUR-NIME-2002].

### Idiomatic phrasing and ornamentation

Tabla composition divides into two mutually exclusive philosophies: cyclic material, which "rolls
along" without resolving (theka, and its ornamented and reworked forms prakar and kisma; kaida, a
rule-governed process of theme and variation; rela, fast pure-tabla-bol figuration; peshkar, an
opening theme-and-variation form leaning on cross-rhythm; laggi, light fast accompaniment; gat, a
fixed, purbi-style composed piece), and cadential material, which moves toward a resolution, almost
always landing on the returning sam (mukhada, a short pre-sam flourish; tihai, the tradition's central
device, a phrase repeated exactly three times so the last stroke of the third repetition lands on sam;
chakradar, three tihais nested together; mohara, paran and tukra/tukada, a short body of material
resolved by a tihai; tipali, three sections of rising density) [sourced: COURTNEY-CYCLIC; COURTNEY-CADENZA]. Which of several overlapping and sometimes gharana-specific names applies to a
given piece depends on independent criteria — structure, function/context of use, and which bol
vocabulary it draws from (pure tabla bols, versus bols inherited from the pakhawaj, versus dance
bols) — and the same composition is routinely called different things by different musicians for this
reason, not out of inconsistency [sourced: COURTNEY-CYCLIC; COURTNEY-CADENZA]. At speed, a kaida's
doubled-tempo presentation is heard by the ear as splitting into separate rhythmic lines by stroke
family (ringing, damped, bass), an auditory-streaming effect that is itself part of the intended
listening experience of a tabla solo [sourced: CHORDIA-SOLO].

Ornamentation in the orchestral sense (vibrato, trills) does not apply; the closest analogue is the
bāyāṅ's continuous pitch bend, treated above as a distinct mechanism rather than a decorative
addition.

### Performer interaction and ensemble role

In accompaniment, the tabla is responsible for holding the theka, the conventionally fixed
stroke-and-division pattern that defines a given tāl in practice (so that two tāls with the same beat
count can still be told apart by their thekas); historically vocalists and instrumentalists expected
their accompanist to play essentially nothing but theka, and while today's practice allows far more
variation and interplay, the underlying responsibility to keep the cycle legible to the soloist and
audience remains [sourced: COURTNEY-CONTRAST; COURTNEY-CYCLIC]. **Theka, sam and khali are Hindustani
terms**; the tāl vocabulary and the way tabla or pakhawaj relate to a Hindustani performance do not
transfer to Carnatic practice, where the mridangam's accompanying role is structured differently — see
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md` and `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/MRIDANGAM.md`. Where the main artist "trades off"
control to the tabla player within an accompanied performance, forms such as mohara or a short tukra
let the player assert briefly without displacing the soloist [sourced: COURTNEY-CADENZA].

### Repertoire contexts

Solo tabla is its own concert form, distinct from accompaniment, with a repertoire of composed and
improvised material (kaida, rela, peshkar, gat, and the cadential forms above) developed across a full
performance and organised against a nagma, a short repeating melody usually played by a secondary
instrumentalist, which lets the listener track the cycle the way a theka would in accompaniment
[sourced: CHORDIA-SOLO]. In accompaniment, the tabla supports khyal and thumri vocal performance,
instrumental music, and kathak dance; gat and several purbi-style forms in particular carry a
documented historical connection to kathak accompaniment [sourced: COURTNEY-BOLS; COURTNEY-CYCLIC].

### Improvisation

Both cyclic material (which admits ornamentation, restructuring, and rule-governed variation within
kaida's fixed constraints: the theme's bols must be retained even as their arrangement is permuted)
and the choice and placement of cadential resolutions are sites of real-time invention bounded by the
tāl's cycle and by gharana convention, not free improvisation in the jazz sense [sourced: COURTNEY-CYCLIC]. `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md` covers how this bounded-improvisation
pattern generalises across Hindustani and Carnatic practice; this page does not repeat that theory.

## What virtual implementations commonly get wrong

- **Strokes mapped to velocity only.** Collapsing the bol vocabulary onto one or two recorded hits and
  a velocity curve loses the tradition's actual unit of meaning: each bol is a different physical
  stroke, and several (Naa versus Tin, for instance) are distinguished by technique and resonance more
  than by loudness [sourced: COURTNEY-BOLS].
- **No bāyāṅ pitch glide.** Treating the left-hand drum as a fixed-pitch bass voice removes its most
  characteristic and most audible expressive device; skilled players play recognisably melodic shapes
  on the bāyāṅ alone by varying heel force and position through the note [sourced: COURTNEY-PSYCHO + academic: KAPUR-NIME-2002].
- **A drum tuned to the soloist's Sa, retuned to 12-TET.** The dāyāṅ's tuning is not an abstract pitch
  class; it is chosen to match a specific performance's tonic, and forcing it onto an equal-tempered
  grid independent of that context misrepresents what tuning the drum is for [sourced: COURTNEY-TUNING].
- **A looped theka with no tāl structure.** Playing theka as an undifferentiated repeating pattern,
  rather than one built from the tāl's actual division into vibhags, its sam and its khali, discards
  the entire reason the pattern exists; see `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md` [sourced: COURTNEY-CYCLIC].
- **Perceived pitch treated as measured pitch.** Because the dāyāṅ's strongly-pitched strokes often
  have a suppressed or absent fundamental, a tuning or pitch-detection process that trusts the loudest
  spectral peak, rather than modelling the ear's extrapolation, will misidentify the drum's actual
  tuned note [sourced: COURTNEY-PSYCHO + academic: RAMAN-1935; SIDDHARTHAN-1994].

## What must not be generalised outside the tradition

- Tabla technique, tuning practice and repertoire are specific to Hindustani practice and must not be
  read onto the Carnatic mridangam, which is a related but distinct instrument in a distinct tradition
  with its own construction, tuning and accompanying role; see `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/MRIDANGAM.md` and Handbook rule
  27's general prohibition on mixing traditions [sourced: COURTNEY-CONTRAST].
- Gharana differences (Delhi/Dilli, Ajrada, Lucknow, Farukhabad, Benares, Punjab) are real and produce
  genuinely different technique, repertoire and aesthetic priorities, not merely different regional
  accents on one shared practice; this page names the specific gharana-attributed differences its
  sources support (the Dilli/Purbi stroke-technique split; Lucknow's distinct release technique on
  Taa; kaida's origin in the Dilli style versus Benares's "bant") and does not extend any single
  gharana's practice to the tradition as a whole [sourced: COURTNEY-BOLS; COURTNEY-CYCLIC]. Courtney's
own teaching-FAQ material stresses this directly: different gharanas hand a player different tools,
none has all of them, and a player who only knows one gharana's vocabulary is functionally limited,
not simply "playing correctly in a different accent" [sourced: COURTNEY-FAQ].
- The "five harmonics" model of the dāyāṅ's spectrum is a well-supported approximation, not an exact
  description; treating it as an exact integer-ratio harmonic series overstates the evidence, and later
  re-measurement finds a shifted fundamental and additional, musically less significant, higher
  partials [academic: RAMAN-1935; SIDDHARTHAN-1994].

- `articulation_unavailable` is the expected result whenever a general hand-percussion or "world
  percussion" library is asked for named tabla bols; report it rather than approximating a bol with a
  different stroke or a pitch-shifted sample.
