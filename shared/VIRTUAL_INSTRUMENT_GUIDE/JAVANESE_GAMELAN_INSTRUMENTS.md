# Javanese Gamelan Instruments

Traditions: Central Javanese court and village gamelan (karawitan), sléndro and pélog tuning
systems, Surakarta (Solonese) and Yogyakarta (Yogyanese) regional styles. Context:
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`. This page does not cover Balinese gamelan, a related but
distinct tradition with different instruments, colotomic names and paired-tuning practice, covered
separately in `BALINESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`; nor Sundanese gamelan, which GAMELAN.md names as a
third, separate tradition this pack has not researched.

This is an ensemble page. Central Javanese gamelan is not only tuned bronze percussion: it includes
the bowed rebab, the suling flute, plucked zithers (siter, celempung), unpitched-adjacent laced
drums (kendhang), and voices (pesindhèn, gérong), alongside the metallophones and gong-kettles
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Each behaviour card below covers one instrument or a small group that
shares construction and playing physics; a card's differences between its members are given inside
the card's rows, not by splitting the card.

> Evidence: Ten Balungan (American Gamelan Institute journal) articles were read at full or section
> depth: pedagogy and technique notation for gendèr, gendèr panerus and gambang, written or compiled
> by named Javanese teachers (Wignyosaputro, Marsudi, Widiyanto); a performer's technique article on
> gendèr cengkok by a scholar-performer trained under named Javanese masters (Brinner); a
> practitioner-scholar's theoretical essays on balungan and gatra (Supanggah, translated by Perlman);
> an interview with a professional gamelan maker and tuner (Sukarno's interview with Panggiyo
> Resowiguna); an interview with the then-director of ASKI, the Surakarta conservatory (Devereaux's
> interview with Hastanto); and a practitioner-pedagogue's memoir of teaching gamelan outside
> Indonesia (Susilo). No manufacturer manual, acoustics paper or dedicated technical source on the
> rebab, suling or siter/celempung specifically was reached in this pass; those cards carry
> `unresolved:` rows honestly rather than invented detail. Source IDs resolve in
> `research/sources/INSTRUMENT_SOURCES.md`; the claims and their limits are recorded in
> `research/instruments/JAVANESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`.

Cross-family failures are in `COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

## Behaviour cards

### Gongs: gong ageng, siyem, suwukan

| instrument_behavior | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| physical_sound_source | A large bronze disc gong with a raised central boss, hung vertically and struck on the boss with a large padded beater; the gong ageng is the biggest of the set, siyem and suwukan (sometimes named as one size, sometimes two) are smaller hanging gongs of the same family, tuned lower than kempul. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| attack_behavior | A single, deliberate strike on the boss; a maker/tuner tunes a gong primarily by hammering (working the bronze) rather than by removing material, "so that the overall mass is not reduced," because removing mass is harder to reverse and changes the gong's character. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| sustain_behavior | Long, with the fundamental and its beating partials continuing to develop after the strike; old, cracked instruments lose this and sound "thin," and a tuner may pack a towel or sponge inside a damaged gong-kettle to help it resonate again (reported for kenong, the same bronze-gong family). | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| release_behavior | Rings until damped by hand or left to decay; decay is part of the arrival the gong marks, not an interruption of it. | inference |
| dynamic_timbre_change | unresolved: no source read in this pass measured how a gong ageng's spectrum changes with striking force. | to-verify: an acoustics source on large Javanese gong spectra at different dynamics |
| register_character | The lowest register in the ensemble; gong ageng marks the largest structural arrivals, siyem/suwukan smaller ones, both below kempul in pitch. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| practical_range | unresolved: no source read in this pass gave pitch names or a frequency for a specific gong ageng, siyem or suwukan; each set's gongs are tuned to that set's own laras, not to a shared standard. | to-verify: a tuner's or maker's account of gong ageng/siyem/suwukan pitch targets for a named set |
| tessitura | Not a melodic instrument; there is no "comfortable stretch" in the wind/string sense. | inference |
| articulation_logic | One articulation: a boss strike with a padded beater, at the metrical position the colotomic structure assigns it. | inference |
| phrase_limits | Not phrase-bound; the gong ageng falls at the end of a gongan (full cycle), the largest formal unit, and siyem/suwukan at points within it. | inference |
| transitions | Not applicable: single discrete strikes, not connected notes. | inference |
| repeated_note_behavior | Repetition is structural (one strike per cycle-position), not melodic; there is no adjacent-pitch alternation to describe. | inference |
| vibrato | None. | inference |
| pitch_instability | A damaged or cracked gong can go flat, sound "thin," or become unclear; makers correct this by hammering, and a cracked kenong-family gong may need physical packing (towel/sponge) to resonate cleanly again. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |

| feasibility | One gong, one player, one strike per required cycle-position; several hanging gongs of different sizes may be played by one player moving between them. | inference |
| ensemble_behavior | Structural/colotomic role: the gong ageng marks the end (and so also the arrival point) of a gongan; siyem and suwukan mark points inside it at a size below kempul. | inference |

### Kenong, kempul, kethuk, kempyang (mid and small punctuating gong-kettles)

| instrument_behavior | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| physical_sound_source | Kenong is a large bronze kettle gong with a boss, mounted horizontally (resting boss-up in a frame) and struck on the boss; kempul is a smaller hanging bronze gong, struck on its boss like the gong ageng family but pitched higher; kethuk and kempyang are small, high-pitched bronze kettle gongs, played in a tight pair and damped quickly. | sourced: SUSILO-1999; SUKARNO-1998 |
| attack_behavior | A single padded-beater strike on the boss for each; kethuk/kempyang strikes are characteristically short and clipped, damped almost immediately after striking, unlike the longer-ringing kenong and kempul. | sourced: SUSILO-1999 + inference |
| sustain_behavior | Kenong and kempul ring for a noticeable duration after the strike, contributing to the felt sense of a metrical unit's span (musicians ask "which kenong unit are we at" during performance, treating the kenong stroke as marking the unit); kethuk/kempyang are damped almost immediately, giving a short, dry, clicking tone that functions as a fast pulse marker rather than a ringing punctuation. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-GATRA + inference |
| release_behavior | Kenong/kempul: left to decay naturally, or damped by hand for the next strike; kethuk/kempyang: actively damped right after the strike as part of normal technique. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-GATRA + inference |
| dynamic_timbre_change | unresolved: no source read in this pass measured spectral change with dynamic for any of these four instruments. | to-verify: an acoustics source on Javanese gong-kettle spectra |
| register_character | Kenong sits below kempul in pitch in the punctuating hierarchy but is played as a rounder, more resonant "terminal" marker of its unit; kempul is higher and lighter; kethuk and kempyang are the highest-pitched and shortest of the four, and two kethuk in an ensemble are conventionally tuned a kempyung (a Javanese interval roughly a fifth) apart. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-GATRA; SUKARNO-1998 |
| practical_range | unresolved: no source read in this pass gave named pitches for kenong, kempul, kethuk or kempyang in a specific set beyond the relative-register facts above; each instrument is tuned to its own ensemble's laras. | to-verify: a tuner's account of kenong/kempul/kethuk/kempyang pitch targets for a named set |
| tessitura | Not applicable; these are punctuating, not melodic, instruments. | inference |
| articulation_logic | One struck articulation each; the instrument choice (which of the four strikes) is itself the "articulation" that marks a given position in the colotomic cycle. | inference |
| phrase_limits | Kenong marks phrase-internal terminals (a kenongan, one subdivision of a gongan); kempul, kethuk and kempyang mark finer subdivisions inside that, at fixed positions the piece's colotomic form specifies. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-GATRA |
| transitions | Not applicable: discrete strikes. | inference |
| repeated_note_behavior | The same kenong (etc.) tone recurs once per cycle by design; a tuner reports that some pitches (kempul 3 in Javanese practice generically, gong suwukan 2 less so) are notoriously hard to tune consistently and can behave "opposite" to what a hammer stroke should do. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| vibrato | None. | inference |
| pitch_instability | A struck kenong/kempul/kethuk can drift out of tune with age and damage; a maker corrects pitch by hammering (raises or lowers depending on where the metal is worked) and, for a cracked or dull instrument, by packing the inside with a towel or sponge to restore clear resonance. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| resonance | Each is its own bronze resonator; kenong is deep-bossed and mounted so the underside is open, unlike a flat saron key. | inference |
| physical_noise | The beater's contact thump is present but usually inaudible under the ring, except on kethuk/kempyang, whose short damped strokes make the attack transient relatively more prominent. | inference |
| feasibility | Typically one player per instrument (or one player covering kenong, another covering kempul, another the kethuk/kempyang pair), each executing one strike at the assigned cycle position; kethuk and kempyang are often played by a single player handling both. | sourced: SUSILO-1999 + inference |
| ensemble_behavior | Together with the gongs, these instruments mark the colotomic structure: kenong and kempul provide accentuation for some pieces and can function as "movable drones" in others (i.e. their fixed pitch recurs as a harmonic-adjacent reference rather than only as an accent); kethuk and kempyang provide the fastest, smallest-scale pulse of the punctuating layer. | sourced: SUSILO-1999 |
| recording_behavior | unresolved: no source read in this pass discussed miking practice for these instruments specifically. | to-verify: a recording-engineering source on Javanese gamelan sessions |

### Saron family and slenthem (balungan instruments)

| instrument_behavior | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| physical_sound_source | Metal (bronze, brass or iron; bronze preferred) keys/slabs mounted over a trough resonator (not individual tube resonators): demung (low), saron barung (middle), saron panerus/peking (high, often played an octave above and at double density); slenthem has metal keys suspended over individual tube resonators like the gendèr family, tuned an octave below demung, and functions as part of the balungan group despite its different construction. | sourced: SUSILO-1999; SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| attack_behavior | Struck with a hard mallet (tabuh); Yogyakarta-style saron use larger, thicker keys and mallets of hard wood specifically chosen "to produce a clear, loud tone," giving a harder, louder attack than the Surakarta style, which favours a softer balungan to leave more interpretive room for the elaborating instruments. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| sustain_behavior | A struck metal key rings; on the saron family this ring must be actively damped by the playing technique before the next stroke (see articulation_logic), rather than left to blend, which is why saron technique is inseparable from its damping. | sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985 + inference |
| release_behavior | Cut short by the damping stroke that precedes the next note, except at phrase ends where a key may be allowed to ring. | inference |
| dynamic_timbre_change | Regional style changes tone as much as dynamic: Yogyakarta favours thicker keys and harder mallets for a louder, clearer, showier tone (a set of named "moncer" techniques: nggenjot, ngecek, gemakan, imbal, pancer, kinthilan); Surakarta favours a softer, more restrained balungan tone that foregrounds the elaborating instruments. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| register_character | Demung, saron barung and saron panerus/peking occupy successive octaves, each a distinct member of the family rather than one instrument playing different registers; slenthem is the lowest, tube-resonated member of the group. | sourced: SUSILO-1999 |
| practical_range | Slendro saron family spans one octave plus two tones (e.g. 1 2 3 5 6 1 2, or 6 1 2 3 5 6 1); pelog saron spans one octave (1 2 3 4 5 6 7); the balungan gendhing itself (the framework these instruments realise) covers a wider range than the saron family alone can play — about two octaves plus two or three tones in slendro, two octaves plus three or four tones in pelog — one of the documented reasons scholars argue the balungan is not simply "what the saron plays." | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| tessitura | Each family member sits in its own fixed octave; there is no single comfortable stretch spanning the family. | inference |
| articulation_logic | Core technique is strike-then-damp: the playing hand (or, on peking/saron panerus, sometimes a technique using the mallet hand alone at speed) strikes the key, and damping (by hand or mallet-hand technique) stops the ring before or as the next note sounds, keeping the fast balungan line clean rather than blurred. | sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985; SUSILO-1999 + inference |
| phrase_limits | Governed by the piece's gongan/kenongan structure and irama level (see GAMELAN.md), not by breath or bow; saron plays every balungan beat at irama lancar and thins its role as irama deepens and the balungan becomes sparser (nibani/nggantung formats) to leave room for elaborating instruments. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| transitions | No legato in the Western sense; each balungan tone is a discrete struck-and-damped stroke, though a phrase may accelerate into tikel (double-time) balungan, especially in Yogyakarta style. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| repeated_note_behavior | balungan kembar ("twin" balungan): the same pitch repeated, often on the two upbeats of a gatra (four-beat unit); this repetition shapes how elaborating instruments (especially bonang) treat the passage, since repeated octave-doublings (gembyangan) on the bonang become burdensome when the balungan repeats a lot, which is one reason the "essential" balungan a musician hears internally is often smoothed before being written or played. | sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988 |
| vibrato | None; fixed-pitch struck metal keys. | inference |
| pitch_instability | A saron key can drift or need retuning; a maker/tuner changes pitch by grinding: thinning the middle of the key lowers its pitch, thinning either end raises it (reported for the gendèr family specifically, with saron-family instruments tuned using an electric grinder rather than a hand file because a file "would not be as efficient" on the thicker saron keys). | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| resonance | Demung/saron barung/panerus sit over a shared wooden trough resonator (not individual tubes); slenthem's keys each have an individual tube resonator like the gendèr family. | sourced: SUSILO-1999 |
| physical_noise | The mallet's attack transient against the metal key; no separate mechanism noise beyond the strike itself. | inference |
| feasibility | One key at a time per player, in strict alternation with the damping motion; a player cannot sound two saron notes simultaneously and let both ring past the next stroke. | inference |
| ensemble_behavior | Carries the balungan, the skeletal melody the rest of the ensemble elaborates (see GAMELAN.md); tuning order in practice starts from the gendèr as reference and works outward, with the saron panerus in particular needing some of its highest pitches built "by ear" in octaves because they lie above the gendèr panerus's own range. | sourced: SUKARNO-1998 |
| recording_behavior | unresolved: no source read in this pass discussed miking practice for the saron family specifically. | to-verify: a recording-engineering source on Javanese gamelan sessions |

## Tradition and context

Theory, history and aesthetics belong to `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`; this section stays on
physical construction and performance behaviour, linking to that file rather than repeating it.

### Construction

A slendro gendèr panerus, taken as a documented instance, is built from a wooden case (plangkan,
teak, roughly 91 cm long by 47 cm high in one documented instrument) with bronze keys (bilah)
suspended over the case on a cord (pluntur, roughly two metres of wound thread) run through end
pieces (denda) and held by bronze, water-buffalo-horn or bamboo pegs (sanggan); each key hangs over
its own tuned zinc or bamboo tube resonator (bumbungan), and where a resonator cannot be tuned low
enough by lengthening, a partial cap (tumbengan) further lowers it [sourced: MARSUDI-1988]. The
saron family (demung, saron barung, saron panerus/peking) instead mount their keys over one shared
wooden trough resonator rather than individual tubes, and are built at different sizes by regional
style: Yogyakarta-style saron use larger, thicker keys and harder-wood mallets than Surakarta style,
built specifically for a louder, clearer tone [sourced: SUSILO-1999; SUPANGGAH-1988]. Gamelan sets
are preferably cast in bronze, with brass or iron as lesser alternatives
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Gongs are shaped and tuned primarily by hammering (working the bronze)
rather than by removing material, "so the overall mass is not reduced"; an old or cracked gong or
gong-kettle can lose clear resonance, and a tuner may pack a towel or sponge inside a damaged
instrument to restore it [sourced: SUKARNO-1998].

### Tuning

There is no shared reference pitch across sets: a maker/tuner is asked what character (for example
"mbranyak kenes", happy and lively, or "luruh", peaceful but powerful) a set's slendro should have,
and tunes toward that character rather than toward a fixed standard [sourced: SUKARNO-1998]. Regional
tuning differs audibly even within Central Java: one tuner reports the interval between pitches 1
and 2 as wide in Cirebon pelog, narrower in Solonese, and narrower again in East Javanese tuning; the
interval between 6 and 7 is wide in Cirebon and narrow in Solo; pitch 4 sits mid-way between 3 and 5
in Cirebon but closer to 5 in Solo; and the interval between 5 and 6 is wide in Yogyanese tuning
[sourced: SUKARNO-1998]. In practice, tuning proceeds outward from a reference instrument: the gendèr
barung and panerus are tuned first (checking both the lower and upper gembyang, octave, of each
pitch for comfort), then the slenthem against the gendèr barung's lowest keys, then the demung an
octave above the slenthem, then the saron barung against the gendèr panerus; some of the saron
panerus's highest pitches lie above the gendèr panerus's own range and must be built "by ear" in
octaves [sourced: SUKARNO-1998]. A maker/tuner changes a key's pitch with a file or, on the thicker
saron-family keys, an electric grinder: thinning the middle of a key lowers its pitch, thinning
either end raises it; grinding heats the metal, and a warmed key reads flat until it cools, so a
tuner checks pitch only after cooling the key in water [sourced: SUKARNO-1998]. Some individual
pitches are reported as characteristically difficult: a maker/tuner describes Javanese kempul 3 as
prone to moving the "wrong" direction under the hammer (going flatter when struck to raise it, and
vice versa) on more than the one set discussed, and gong suwukan 2 as showing the same tendency less
severely [sourced: SUKARNO-1998].

### Note production and technique

Two documented technical logics recur. On the saron family and gendèr family, a note is a struck
tone that must be actively damped, not left to ring: the gendèr technique is documented in the most
detail, where a key is struck with a padded disc-mallet and damped by the heel of the same hand,
timed to land at or just after the following attack (except that a repeated tone following a rest is
damped during that rest instead), and most melodic activity is carried by the left hand with the
right hand playing a complementary part [sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985]. On the gendèr, gendèr panerus
and gambang, elaboration is organised as cengkok, named patterns aimed at a seleh (cadence) tone,
realised idiomatically from the balungan rather than by doubling it, and documented as varying
between individual players even for the same named cengkok: three named Solonese gendèr panerus
players' realisations of the same cengkok, transcribed side by side, differ from each other in
detail while sharing the same seleh target [sourced: MARSUDI-1988; BRINNER-1985]. On the bonang
barung and panerus, two beaters (one per hand) strike a two-row rack of small gong-kettles, and
physical reach across the rack is itself a real technical requirement: a teacher reports starting a
long-armed student on bonang earlier than usual specifically because of that reach
[sourced: SUSILO-1999].

### Idiomatic phrasing and ornamentation

Elaborating-instrument density is set by irama level, and this page carries two independently
documented, directly measured instances of that relationship. The gendèr panerus is played in four
named densities (laku): laku 2 (8 strokes per gatra, a four-beat balungan unit, in irama lancar),
laku 4 (16 strokes per gatra, irama I/tanggung), laku 8 (32 strokes per gatra, irama II dados or
irama III wilet) and laku 16/rangkep (64 strokes per gatra, irama IV rangkep and sometimes wilet),
each level exactly doubling the stroke count of the one before it [sourced: MARSUDI-1988]. The
gambang's density scales the same way in a separately documented example: one lesson piece plays at
gambang-to-balungan density 4:1 in irama I and 8:1 in irama II [sourced: WIDIYANTO-2010]. Cengkok
themselves come in named types with different functions: gantung ("hanging") patterns circle a pitch
without resolving to it; seleh ("goal") patterns move toward and land on a balungan pitch on their
final beat, and may open from above or below that goal tone depending on which continues most
smoothly from the cengkok before it [sourced: WIDIYANTO-2010].

### Performer interaction and ensemble role

Musicians divide their listening by dynamic level: in loud (louder-dynamic) sections of a piece,
the ensemble follows the bonang barung's melodic lead; in soft sections, they follow the rebab's
lead [sourced: SUSILO-1999]. The kendhang directs tempo, cues transitions between irama levels
("drumming cues the change of level," per `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`), and, in dance or
shadow-puppet (wayang) accompaniment, translates the dancer's or dalang's (puppeteer's) visual cues
into audible signals the rest of the ensemble follows [sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Kendhangan (drum
patterns) and genderan (gendèr patterns) are reported as more codified and fixed in conservatory
teaching than the patterns of gambang, celempung or rebab, which by implication leaves those three
instruments' realisation comparatively more player-dependent [sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Regional style
extends to ensemble balance itself: Surakarta style favours a softer, sparser balungan that gives the
elaborating (garap) instruments more interpretive room, audible in techniques such as milah, nauduk,
kosok wangsul, sendhal pancing and mbesut on the rebab; Yogyakarta style favours a louder, denser
balungan and gives the balungan instruments more prominence, audible in techniques such as nggenjot,
ngecek, gemakan, imbal, pancer and kinthilan [sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988].

### Repertoire contexts

Gamelan accompanies concert performance (klenengan), dance, shadow-puppet theatre (wayang) and
theatre generally, and social/state/family/religious ceremony; a Javanese saying, "yen durung
nggantung gong kuwi ora resmi" ("it's not official until the gong is hung"), reports that music,
specifically marked by the gong, was traditionally considered necessary to make an occasion formal
[sourced: HASTANTO-1989; SUPANGGAH-1988]. This page's sources describe the general ensemble and its
instruments across these contexts rather than a single named repertoire; see Restricted and
ceremonial repertoire below for what could and could not be confirmed about specific ceremonial
material.

### Improvisation

Elaborating instruments realise cengkok from the balungan according to idiom, pathet (mode) and
irama level rather than inventing freely, and the kendhang directs tempo and irama, per
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md` [inference]. Individual realisation still varies: the same named cengkok is documented
played differently, in matching detail, by three different named gendèr panerus players, which is
evidence of a bounded, idiom-governed realisation practice rather than either fixed reproduction or
free improvisation [sourced: MARSUDI-1988].

## What the instrument is

Central Javanese gamelan is a set of tuned bronze (occasionally brass or iron) percussion —
gongs, gong-kettles, and metal-keyed metallophones — played together with a bowed spike fiddle
(rebab), a bamboo ring flute (suling), plucked box zithers (siter, celempung), laced hand drums
(kendhang) and voices (pesindhèn, gérong); it is bronze-centred but not bronze-only
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Three functional groups organise the ensemble: balungan instruments
(saron family, slenthem, bonang panembung) that carry the skeletal melody; garap ("elaborating")
instruments (rebab, kendhang, gendèr, gendèr panerus, bonang, bonang panerus, siter, suling,
gambang, and the singers) that realise that skeleton idiomatically; and structural instruments
(kethuk, kempyang, kenong, kempul, gong, and others) whose combined parts mark the piece's form
[sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988]. Every set is tuned to its own laras (sléndro or pélog); there is no
shared reference pitch across sets [inference].

## Range and register

No instrument on this page has a single fixed range independent of its own set's tuning
[inference]. Where a documented range exists it is given per instrument above
as a documented instance, not a universal figure: the slendro saron family's one-octave-plus-two-
tone range against the balungan gendhing's own two-octaves-plus range is the clearest documented
case that an instrument's playable range and the music's conceptual range are not the same thing
[sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988]. The gendèr panerus's 13-14 physical keys but 9-10 played keys is a second
case of the same pattern: physical construction and playing range diverge, here for visual balance
rather than melodic reach [sourced: MARSUDI-1988].

## Articulation and note transitions

Two documented articulation logics recur across this ensemble. On the balungan and elaborating
metallophones (saron family, gendèr, and by direct evidence for damping mechanics, gendèr
specifically), a note is a discrete struck-and-damped event: the damping motion, not a separate
release trigger, ends the note, and is timed against the next attack rather than fixed in duration
[sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985]. On the elaborating instruments generally, melodic material is
organised as cengkok, named patterns aimed at a seleh (cadence) tone, realised idiomatically rather
than by doubling the balungan literally, and realised differently by different players even for the
same named cengkok [sourced: BRINNER-1985; MARSUDI-1988]. Neither logic was confirmed in this pass
for rebab, suling or siter/celempung specifically, which is recorded honestly as `to-verify` in
their cards rather than assumed by analogy [inference].

## Phrase behaviour

Phrase and density are governed by irama, the balungan-to-elaboration density ratio, and this page
carries two independently documented, directly measured instances of GAMELAN.md's density-equals-
tempo-level rule: the gendèr panerus's laku system (8, 16, 32 or 64 strokes per gatra across four
irama levels, each level doubling the last) and the gambang's density (4:1 at irama I, doubling to
8:1 at irama II for one documented piece) [sourced: MARSUDI-1988; WIDIYANTO-2010]. The kendhang
leads changes between irama levels; drumming cues the change, and the rest of the ensemble follows
[sourced: SUSILO-1999].

## Ensemble behaviour

The ensemble divides its attention by dynamic level as much as by function: in loud sections,
players follow the bonang barung's lead; in soft sections, they follow the rebab
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Regional style further shapes ensemble balance: Surakarta style favours a
softer, sparser balungan that gives the garap instruments more interpretive room, while Yogyakarta
style favours a louder, denser balungan played on larger, thicker-keyed instruments with harder
mallets, giving the balungan instruments themselves more prominence
[sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988]. Tuning order in practice starts from the gendèr as the shared reference
and works outward through the rest of the set [sourced: SUKARNO-1998].

## Programming it: the control model

Every metallophone and gong-kettle on this page is a discrete struck event, not a sustained,
continuously-controlled tone; the two documented control logics are strike-and-damp (saron family,
gendèr, where damping timing is itself the note-length control) and struck-and-left-to-ring (gongs,
kenong, kempul), with kethuk/kempyang a fast, quickly-damped special case of the latter
[sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985; SUKARNO-1998]. Density (how many strokes per balungan beat an
elaborating instrument plays) is the primary "continuous" control in this tradition, and it is set
structurally by irama level, not drawn as a per-note curve [sourced: MARSUDI-1988]. Gendèr and gambang both require genuine two-voice independence (left and
right hand play substantially different material), which a monophonic or simple-doubling patch
cannot represent [sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985; WIDIYANTO-2010].

## Programming it: what makes it sound real

Tie elaborating-instrument density to irama level using the documented ratios (gendèr panerus's
laku 2/4/8/16; gambang's 4:1/8:1), not a fixed subdivision chosen for convenience
[sourced: MARSUDI-1988; WIDIYANTO-2010]. Model the saron and gendèr family's damping as an active,
timed event, not silence after a sustain [sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985]. Let the bonang barung lead
in loud passages and the rebab lead in soft ones, an arrangement-level decision documented directly
from performer practice, not a stylistic guess [sourced: SUSILO-1999]. Vary cengkok realisation
between repetitions the way different named players are documented doing for the same cengkok,
rather than looping one fixed realisation [sourced: BRINNER-1985; MARSUDI-1988].

## What sounds fake here

A saron or gendèr line with notes left ringing into the next stroke instead of damped
[sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985]; a bonang barung or rebab present at full strength regardless of
whether the passage is loud or soft, instead of trading leadership as documented
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]; balungan density that ignores irama level, or an elaborating instrument's
density that does not scale with it [sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988; MARSUDI-1988; WIDIYANTO-2010]; a
kethuk/kempyang rendered as a long-ringing tone instead of a short, damped one
[sourced: SUSILO-1999]; a single uniform regional tone applied regardless of whether the piece is
meant to sound Surakarta-soft or Yogyakarta-loud [sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988]. See also
`COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

- Model saron/gendèr note endings as a timed damping event relative to the next attack, not a fixed
  note-length or a silent cutoff.
- Where a part calls for rebab, suling or siter/celempung technique beyond what this page resolves
  (bow strokes, embouchure, plucking technique, tuning), report `articulation_unavailable` or the
  relevant gap rather than inventing technique this page marks `to-verify`.

## What virtual implementations commonly get wrong

The most common failure is treating the whole ensemble as one undifferentiated "gamelan patch" at a
fixed density and a fixed twelve-tone-equal tuning, which erases both the irama-driven density
changes documented for gendèr panerus and gambang and the per-set tuning this page's sources insist
on [sourced: MARSUDI-1988; WIDIYANTO-2010]. A second common failure is treating
saron and gendèr notes as ordinary sustained samples rather than actively damped events, which
misses the core technical demand two named practitioner sources describe as central to those
instruments [sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985]. A third is a bonang or rebab present uniformly rather
than trading leadership by dynamic section, erasing a documented, named performance practice
[sourced: SUSILO-1999].

## What must not be generalised outside the tradition

The colotomic instrument names, positions and roles documented here (kenong, kempul, kethuk,
kempyang, gong ageng/siyem/suwukan) are Central Javanese; Balinese gamelan uses different
colotomic instruments and names, covered separately in `BALINESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`, and the
two must not be merged [inference]. Balinese paired detuning (ombak,
pengumbang/pengisep) is not a feature of Central Javanese gamelan on this page's sources and must
not be assumed present here [inference]. The regional-style contrast documented
above (Surakarta versus Yogyakarta) is specific to those two named styles and should not be treated
as a universal "soft school versus loud school" split applicable to gamelan generally
[sourced: SUPANGGAH-1988]. The technique vocabulary and teaching lineage documented for gendèr
(Wignyosaputro's teaching method; Brinner's named teachers K.R.T. Wasitodipuro, R.Ng. Martopangrawit,
R.Ng. Mloyowidodo, L.M. Harjito; the three named gendèr panerus players Martopangrawit, Ngasinah and
Sarno) are specific, named lineages and individuals, not "Javanese gamelan technique" in general
[sourced: WIGNYOSAPUTRO-1985; BRINNER-1985; MARSUDI-1988].

## Restricted and ceremonial repertoire

No source read in this pass for this page identified restricted, ceremonial or lineage-held
repertoire specific to Central Javanese court and village gamelan of the kind covered here
[to-verify: a named source on restricted or lineage-held Central Javanese karawitan repertoire].
Two adjacent facts are worth carrying forward rather than treated as settled findings
about this page's own repertoire: a Javanese saying quoted in one interview ("yen durung nggantung
gong kuwi ora resmi", "it's not official until the gong is hung") reports that music, and
specifically the gong, was traditionally considered necessary to make an occasion (a birth, for
example) formal, indicating the gong's ceremonial weight in daily and court life without itself
describing a restricted repertoire [sourced: HASTANTO-1989]; and a practitioner-tuner reports that
gamelan sets are still addressed by honorific names ("Kiyai" or "Nyai") and treated with
deference — "we have to respect them," and a composer cannot simply have players strike a gong
however a score demands — in ways that go beyond an ordinary instrument, particularly in keraton
(palace) contexts [sourced: SUKARNO-1998]. `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md` separately documents
sacred and ceremonial gamelan repertoire declined by the studio, including ceremonial genres such as
gamelan sekaten; that governing document's own sacred-ensemble example (iron *selonding* gamelan)
is Balinese, not Central Javanese, and is covered on `BALINESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md` instead. This
page's own sources did not independently identify a specific restricted or ceremonial repertoire for
the Central Javanese court/village instruments covered here, beyond the general ceremonial weight
evidenced above, and the question is left open rather than assumed resolved
[inference].
