# Balinese Gamelan Instruments

Traditions: Balinese gamelan, centred on gamelan gong kebyar (the modern, dynamic, widely-played
style most of this page's sources describe). Older and other Balinese genres (semar pegulingan,
gender wayang, gambang, selunding, angklung, and others) are named only where a source read for this
page actually describes them; they are not covered in the same depth, and a claim below should not
be extended to them without checking. Context: `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`. This page does
not cover Central Javanese gamelan, a related but distinct tradition with different instruments,
different colotomic names and a different relationship to paired tuning, covered separately in
`JAVANESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`; the two traditions must not be merged.

This is an ensemble page. Balinese gamelan is centred on tuned bronze percussion, but a gong kebyar
set also normally includes the rebab and suling (some teaching ensembles omit them), and the
kendang drum pair leads it; this page follows the sources in covering the metallophones, gongs,
gong-kettles, cymbals and drums of gong kebyar in the most depth [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Each
behaviour card covers one instrument or a small group that shares construction and playing physics;
differences between a card's members are given inside its rows.

> Evidence: Seven sources were read at full or section depth. Wayne Vitale, a composer, performer
> and Balinese-gamelan-trained practitioner (director of an American Balinese gamelan group), wrote
> two of them read in full: a technical article on kotekan interlocking technique, and, with Marc
> Paelinck, a repair-and-tuning workshop report from a Bali Summer School course. An interview with
> the Balinese composer and KOKAR (Bali's karawitan conservatory) faculty member I Wayan Sinti,
> conducted with Michael Tenzer (director of another named American Balinese gamelan group)
> participating, was read in full. A player's handbook prepared for a New Zealand School of Music
> continuing-education course was read in full. Ethnomusicological notes on the range of Balinese
> gamelan ensembles, presented by a named ethnomusicologist with fieldwork and performance
> experience in Bali, were read in full. Two academic acoustics sources measuring paired-tuning
> ("ombak") beat rates were read: one in full (an electroacoustic-composition paper analysing a
> University of Hawaii gamelan set), one at section depth (a Nihon University acoustics report
> measuring eleven Balinese gong kebyar sets). Source IDs resolve in
> `research/sources/INSTRUMENT_SOURCES.md`; the claims and their limits are recorded in
> `research/instruments/BALINESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`.

Cross-family failures are in `COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

## Behaviour cards

### Gangsa: pemade and kantilan

| instrument_behavior | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| physical_sound_source | Ten bronze keys, each suspended by leather cord over its own bamboo resonating tube; kantilan is tuned one octave above pemade, and both come in pairs tuned slightly apart (see resonance). | sourced: VITALE-1990; SINTI-1985 |
| attack_behavior | Struck with a hard wooden mallet held in one hand, producing an extremely bright, incisive metallic attack; the other hand damps. | sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| sustain_behavior | When properly tuned, the bamboo resonator enormously lengthens the key's sustain: a single undamped tone can ring over a minute before fading, demonstrated by decoupling the resonator (placing paper between key and tube), which leaves a strong attack but a fast-fading, barely audible fundamental. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| release_behavior | Damped by the hand not holding the mallet; damping must be as precisely timed as the attack, since a note allowed to ring too long "bleeds into" the following tone and blurs it, while a note damped too fast leaves a staccato gap in the texture. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| dynamic_timbre_change | unresolved: no source read in this pass measured how a gangsa's spectrum changes with striking dynamic specifically. | to-verify: an acoustics source on gangsa spectra at different dynamics |
| register_character | Kantilan (soprano-voiced) sits an octave above pemade (alto-voiced); together they are the instruments that play kotekan (fast interlocking figuration), distinguishing them from the other, lower gangsa-family members (jegogan, calung/jublag, ugal), which play the core melody instead. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| practical_range | Ten keys spanning two octaves of the five-tone pelog (selisir) scale used in gong kebyar; a standard gong kebyar set includes four pemade and four kantilan. | sourced: VITALE-1990 + academic: SHIOKAWA-2016 |
| tessitura | Each of the ten keys is in active use (unlike the Javanese gendèr panerus's partly-decorative outer keys); there is no unplayed region documented for gangsa. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| articulation_logic | Two named parts, polos and sangsih, divide fast melodic elaboration (kotekan) between players; polos is often described as the "simple"/on-the-beat part and sangsih the "differing"/off-the-beat part, but in many kotekan forms (kotekan telu, kotekan empat) the two parts' rhythmic roles are more complex and can shift or interchange within a phrase. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| phrase_limits | Kotekan figurations are built almost exclusively from three small units — a single note surrounded by rests, a single rest surrounded by notes, or a two-note group surrounded by rests — because longer unbroken runs (four or more notes, or two non-adjacent leaps) become too difficult to play cleanly at kotekan tempo; sections are punctuated by angsel (breaks) that give phrases shape. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| transitions | Kotekan is elaboration around a slower core melody (the pokok, played by calung and ugal), meeting it in unison or at the octave at structurally important points (especially primary downbeats) and taking excursions away from it between those points. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| repeated_note_behavior | In the nyog cag kotekan type, the polos part repeats on every other beat (joining the pokok at the octave) while sangsih fills the offbeats; a documented example is played at roughly MM 140, requiring about 280 notes per minute from each part and roughly 560 notes per minute for the composite figuration. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| vibrato | None; fixed-pitch struck bronze keys. | inference |
| pitch_instability | Deliberate and structural, not a flaw: each gangsa is tuned as part of a pengumbang (lower)/pengisep (higher) pair, with the two instruments' corresponding keys differing in frequency across the full four-plus-octave range of a gong kebyar set, producing the paired-tuning beating called ombak. | sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006 |
| resonance | Each key's bamboo tube resonator is individually tuned to that key, matching its frequency; the resonating chamber runs from the tube's open top to its first sealed internal node, and the higher a key's pitch, the higher that node sits (a shorter chamber). | sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006 |
| physical_noise | The hard wooden mallet's attack against bronze produces the "extremely bright metallic" transient that is part of the instrument's identity, not incidental noise. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| feasibility | One mallet, one damping hand, per player; standard gong kebyar kotekan divides the melodic material between two players per part-pair (polos and sangsih), doubled across the pemade/kantilan group so a documented eight-gangsa ensemble (four pemade, four kantilan) sounds each kotekan strand at both the unison and the octave. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| ensemble_behavior | Gangsa (specifically pemade and kantilan) are the ensemble's most prominent family by both sound and number, and normally carry the primary melodic thread via kotekan; the technique of kotekan also extends, with adjustments, to reyong and to the kendang pair. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| recording_behavior | unresolved: no source read in this pass discussed miking practice for gangsa specifically. | to-verify: a recording-engineering source on Balinese gamelan sessions |

| virtual_programming | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| note_length | Needs precise damping, not a fixed sustain: a note held too long bleeds into the next, one damped too early leaves an audible gap; a long undamped decay (over a minute) is physically real and should not be treated as a sampling error to gate away by default. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| overlap | Not legato in the Western sustained-tone sense; kotekan is built from short, precisely timed and damped strokes, so overlap here means correct attack/damp timing between two interlocking parts rather than a monophonic legato transition. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| velocity | Selects dynamic; the instrument's identity as bright/metallic is constant across the normal dynamic range documented, not a velocity-selected timbre change. | inference |

| articulation_switching | Polos and sangsih are not articulations of one part but two distinct, simultaneously-sounding parts; a patch or generator must represent kotekan as genuinely two independent, interlocking voices, not one voice with a switch. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |

| pedal_or_breath_behavior | Not applicable (hand-struck, hand-damped metallophone). | inference |
| transition_samples | Not applicable in the sampled-legato sense; kotekan "transitions" are the choice of which small rhythmic unit (note/rest/pair) each part plays next, a compositional choice, not a recorded slide. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| mic_or_room_behavior | unresolved: no source read in this pass. | to-verify: a recording-engineering source on Balinese gamelan sessions |
| likely_fake_sounding_errors | Kotekan rendered as one voice instead of two independently-played, precisely interlocking parts; polos and sangsih assumed strictly on-beat/off-beat everywhere, when several common kotekan forms deliberately complicate or interchange that relationship; paired pemade/kantilan tuned identically, removing the ombak beating that is a defining feature of the sound. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| organic_programming_methods | Model the sangsih part's timing as tightly locked to the composite pattern rather than as a Western "upbeat" with implied tension and release: Vitale reports that skilled sangsih players do not feel their offbeats as rhythmically tense upbeats but concentrate on the resultant composite pattern, playing with the same undiminished speed and technique as polos, simply placed in the gaps. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |

### Jegogan and calung/jublag (core-melody metallophones)

| instrument_behavior | behaviour | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| physical_sound_source | Five-key bronze metallophones over bamboo resonators; jegogan is the lowest-voiced (bass), calung/jublag the next register up (tenor), pitched an octave above jegogan; played with a mallet in one hand and damped with the other. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; VITALE-1990 |
| attack_behavior | Struck legato-style: the playing hand's mallet controls when a note starts, and the other hand grasps the key to stop it, so that one note stops exactly as the next starts. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| sustain_behavior | Calung is struck with a rubber-faced (soft) mallet, producing a sustained, humming tone with almost no attack transient, audibly different from the hard-mallet, bright-attack tone of gangsa even where their ranges overlap; jegogan's mallet is described as similar to a gong beater (also soft-headed). | sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| release_behavior | The damping (non-playing) hand's grasp stops the key so the outgoing note ends exactly as the next begins; this two-handed strike/damp coordination is the core technical demand of the instrument. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| dynamic_timbre_change | unresolved: no source read in this pass measured spectral change with dynamic for jegogan/calung specifically. | to-verify: an acoustics source on jegogan/calung spectra at different dynamics |
| register_character | Jegogan (bass) plays the sparsest, lowest reinforcement of the metre; calung/jublag (tenor, an octave higher) plays the unadorned core melody (pokok) that the gangsa/reyong elaborate around. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| practical_range | Five keys is the standard documented count for both, though some measured sets have six or, in one documented case, eight keys on jublag and six on jegogan, reflecting regional variation. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 + academic: SHIOKAWA-2016 |
| tessitura | The full five (or measured six-to-eight) keys are in active use; no unplayed/decorative keys are documented, unlike the Javanese gendèr panerus. | inference |
| articulation_logic | Jegogan plays very sparsely, reinforcing the metre and providing a basic backbone; calung/jublag plays the unadorned pokok (core melody) that the rest of the ensemble elaborates around, and is the melodic reference kotekan is built from. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; VITALE-1990 |
| phrase_limits | Governed by the piece's colotomic structure; jegogan strikes fall at large-scale metrical positions (documented for one 8-beat measure as beats 4 and 8), far sparser than the pokok melody calung/jublag carries. | sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010 |
| transitions | The legato strike/damp technique (see attack_behavior) is itself the note-to-note transition mechanism: no separate slide or bend, but a continuous, gapless sequence of struck-and-damped tones. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| repeated_note_behavior | unresolved: no source read in this pass described jegogan/calung-specific repeated-note technique beyond the general legato strike/damp logic. | to-verify: a source on jegogan/calung repeated-note technique |
| vibrato | None; fixed-pitch struck bronze keys. | inference |
| pitch_instability | Paired tuning applies: jegogan and jublag/calung each come as a pengumbang/pengisep pair (a documented set has two of each), tuned apart to produce ombak; in one measured University of Hawaii set, jegogan beat rates ran roughly 6.3-7.2 Hz and calung roughly 6.3-8.1 Hz across the sampled pitches. | sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006 + academic: HEARON-VANTROYER-2008 |
| resonance | Individual bamboo resonator per key, per the general gangsa-family construction documented for pemade/kantilan. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| physical_noise | The soft (rubber-faced or gong-beater-style) mallet gives jegogan/calung "almost no attack sound," distinct from the sharp metallic attack of the hard-mallet-struck gangsa. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |
| feasibility | One mallet, one damping hand, per player, per instrument; the two-handed strike/damp coordination bounds how fast a clean legato line can be played. | sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014 |
| ensemble_behavior | Calung/jublag's pokok is the actual core melody of the piece — the Balinese metaphor is of kotekan as "flowers" on the "branches" of the pokok, with jegogan and the punctuating gongs as the "trunk" — not the surface elaboration a listener's attention is drawn to. | sourced: VITALE-1990 |

## Tradition and context

Theory, history and aesthetics belong to `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`; this section stays on
physical construction and performance behaviour, linking to that file rather than repeating it.

### Construction

Gangsa (pemade, kantilan) are bronze-keyed metallophones, each key suspended by leather cord over
its own individually tuned bamboo resonator, the resonating chamber running from the tube's open top
to its first sealed node; the higher a key's pitch, the higher that node sits, giving a shorter
chamber [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Jegogan and calung/jublag share this suspended-key-over-tube
construction at five keys each, but are struck with soft (rubber-faced or gong-beater-style) mallets
rather than the gangsa family's hard wooden mallet, giving a rounded, near-attackless tone very
different from gangsa's bright metallic strike even where their ranges overlap [sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Reyong and trompong are gong-kettle rows rather than suspended-key
metallophones, closer in construction to the hanging-gong family than to gangsa
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Repair practice documents that bamboo resonators fail by cracking,
identifiable by a struck key sounding "thin," and are sealed with silicone caulk (small cracks) or a
fitted bamboo/wood patch and epoxy (larger cracks); reyong and trompong pots are tuned by grinding
the flat surface (to lower pitch) or the knob (to raise pitch, with care not to grind through it), or
by adding mass to the boss with wax or solder to lower pitch further, sometimes with metal shot
pressed into the wax for extra weight [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006].

### Tuning

Nearly every metallophone in a Balinese gamelan — gangsa (pemade, kantilan), jegogan, calung/jublag,
and, where present, ugal — is tuned in pairs: a lower pengumbang and a higher pengisep, the frequency
difference producing the beating called ombak, chosen by the tuner's taste and differing by ensemble
type [sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Documented typical ombak ranges by ensemble type:
gender wayang 3-6 Hz, semar pagulingan 6-8 Hz, gong kebyar 7-10 Hz, angklung 6-10 Hz
[sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]; a separate practitioner-scholar source gives, for the kebyar
gamelan, 6-10 beats per second, and for the older semar pegulingan, 4-8 beats per second
[sourced: SINTI-1985]; and a large-scale acoustic survey of Balinese gamelan generally (not limited
to gong kebyar) reports interference beat frequencies "generally tuned between 5 Hz and 10 Hz"
across the sets it measured [academic: SHIOKAWA-2016]. One measured University of Hawaii gong-kebyar-
type set showed beat rates in that same range across its registers: roughly 6.3-7.2 Hz on jegogan,
6.3-8.1 Hz on calung, and 5.6-8.95 Hz across the sampled gangsa pitches, with no paired beat rate
recorded for that set's reyong [academic: HEARON-VANTROYER-2008]. Ombak must stay consistent across
octaves, which is not automatic: tuning both partners in exact octaves multiplies the beat rate by
the octave ratio (doubling it), so tuners instead compress the pengisep's octave, stretch the
pengumbang's octave (the more traditional solution), or stretch both octaves (a more "aggressive"
sound common in South Bali), to keep the same beat rate at every register
[sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Reyong is conventionally tuned to pengumbang and trompong to
pengisep when the two are treated as a pair, though some tuners have experimented with the reverse;
where a set has only one ugal it is tuned to pengumbang, and where it has two, the lead (forward)
instrument is the pengumbang [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Key pitch is changed by grinding: one
tuner grinds an end of the key to raise its pitch, or the "belly" between the two suspension holes to
lower it, working in short (one-to-three-second) passes and cooling the key in water before checking
pitch, since a warm key reads flat [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Tuning starts by fixing the
interval structure on two reference pemade as precisely as possible, since the rest of the ensemble
is tuned against them [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Because of paired tuning, a Balinese gamelan
is not tuned to a shared concert pitch, and most individual instruments cannot be played with another
orchestra's set [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014].

### Note production and technique

The gangsa family's technique centres on precise, hand-timed damping matched to a hard-mallet attack:
a note allowed to ring too long "bleeds into" the next and blurs it, while one damped too early
leaves an audible gap, and this precision underlies the extreme tempos kotekan reaches
[sourced: VITALE-1990]. Jegogan and calung/jublag use a related but distinct two-handed technique:
the playing hand's mallet starts a note and the damping hand's grasp stops it exactly as the next
note starts, producing true legato rather than gangsa's discrete, damped strokes
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Reyong technique adds kecek (a percussive shoulder-strike, distinct
from the melodic boss-strike) and careful soundless-mallet damping between melodic strikes
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Kendang technique is a named vocabulary of hand and mallet strokes —
pak (a loud finger slap), de (a resonant bass palm stroke), cung (a partially damped harmonic
stroke) and dag (a bass stroke with a mallet) — recorded in one documented notation system as three
simultaneous technical layers per player (left hand's non-pitched sound, left hand's pitched sound,
right hand's stroke) [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; SINTI-1985].

### Idiomatic phrasing and ornamentation

Kotekan, the fast interlocking technique that gives gong kebyar its characteristic dense texture, is
documented as an almost certainly modern development, arising with the kebyar style itself around
the turn of the twentieth century, and is not a timeless feature of Balinese music generally
[sourced: VITALE-1990]. It divides melodic material between two named parts, polos and sangsih,
often described (correctly, as a simplification) as the on-beat and off-beat parts, but the actual
relationship is more varied: in nyog cag figuration polos is strictly on the beat and sangsih fills
the offbeats; in nyok cok figuration the two parts share anticipatory tones just before the pokok
melody's own strikes; in kotekan telu (three-tone-cell) figuration the two parts share a pivot tone,
and either part may fall on the beat depending on the cell's position; in kotekan empat (four-tone-
cell) figuration the two parts share no tone at all, but their outer tones sound together roughly a
fifth apart, producing a distinct emergent rhythm from their coincidence [sourced: VITALE-1990].
Kotekan figurations are elaboration around the pokok (core melody, played by calung/jublag and
ugal), not free invention: they meet the pokok in unison or at the octave at important structural
points (especially primary downbeats) and take excursions from it between those points, and the
Balinese describe kotekan as the "flowers" (bunga) growing from the pokok's "branches"
[sourced: VITALE-1990]. The same interlocking principle extends, with adjustments, to reyong and to
the kendang pair [sourced: VITALE-1990].

### Performer interaction and ensemble role

There is no conductor in Balinese gamelan; wherever a kendang (or a related drum) is present, it
functions as the ensemble's leader, and ketuk and ceng-ceng function as the ensemble's timekeepers
[sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. The ugal doubles as a visible cue-giver for the
gangsa section, sometimes playing in unison with it and sometimes improvising an ornamented line
(payasan) derived from the pokok [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; SINTI-1985]. A learning progression
documented for one Balinese practitioner starts on a small gangsa (kantilan), moves to the larger
pemade after roughly a month, and only after about six months progresses to leading as ugal, followed
by reyong and trompong after roughly a year, and kendang after that — evidence that the ensemble's
own instruments are sequenced by increasing technical and leadership demand in at least one
documented teaching lineage [sourced: SINTI-1985]. Because of paired tuning, a solo voice singing
against the gamelan has comparatively few notes it can align cleanly with the ensemble's own
ngumbang-ngisep pairs, which one composer reports as a real practical obstacle to combining voice
with gamelan and a reason voice historically appeared with only some genres (Arja, Janger) rather
than the large orchestral forms (Pelegongan, Gong Gede, Semar Pegulingan, Kebyar)
[sourced: SINTI-1985].

### Repertoire contexts

This page's sources describe gong kebyar most fully, spanning concert, instrumental and dance
accompaniment repertoire [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Other Balinese ensembles are named only in
passing, each with its own instrumentation and ceremonial association documented by one source: for
example, a gambang/saron ensemble used only at cremation ceremonies, several ensembles tied to
specific life-cycle or temple ceremonies, and iron-keyed selunding, found only in a few remote
villages for specific ceremonial and war-dance repertoire [sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]; see
Restricted and ceremonial repertoire below for how this page treats that material.

### Improvisation

Vitale's account of kotekan states directly that it is not free improvisation, harmonic invention or
a purely rhythmic overlay, but a highly detailed, structured elaboration of the pokok melody, learned
and combined from a small, named vocabulary of figuration types (nyog cag, nyok cok, kotekan telu,
kotekan empat) rather than invented note by note in performance [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Trompong and
reyong parts are described as "improvised" in the sense of being realised by the player from the
pokok in the moment, anticipating its tones at documented rhythmic positions, which is closer to
idiomatic realisation than to unconstrained invention [sourced: VITALE-1990].

## What the instrument is

Balinese gamelan is a bronze-centred percussion ensemble, but a gong kebyar set can also include the
bowed rebab and the bamboo suling, and is directed throughout by a hand-drum pair (kendang) rather
than a baton conductor [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. Two features distinguish
it sharply from Central Javanese gamelan and must not be conflated with it: nearly every metallophone
is built and tuned as a pengumbang/pengisep pair to produce the beating called ombak, and the fast
interlocking technique kotekan, divided between named polos and sangsih parts, is central to how the
ensemble's melodic instruments (principally gangsa, and with adjustments reyong and the kendang
pair) actually play [sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006].

## Range and register

No instrument on this page has a fixed range independent of its own set's tuning; ranges and key
counts documented above are instances from named sources, not universal figures, and some vary by
region (a jublag/jegogan with six or eight keys instead of the standard five is documented in one
measured set) [academic: SHIOKAWA-2016]. The clearest documented register layering is functional
rather than purely pitch-based: jegogan (lowest, sparsest), calung/jublag (tenor, carries the pokok),
ugal (tenor/wider range, leads and elaborates), gangsa (alto/soprano, kotekan), and reyong/trompong
(overlapping the gangsa/calung registers but built and played differently) [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014].

## Articulation and note transitions

Two distinct articulation logics recur. Gangsa (and, with adjustments, reyong) are struck-and-damped:
a hard mallet starts each note and a separate damping hand ends it, precisely timed against the
composite kotekan pattern rather than against a fixed note length [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Jegogan and
calung/jublag are struck-and-legato: the damping hand ends one note exactly as the playing hand starts
the next, producing a continuous line rather than discrete damped strokes
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Kendang technique is a third, separate logic again: a named vocabulary
of hand and mallet strokes distinguished by timbre and damping (pak, de, cung, dag), not by loudness
alone [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014].

## Phrase behaviour

Kotekan phrases are built from a small set of short units (a note, a rest, or a two-note group, each
surrounded by rests) because longer unbroken runs are too fast to execute cleanly, and are punctuated
by angsel (breaks) that give sections shape [sourced: VITALE-1990]. A documented fast passage (nyog
cag at roughly MM 140) requires about 280 notes per minute from each of the two interlocking parts,
around 560 for the composite [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Slower, structural phrase units are marked by
the punctuating gongs, at the specific named colotomic positions documented above for one piece's
eight-beat cycle [sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010].

## Ensemble behaviour

There is no conductor; leadership runs through the kendang pair (and, more locally, the ugal's
visible cueing of the gangsa section), with ketuk and ceng-ceng keeping time
[sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Kotekan divides melodic elaboration between
polos and sangsih across the gangsa (doubled at the unison and octave across four pemade and four
kantilan in a standard set) and extends to reyong with adjustments [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Ombak
paired tuning runs through nearly the whole ensemble simultaneously with this rhythmic division, so
every kotekan strand is also heard through its own beating pair [sourced: VITALE-1990].

## Programming it: the control model

The defining control-model fact for this ensemble is that pitch itself is doubled and deliberately
detuned: nearly every metallophone voice needs two instances (or two channels) tuned apart by the
ensemble's chosen ombak rate, consistent across octaves by one of the three documented
octave-treatment strategies, not by naive equal-tempered octaves on each partner
[sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. On top of that, gangsa and reyong kotekan needs genuinely two
independent, precisely interlocking parts (polos and sangsih), not one voice with an ornament layer
[sourced: VITALE-1990]. Jegogan/calung need a true legato mechanism (damp-on-next-attack); gangsa and
reyong need precise discrete damping instead [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; VITALE-1990]. Kendang
needs a named multi-stroke articulation vocabulary, not a generic drum sample varied by velocity
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014].

## Programming it: what makes it sound real

Tune every paired instrument to the ensemble's own chosen ombak rate (5-10 Hz is the documented
general range across the sources read for this page, with gong kebyar toward the upper half, 6-10 Hz,
of that range) [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006; SINTI-1985 + academic: SHIOKAWA-2016; HEARON-VANTROYER-2008],
using a stretched- or compressed-octave strategy so the beat rate stays constant across registers
[sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. Model the sangsih part's timing as tightly locked to the resultant
composite pattern rather than as a Western-feeling "upbeat," per Vitale's direct account of how
skilled sangsih players actually conceive their part [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Keep jegogan/calung's
soft-mallet legato tone distinct from gangsa's hard-mallet damped-staccato tone, since the contrast
is structural to the ensemble's timbral stratification, not an inconsistency to smooth over
[sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]. Place the kendang's cueing ahead of the ensemble's
response, and the large gongs at the specific colotomic positions their form specifies, not at
generically convenient bar lines [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010].

## What sounds fake here

Paired instruments tuned identically, removing ombak entirely [sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]; ombak present but inconsistent across octaves because the octaves were tuned
exactly rather than compensated [sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]; kotekan rendered as one voice with
an ornament instead of two independently-played interlocking parts, or with sangsih forced into a
Western upbeat feel [sourced: VITALE-1990]; jegogan/calung given gangsa's bright, damped-staccato
attack instead of their own soft, near-attackless legato tone [sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]; kendang's four named strokes collapsed into one generic drum sound
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014]; large gongs cut short instead of left to ring their full decay, or
placed at rhythmically convenient rather than colotomically correct positions
[sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. See also `COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

## What virtual implementations commonly get wrong

The single most common and most consequential failure is omitting or flattening ombak: a "gamelan"
patch tuned to twelve-tone-equal, with one instance per part instead of a pengumbang/pengisep pair,
erases the shimmering beating that multiple independent sources across pedagogy, practitioner report
and acoustics measurement agree is a defining, near-universal feature of the tuning
[sourced: VITALE-1990; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006; SINTI-1985 + academic: SHIOKAWA-2016; HEARON-VANTROYER-2008]. A
second common failure is treating kotekan as a single fast ornamental line (or a single arpeggiated
pattern) rather than two independently-played, precisely interlocking parts, which misses both the
documented performance technique and the specific relationship (shared pivot or outer tones,
depending on figuration type) that gives each kotekan type its character
[sourced: VITALE-1990]. A third is using one damping/articulation model across the whole ensemble,
erasing the real, documented contrast between gangsa's damped-staccato technique, jegogan/calung's
true legato, and kendang's named multi-stroke vocabulary [sourced: VITALE-1990; NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014].

## What must not be generalised outside the tradition

Kotekan is documented as an almost certainly modern, kebyar-era technique, and should not be presumed
present in older or other Balinese genres (semar pegulingan, gender wayang, gambuh, selunding, and
others) without a source confirming it for that genre specifically; Vitale's own article scopes its
detailed analysis to kebyar, noting the technique is used "to a lesser degree" in semar pegulingan and
is not claimed for the others [sourced: VITALE-1990]. Balinese paired tuning (ombak,
pengumbang/pengisep) is not a feature of Central Javanese gamelan, covered separately on
`JAVANESE_GAMELAN_INSTRUMENTS.md`, and the two traditions' colotomic instruments and names are
likewise distinct and not interchangeable [inference]. The specific ombak ranges documented above
(3-10 Hz depending on ensemble type) are typical figures reported by named sources for named ensemble
types, not universal constants; each set's own tuner sets the actual rate by taste
[sourced: PAELINCK-VITALE-2006]. The learning-sequence and technique detail drawn from one
practitioner's account (Sinti) and one workshop report (Paelinck/Vitale) describe those individuals'
and that workshop's documented practice; they are not asserted here as the single correct method
across all banjar (village club) and teaching lineages, which this page's sources do not survey
[sourced: SINTI-1985; PAELINCK-VITALE-2006].

## Restricted and ceremonial repertoire

One source read for this page names specific ceremonial and restricted-use Balinese ensembles
outside gong kebyar, though gong kebyar itself, this page's main subject, is documented as concert,
dance-accompaniment and general ceremonial repertoire rather than restricted [sourced: NZSM-HANDBOOK-2014; PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. A gambang-and-saron ensemble is described as used only at cremation
ceremonies, to guide the spirits of the dead to their resting place, and explicitly "never performed
as a music piece" outside that context [sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. Selunding, an iron-keyed
ensemble with no kendang, is described as found in only a few remote villages (Tenganan-Karangasem
is named specifically) for the accompaniment of the Mekare war dance and other ceremonial dances
[sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010], matching `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/GAMELAN.md`'s separate statement
that sacred iron selonding gamelan is ritual property the studio declines to sample or imitate; this
page's own source independently corroborates that selunding is tied to specific villages and
ceremonial/war-dance repertoire, without itself using the word "sacred," so the studio's declination
is carried forward from the governing document, applied here to a tradition this page's own source
confirms is ceremonially and geographically restricted in practice [inference]. Gambelan Luang, a
second iron/bronze-mixed, rare ensemble, is similarly described as used for ritual ceremonies and
temple dances, with "only a few" such ensembles existing in Bali [sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. A
pelegandjur ensemble is documented as used at temples for fetching holy water and calling on the
spirits of the dead [sourced: PANDJI-ORNSTEIN-2010]. None of this page's sources describe gong kebyar
itself as restricted; where a request calls for gambang-and-saron cremation music, selunding,
gambelan luang or pelegandjur repertoire specifically, the studio declines to imitate it and should
say why, offering gong kebyar's own concert and dance repertoire as the adjacent, non-restricted
alternative, per `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/INDEX.md` rule 5 [inference].
