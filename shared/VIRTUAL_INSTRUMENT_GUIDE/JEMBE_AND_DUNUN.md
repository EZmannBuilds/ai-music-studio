# Jembe and Dunun

Traditions: Mande jembe music (Bamako, Mali, and the wider Manden region into northeastern Guinea),
with the Khasonka dundunba tradition of western Mali covered for the dunun's construction and duo
technique. Context: `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MANDE_JEMBE_MUSIC.md`.

This page covers the **jembe**, a goblet-shaped hand drum of the Mande peoples, and the **dunun**
family of stick-beaten cylindrical drums that accompany it. Its technical detail comes from two
pieces documented through named fieldwork: **Manjanin**, a jembe-and-dunun piece from the classical
Bamako repertoire, and **Bire**, a piece of the Khasonka **dundunba** tradition from western Mali,
played by a paired drum-and-bell technique. Bamana **bòn** drumming, a related but differently
constructed Mande ensemble tradition (its drums are called cunba and ngangan, not jembe or dunun)
documented in the same source, is a different instrument family and is not covered here. Regional
naming and construction for the dunun family vary; where this page's sources did not reach a claim,
it says so rather than filling the gap from general jembe-scene pedagogy. Other Mande instruments
(balafon, kora, ngoni) are out of scope for this page.

### Dunun (dundunba and the dunun family)

The best-documented case here is the **Khasonka dundunba**, a specific double-headed cylindrical
drum from western Mali played together with a hand-held bell; Polak's Bamako-jembe-ensemble material
documents a first and second **dunun** more generally, each stick-played, without describing an
integrated bell for that context. The wider jembe-scene convention of a three-drum dunun family
(dundunba, sangban, and kenkeni, graded largest/lowest to smallest/highest, each sometimes carrying
its own **kenken** bell) is widely cited in commercial and pedagogical jembe literature (Charry is
the standard reference) but was **not** found described in these terms in the fieldwork sources read
for this page; it is marked `standard-reference`/`to-verify` below rather than asserted as fact.

## Tradition and context

### Construction

The jembe is a carved, goblet-shaped, single-headed hand drum. The Khasonka dundunba is a
double-headed cylindrical drum with goatskin membranes, documented at about 33-37 cm in diameter and
55-70 cm long; Bamako-style dunun are described only as "cylindrical, double-headed" drums "beaten
with a stick," without comparable dimensions given. Neither drum's head-tensioning mechanism (rope
lacing is standard in the wider jembe-scene literature, per Charry, but was not independently
confirmed in the fieldwork sources read here) is documented in the sources actually read for this
page. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014 + to-verify: what would settle this is not recorded]

### Tuning

No source read here gives an absolute or cents-referenced pitch for either drum. What is documented
is relative: within an ensemble, the jembe(s) carry the most differentiated pitch/timbre vocabulary
(bass, tone, slap), while the dunun(s) carry a simpler open/muted distinction at a lower, more
resonant register. Tuning as such (matching drums to each other, or to a reference) is not addressed.
[to-verify: any tuning or matching practice, in a source that addresses jembe or dunun construction
directly]

### Note production and technique

Jembe is played with two bare hands using three named strokes (bass, tone, slap), each open or
muted. Dunun is played with a single stick in the strong hand; in Khasonka dundunba practice, the
same performer simultaneously plays a small hand-held bell with the weak hand (an iron ring worn on
the thumb striking a bell fixed to the fingers), so that one player produces a coordinated two-part,
two-timbre texture alone. This bimanual drum-and-bell task is documented as measurably harder to
keep regular than playing the drum alone. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

### Idiomatic phrasing and ornamentation

Jembe phrases are described by the source's own author as not being reducible to "just rhythm":
their stroke sequences form timbre-melodies as much as rhythmic figures, and lead players are said to
value a "calm, serene, staid" recycling of standard phrases over spectacular, individualistic
soloing, at least among the professional Bamako musicians documented. Fast **échauffement** passages
are a specific, named ornament-like device tied to intensifying a dancer's solo toward a climax, not
generic fills. On the dunun side, the first/lead part's basic phrase is close to fixed and functions
as the piece's identifying "hook," which dancers sing or shout to call up the piece by name; this is
closer to a refrain than to ornamentation. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]

### Performer interaction and ensemble role

The lead jembe continually relates to the dunun's phrase, building cross-rhythm and off-beat
phrasing against it; a second jembe and/or second dunun, where present, hold short, fixed ostinato
accompaniment figures. In Khasonka Bire, lead and accompaniment roles are documented as
interchangeable between two named performers across different recordings of the same piece (each
plays lead in some recordings and accompaniment in others), and personal style differences (how even
or uneven one performer's long-short ratio is) are documented as real and audible between them.
[sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

### Repertoire contexts

**Manjanin** is documented as a jembe piece, a dance event, and a ritual institution centred on a
performance by selected pre-adolescent girls, practised across the Manden region and, since the
1960s, also staged as national folkloric ballet repertoire; it is described by the source as among
the most culturally valued compositions in the standard Bamako repertoire. **Bire** is part of a
Khasonka dundunba suite called **Jeli Dòn**, "dance of the griots," performed by professional griot
drummers (the Sacko brothers), griots being a Mande social group specialising in oral history and
performing arts. Both pieces are documented through open academic publication with accompanying
audio and video. See "Restricted and ceremonial repertoire" below for what this does and does not
settle about restriction. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

### Improvisation

Jembe performance is described as "the improvisational interpretation of pre-composed materials and
models" ("performance-composition"): a lead player draws on a stable body of thematic material
(shared across many pieces for accompaniment motives, and largely piece-specific for lead themes)
and recombines it in performance, rather than either reciting a fixed score or inventing freely. One
documented professional's recording shows standard-phrase recycling filling about 85% of playing
time. Dunun is not documented as improvising in the sources read: its identifying phrase is close to
invariant, aside from one documented standard variation that omits a single stroke. Tempo itself is
not fixed either: performances are documented accelerating substantially over their course (for
Manjanin, from about 135 bpm to 205 bpm; for Bire, from about 85 bpm to 125 bpm in one recording),
which is itself a structural, not incidental, feature of Malian dance drumming. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## What the instrument is

Jembe and dunun are two different instruments that are conventionally played together: jembe, a
bare-hand goblet drum with a three-stroke, six-articulation vocabulary, carries the most
improvisational and timbral weight; dunun, a stick-beaten cylindrical drum (sometimes paired with a
hand-held bell played by the same performer), carries the ensemble's core metrical accompaniment and
the piece's identifying phrase. Neither instrument alone is "the" Mande ensemble; the smallest
attested ensemble is already a duo of one jembe and one dunun. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]

## Range and register

Not a Western pitch range on either instrument. Jembe: three relative tone/timbre categories (bass,
tone, slap), each open or muted. Dunun: open and muted strokes on one drum, plus, in Khasonka
practice, a separate bell voice from the same player. No absolute or cents-referenced pitch data was
found for either instrument. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Articulation and note transitions

No legato in the sustained-pitch sense on either instrument. Jembe's six articulations (bass/tone/
slap, each open or muted) and dunun's open/muted stroke pair are the full documented vocabularies;
fast échauffement passages on jembe are sequences of discrete strokes, not a glide. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]

## Physical constraints

Jembe: two bare hands, so no more than two simultaneous strokes. Dunun in Khasonka style: one hand
on the stick, the other simultaneously playing a bell, which is documented as a genuinely demanding
bimanual, two-instrument task for a single performer, with measurably more timing variability than
playing the drum alone. Dunun in Bamako jembe-ensemble style: one hand, one stick, no bell described.
[sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Phrase behaviour

The dunun's identifying phrase is close to fixed across a piece; jembe's lead part balances recycled
standard phrases (documented as filling the large majority of playing time for one professional) with
occasional bridges and échauffement climaxes. Tempo itself is not held constant: both documented
pieces accelerate substantially over a performance. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Ensemble behaviour

Smallest ensemble: one jembe, one dunun (a duo). Classical Bamako style also documents trios (two
jembes, one dunun) and quartets (two jembes, two dununs); larger ensembles are described as common
today. The dunun (or, in Khasonka style, the drum-plus-bell dundunba part) is the metrical and
identity-carrying core; jembe(s) build density, cross-rhythm, and lead improvisation against it.
[sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Recording behaviour

Documented through field multitrack recordings made for research purposes; a duo-versus-trio
comparison in one Khasonka recording set found that adding a third player did not change the
accompaniment's basic timing pattern. Commercial studio recording practice for this repertoire was
not addressed. [sourced: POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Programming it: what makes it sound real

- Identify which piece and which regional style (classical Bamako jembe ensemble, or Khasonka
  dundunba) a part is modelling before deciding whether the dunun part includes an integrated bell.
  [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- Use the named stroke vocabularies (jembe's bass/tone/slap, each open or muted; dunun's open/muted)
  as the articulation set, not a generic hand-drum patch with velocity layers standing in for
  genuinely different strokes. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]
- Model tempo acceleration across a performance where the brief calls for a traditional-style
  rendering; a fixed click tempo is a documented departure from how these pieces are actually played.
  [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- Where a drum-and-bell dundunba part is modelled, give the bell measurably looser timing than the
  drum, reflecting the real bimanual coordination load, rather than machine-locking the two.
  [sourced: POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- Use échauffement (fast lead-jembe passages) as a structural device tied to a dance climax, and keep
  the dunun's identity phrase close to fixed as the reference the rest of the ensemble is heard
  against, per `COMMON_ERRORS.md` item 13. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]

## What sounds fake here

The general list is in `COMMON_ERRORS.md`. Family-specific tells:

- jembe's six-articulation stroke vocabulary collapsed into two or three velocity layers; [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]
- a Khasonka-style drum-and-bell part machine-locked into perfect sync instead of carrying the real,
  measured extra looseness of the bell relative to the drum; [sourced: POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- a dunun identity phrase that wanders or varies freely, when the documented norm is closer to fixed;
  [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]
- a fixed, unchanging tempo across a whole piece, when both documented pieces accelerate
  substantially; [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- three named dunun sizes with three bells assumed present by default, when the fieldwork sources
  read for this page document at most one drum and one bell per performer. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## What virtual implementations commonly get wrong

The most common failure named in `CULTURALLY_SPECIFIC_INSTRUMENTS.md` — a fixed-pitch sample patch
with uniform velocity, standing in for the whole tradition — applies here as: **treating "jembe" as
one generic hand-drum sound rather than a six-articulation vocabulary**, and **treating "dunun" as
one fixed drum-and-bell combination rather than a construction and technique that is documented here
to vary by region and style**. A further, specific failure this page's sources correct directly is
**quantising jembe or dunun timing to an isochronous grid and presenting the result as the
tradition**: the wider Mande drumming literature this page's system file draws on documents
systematically non-isochronous beat subdivision as a real, inherent feature of the repertoire, not a
performance imperfection to be corrected by quantising. See
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MANDE_JEMBE_MUSIC.md`. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## What must not be generalised outside the tradition

- **Mande jembe music is not "African percussion" and not interchangeable with Ewe dance-drumming or
  any other West African ensemble tradition.** See `EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md` for a differently
  constructed, differently played ensemble from a different people, region, and repertoire. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010]
- **Bamana bòn drumming, documented in the same source as Khasonka dundunba music, uses different
  instruments (cunba, ngangan) and is not covered by this page**; do not extend jembe/dunun technique
  or construction to it. [sourced: POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- **The dunun's construction and technique are not one fixed thing across the Mande region.** Whether
  a performer plays drum and bell together (documented for Khasonka dundunba) or the drum is played
  alone with a stick (documented for classical Bamako jembe ensembles), and whether the ensemble uses
  one dunun or several, are documented regional and stylistic variables, not constants of "the
  dunun." [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]
- **The commonly marketed three-drum dunun family (dundunba, sangban, kenkeni, each with a kenken
  bell) is a real convention in parts of the wider jembe scene (Charry is the standard reference) but
  was not documented in these terms in the fieldwork sources read for this page.** Do not present it
  as confirmed by the sources this page actually opened; it is marked `to-verify` on the dunun card.
  [standard-reference: CHARRY-2000]
- Manjanin and Bire are two specific, named pieces from two specific regional styles (classical
  Bamako jembe ensemble; Khasonka dundunba). Do not present either piece's documented phrases,
  tempos, or timing ratios as generic facts about "jembe music" or "dunun music" as a whole. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## Restricted and ceremonial repertoire

Neither source read for this page documents shrine-secrecy or an explicit performance restriction
comparable to what `EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md` records for Yewevu. **Manjanin** is described as a "ritual
institution" centred on the initiation of selected pre-adolescent girls into "specialized
ritualistic knowledge," but the drumming and dance themselves are documented as publicly performed,
staged as national folkloric ballet repertoire since the 1960s, and published in an open academic
article with accompanying audio and video; nothing in the source suggests the music is restricted to
initiates, though the specific ritual knowledge the girls receive may be. **Bire** is professional
griot repertoire, performed by named, professional drummers and likewise openly published with
audio and video; griot status is a hereditary occupational specialisation, not a secrecy-holding
office in the sense Yewevu documents. **This page's finding is genuinely mixed rather than clean**:
the drumming is public and documented, but Manjanin's initiatory dimension is real and this page's
sources do not settle whether any part of that dimension (beyond the publicly performed music) is
restricted. Where a request specifically asks to reproduce the initiation content of Manjanin, or any
other Mande repertoire this page has not examined, the studio says the question is open and asks
the user what they know of that repertoire's context, rather than proceeding as unrestricted. The
publicly documented drumming and dance material itself is the adjacent, usable repertoire this page's
technical claims are drawn from. [sourced: POLAK-JEMBE-2010; POLAK-LONDON-2014]

## What the Performance Director needs from this file

- `performance_reference` should name the specific piece and regional style (classical Bamako jembe
  ensemble, or Khasonka dundunba) being modelled, since dunun construction and technique differ
  between them.
- `timing_character` for a traditional-style render should include a documented, named tempo
  acceleration (see `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MANDE_JEMBE_MUSIC.md` for the measured ratios) rather
  than a fixed tempo, and should treat beat subdivision as non-isochronous per that file rather than
  quantising it.
- `marker_parts_excluded` should include the dunun's identity phrase by default; where a
  Khasonka-style bell is modelled, it should **not** be excluded from timing variation the way a
  marker part normally is, since the sources document it as measurably less regular than the drum it
  accompanies, an `ensemble_spread`-like effect from one performer's bimanual coordination rather
  than a steady reference.
- `articulation_unavailable` is the expected outcome when a general-purpose percussion library is
  asked for jembe's six-stroke vocabulary or a Khasonka-style integrated drum-and-bell part; report
  it rather than substituting a generic stroke.
- A request touching Manjanin's initiatory content specifically routes to "Restricted and ceremonial
  repertoire" above and the feasibility report records the open question rather than proceeding.

## Sources and what to verify

Full citations and read depth are in `research/sources/INSTRUMENT_SOURCES.md`; the claims and their scope and
limits, record by record, are in `research/instruments/JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md`. In summary: both Rainer Polak
articles are ethnomusicology built on long-term, named fieldwork and apprenticeship with named
professional drummers, read at section depth, covering construction, stroke vocabulary, ensemble
configuration, tempo, and (for Khasonka dundunba specifically) the drum-and-bell technique in real
technical detail. Eric Charry's *Mande Music*, the field's standard reference, was not independently
opened for this pass and is cited only by attribution, at `standard-reference`, mainly for the
widely-marketed three-drum dunun family convention that Polak's fieldwork material did not itself
describe in those terms.

**To verify, before this page's claims are extended further:** the jembe's and dunun's
head-tensioning/tuning mechanism, and any absolute or measured pitch data; whether the three-drum
dunun family (dundunba/sangban/kenkeni) applies to the specific regional styles this page covers, in
a source that documents it directly rather than by general jembe-scene convention; conventional
recording/miking practice for this ensemble; and whether jembe pitch can be actively bent by hand
pressure, which was not addressed in the sources read.
