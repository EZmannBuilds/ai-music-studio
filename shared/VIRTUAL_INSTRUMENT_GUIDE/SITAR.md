# Sitar

Traditions: Hindustani (North Indian) classical music. Context: `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`
 and `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/DRONE_TRADITIONS.md` (for the sitar's own drone
strings, chikari, and its relationship to the tanpura). The related Carnatic veena is a different
instrument with its own construction and technique and is not covered here.

The sitar is a long-necked, movable-fretted plucked lute with a large gourd resonator, six or seven
main playing strings, a set of sympathetic (taraf) strings, and, like the tanpura and veena, a
curved "jawari" bridge that gives it its characteristic buzzing timbre. Where a claim below is
specific to one teaching lineage, it is named as such: the practitioner source read for this page,
Ravi Shankar's sitar manual, teaches the Maihar gharana's method (Shankar trained under Ustad
Allauddin Khan), and no comparably deep source for another gharana (Imdadkhani/Etawah in
particular) was reached in this research pass.

> Evidence: the bridge/dispersion physics rests on C. V. Raman's 1921 founding paper and two
> overlapping physical-modelling papers by Sadjad Siddiq (2010, 2012), all read in full. The taraf
> settings, the measured reach of meend, and players' own account of controlling sympathetic
> resonance rest on ethnomusicology with named fieldwork at the ITC Sangeet Research Academy
> (Weisser, Demoucron and Leman, 2012, read in full). Construction, tuning, playing position,
> mizrab strokes and left-hand ornament technique rest on Ravi Shankar's sitar self-instruction
> manual, read at section depth. Source IDs resolve in `research/sources/INSTRUMENT_SOURCES.md`;
> claims and their limits are recorded in `research/instruments/SITAR.md`.

Cross-family failures are in `COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

## Tradition and context

### Construction

The sitar is built from teak, with a large gourd resonator; Shankar's own manual describes six or
seven main playing strings and, on instruments with sympathetic strings, nine to thirteen taraf
(his count; Weisser, Demoucron and Leman separately report eleven to thirteen for the instruments
they studied), plus, on some builds, a second small resonating gourd partway down the neck
[sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012]. Frets are movable, tied on with gut or silk thread
rather than fixed, which lets a player retune individual scale steps (see Tuning); Shankar's manual
states 19 to 23 frets are found in practice, with 19 standard, and recommends adding a 20th so the
upper-octave Ma does not need to be pulled into tune [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. Sitar tradition
attributes the instrument's movable frets, and its name, to the 13th/14th-century poet-musician
Amir Khusru, who is said to have reversed the veena's string order and replaced its fixed,
wax-set frets with a movable, thread-tied system; this is reported here as a traditional
attribution carried in a practitioner's own account, not as verified organological history
[sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. Both the playing strings and the taraf pass over their own curved jawari bridge; the
bridge surface (not literally flat, despite the term used in the acoustics literature) is what
produces the buzzing spectral character common to the tanpura, veena and sitar
[sourced: RAMAN-1921; SIDDIQ-2012].

### Tuning

The tonic Sa is not fixed in absolute pitch: Shankar reports it may fall anywhere from about B
natural to D natural depending on the individual instrument's size and strength, with C sharp a
common choice on his own instruments [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. Certain scale steps (komal Ri, komal
Dha, and, in some scales, komal Ga) are not available with the frets in their default position and
require physically sliding specific frets toward the pegs, a manual retuning step separate from
tuning the open strings [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. The taraf strings are tuned according to the raga
being played, in a documented range of roughly 280 to 750 Hz [sourced: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012];
Shankar's manual gives worked examples of which notes the taraf set is tuned to for specific named
ragas (for instance, an evening raga such as Yaman Kalyan), as part of his graded exercises
[sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. These are documented instances from one teaching method, not a general
table valid for every gharana or performer's taraf set [inference].

### Note production and technique

The mizrab (a wire plectrum) is worn on the right index finger and struck in two named directions,
da (outward, away from the thumb) and ra (an inward follow-stroke); the right thumb stays fixed on
the edge of the neck throughout playing as an anchor point [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. The two left-
hand fingers most used are the index and middle; they stop the string just behind a fret, not
directly on top of it, to avoid muffling the tone [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. Left-hand-only
techniques (krintan: cut, slip, hammer; zamzama; gamak) sound additional notes without a new
mizrab stroke, and meend pulls the string sideways at a fixed fret to bend its pitch, reaching more
than a sixth in one documented case, without moving to a new fret [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012]. Because a taraf string near the departed pitch keeps ringing during a
meend, the resulting sound is acoustically two overlapping components (the sliding main-string
pitch and the decaying taraf resonance), which is both a defining timbral feature and, for anyone
building automatic melody-tracking, a genuine ambiguity [sourced: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012].

### Idiomatic phrasing and ornamentation

Jhala, the fast, rhythmically driving final portion of a raga's unfolding (see
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`), is where the chikari drone strings are struck most
densely, interleaved with the melodic line at speed; a single melodic pluck can be followed by
several chikari strokes in this section, functioning as a rhythmic-drone "ground" against the
melodic "figure" [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012]. Metrical accent within a
rhythmic group is marked by a stressed da stroke on the first note of the group
[sourced: SHANKAR-1968].

### Performer interaction and ensemble role

The sitar is normally the solo melodic voice, with a tabla player and a drone (tanpura or an
electronic substitute) accompanying; see `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md` for the general
Hindustani instrumental ensemble and `TANPURA.md`/`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/DRONE_TRADITIONS.md` for
the drone's role. The chikari strings give the sitar player a second, internal drone/rhythm layer
independent of the external tanpura [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012]. Players report
having comparatively few means of controlling how strongly the taraf responds during performance,
compared to (for example) a sarangi player: the direction of plucking (da or ra), the choice of
plucking technique, and the strength of the pluck are what they name
[sourced: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012].

### Repertoire contexts

Solo and small-ensemble Hindustani instrumental performance generally, following the
alap-jor-jhala-gat structure documented in `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`; nothing in
the sources read here scopes the sitar to a narrower repertoire than that.

### Improvisation

Governed by the same raga- and tala-bounded improvisation described in
`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/RAGA_AND_TALA.md`; the sitar-specific technical vocabulary documented above
(meend, krintan, zamzama, gamak, chikari punctuation) is the material that improvisation is built
from, per Shankar's own graded teaching method [sourced: SHANKAR-1968].

## Physical constraints

One player; one melodic line on the playing strings at a time; the chikari struck as punctuation,
not as simultaneous polyphony; meend range bounded by how far a string can be pulled at a given
fret (more than a sixth is documented in one case, not a guaranteed maximum); left thumb fixed on
the neck edge throughout, so hand position shifts happen along the neck rather than by thumb
movement [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012].

## Programming it: the control model

A sitar part needs: a genuinely continuous pitch path for meend (per-note bend or MPE, bend range
declared explicitly and wide enough to cover more than a sixth, per `shared/TUNING_AND_MPE.md`
sections 3 and 7, not a guitar-style bend range); a separate mechanism, or at minimum a deliberate
choice, for krintan-style un-plucked left-hand notes versus ordinary mizrab-struck notes;
recognition that the taraf and chikari are separate sounding layers from the melodic line, not
folded into one patch's sustain; and velocity used as a da/ra and accent selector rather than a pure
loudness control [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012].

## Programming it: what makes it sound real

Alternate da/ra strokes for repeated notes and accent the first note of a rhythmic group, rather
than repeating one identical stroke; represent meend as a continuous bend gesture, not a
re-triggered note; let a taraf/chikari layer ring under the melodic line rather than omitting it;
preserve the jawari buzz rather than choosing a "cleaner" patch, since the buzz is the instrument's
identity, not a flaw [sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012; SIDDIQ-2012].

## What sounds fake here

A meend played as two separately triggered discrete pitches; krintan/gamak/zamzama played as
re-picked notes; a taraf-free or chikari-free "solo sitar" patch; jhala rendered without the
characteristic density of chikari punctuation; any sitar patch with no buzz in the tone at all.
[sourced: SHANKAR-1968; WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012; SIDDIQ-2012] See also `COMMON_ERRORS.md`.

## What the Performance Director needs from this file

- Meend needs a declared, sufficiently wide bend range and a genuine per-note or monophonic bend
  path; `articulation_unavailable` should be reported, not silently approximated with a fixed
  guitar-style bend range, if the instrument in use cannot cover the interval called for.
- Krintan techniques (cut/slip/hammer), zamzama and gamak are left-hand articulations without a new
  pluck; where the available patch has no such transition recorded, report
  `articulation_unavailable` rather than substituting a re-picked note.
- Chikari and taraf are separate sounding layers, not decoration folded into the melodic patch's
  sustain; a plan should represent them as their own parts or layers, per
  `shared/HUMAN_PERFORMANCE_SCHEMA.md`'s `performance_state` structure.
- `performance_state.performance_reference` should record that the technique vocabulary here is
  Ravi Shankar's Maihar-gharana teaching method specifically, not "the sitar" generically; a brief
  calling for a different gharana's style should say so and should not assume this page covers it.

## What virtual implementations commonly get wrong

Most sampled or modelled sitar patches are built around a keyboard-triggered, constant-vibrato
long-note sample with a pitch-bend wheel standing in for meend, at a bend range set for Western
guitar-style bends rather than the more-than-a-sixth range documented here
[sourced: WEISSER-DEMOUCRON-2012]. This misses: the taraf's own decaying resonance during a meend
(see release_behavior); the distinction between a mizrab-struck note and a krintan/gamak/zamzama
note produced entirely by the left hand; and the chikari's function as a separate rhythmic/drone
layer rather than a texture folded into the main patch. A "clean," non-buzzing sitar sample or
model also misrepresents the instrument: the buzz is the documented physical consequence of the
jawari bridge, not noise to be engineered out [sourced: RAMAN-1921; SIDDIQ-2012].

## What must not be generalised outside the tradition

The technique vocabulary and pedagogy documented here (da/ra naming, the specific krintan/zamzama/
gamak definitions, the taraf tuning examples, the playing-position instructions) is Ravi Shankar's
Maihar-gharana teaching method, developed explicitly for teaching sitar to students without access
to a teacher in India [sourced: SHANKAR-1968]. It should not be presented as "the" sitar technique:
other gharanas, Imdadkhani/Etawah in particular, are described in general knowledge as differing in
emphasis (often described as closer to vocal, gayaki-style phrasing), but no source at section
depth for that lineage's specific technique was reached in this research pass, and no claim here
should be extended to it [to-verify: what would settle this is not recorded]. Nor should the sitar's technique vocabulary be assumed to
transfer to the surbahar, sarod or veena, which are related but distinct instruments with their own
construction and idiom, per `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/INDEX.md` rule 2.
