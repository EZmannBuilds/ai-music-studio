# Clave and timeline patterns

## Cultural context

A **timeline** is a short, repeating, timbrally distinct pattern that the rest of an ensemble is
heard against. Timelines are central to a large family of practices: West African ensemble
drumming, and the Afro-diasporic musics of Cuba, Brazil, Haiti and elsewhere. **Clave** is the
Cuban form of this idea, and the name of the instrument that plays it.

The internal distinctions matter. Ewe dance-drumming in Ghana, Mande jembe music in Mali, Cuban
son and rumba, Haitian rara and Brazilian samba each have named repertoires, named patterns and
specific social functions. "Afro-Cuban rhythm" is not one thing, and "African rhythm" is even less
one thing (Agawu, 2003). This file covers the shared logic; `WEST_AFRICAN_POLYRHYTHM.md` covers
the West African side in more depth.

## Pitch organisation

Not applicable as a pitch system. Timeline practice is a rhythmic and organisational logic, and it
appears under many different pitch systems. Where harmony is present (Cuban son, salsa, timba), it
is usually a functional or loop-based harmony that is **aligned to** the clave rather than
generated by it.

## Rhythm and cycle

The cycle is symmetric: 12 pulses, or 16. The timeline inside it is **asymmetric**. That tension
is the point. The pattern is neither the metre nor the melody; it is a reference that both are
measured against.

Two families in common use:

```text
12-pulse cycle, the standard pattern (a seven-stroke figure over twelve eighth notes)
16-pulse cycle, son clave and rumba clave, two bars of eight pulses
```

**Tresillo** is the durational pattern 3-3-2 within eight pulses, and in Cuban usage names the
first three strokes of son clave. **Cinquillo** is the related five-stroke cell. Both work as
sub-cells that can be redistributed among instruments.

**3-2 versus 2-3 is an orientation decision, not two different rhythms.** The pattern is the same
necklace; what changes is which half of it coincides with the start of the harmonic or lyrical
phrase. Arrangers make that decision for a section, and once made, everything else respects it. A
song that changes orientation without intending to is "crossed", and it is audible to anyone
inside the practice.

Toussaint (2013) describes timelines geometrically, as necklaces on a cycle, with rotations of a
pattern belonging to the same necklace, and notes that the common timelines are near-maximally
even (Euclidean) distributions. That account is useful and it is **a description from outside**,
not the tradition's own theory. See the cautions below.

## Phrase structure and form

Form is built by what aligns to the timeline. Sections are often long stretches of a repeating
cycle whose interest comes from changes of layer, density and call-and-response rather than from
chord movement. In Cuban forms, the shift from a song section into the montuno is a shift into a
more insistent, more call-and-response-driven relationship with the clave.

Bass and comping figures are written relative to the pattern, with named landing points in Cuban
practice (Penalosa, 2009, discusses main beats and secondary beats, bombo and ponche, and the way
tresillo nests into both the three side and the two side of clave).

## Ornamentation

Not the right frame here. Variation happens as density and displacement in the supporting parts
and in the lead drum, not as melodic decoration of the timeline. The timeline itself does not get
ornamented: its value is that it does not change.

## The role of improvisation

The lead drum, the soloist and the singer improvise. The timeline does not. Improvisation is
bounded by the cycle and by the orientation: a phrase can cross the pattern for tension, but the
pattern remains the reference, and the crossing is heard as a crossing.

## Ensemble behaviour

Layers with distinct roles: the timeline instrument (bell, claves, woodblock), supporting drums or
percussion with fixed interlocking parts, a bass part with its own relationship to the cycle, a
comping instrument, and a lead voice or lead drum that varies. Everyone hears their part
**against** the timeline rather than against a conductor's beat.

## What generalises

- A short asymmetric ostinato inside a symmetric cycle gives a groove a reference that is not the
  downbeat. This works in any genre and does not require the tradition's instruments.
- Deciding an orientation (which half of the pattern the phrase starts against) and then holding
  every part to it is a real arranging discipline, useful anywhere with a two-bar loop.
- Sub-cells such as 3-3-2 can be distributed across instruments so that no one part states the
  whole figure.
- Aligning bass, comping and vocal phrasing to a common reference produces interlock rather than
  stacked independent parts.

## What must not be casually universalised

- **Do not call the bell pattern "the metre".** It is a reference ostinato inside the metre.
  Notating it as a time signature misdescribes what the players are doing.
- **Do not assume the downbeat is where a Western listener first hears it.** Timeline music
  routinely places the pattern so that the naive entry point is wrong, and the tradition's own
  count differs.
- **Euclidean tidiness is a description, not the tradition's theory.** Toussaint's necklaces
  explain why these patterns feel balanced. They do not explain what the pattern means, which
  repertoire it belongs to, or how it is taught.
- Do not move a named pattern between traditions and keep the name. A Cuban son clave is not a Ewe
  bell pattern with a different label.
- Do not treat these musics as rhythm-only source material for a track that is otherwise
  unchanged.

## Working with this in the studio

**Composer** sets: the cycle length in pulses, the timeline pattern written as a pulse grid, the
orientation (which half aligns with the phrase start), the sub-cells in use, and the alignment of
bass and comping to named landing points.

**Performance Director** needs: the orientation, whether the grid is isochronous (see
`WEST_AFRICAN_POLYRHYTHM.md`, where it often is not), and the intended feel of the supporting
parts, which is usually not straight quantisation.

**MIDI Builder and Plugin Auditor** must check that the percussion instrument has distinct enough
timbres for the timeline to cut through the supporting layers, since timbral distinctness is part
of how the reference works. Retuning is not usually a concern here. Grid resolution is: a 12-pulse
cycle needs a triplet-capable grid, and forcing it into sixteenths will misplace strokes.

**Listener Model** should not assume the listener hears beat one where the sequencer's bar line
is, and should not score an "off" backbeat as syncopation against a norm the music does not hold.
