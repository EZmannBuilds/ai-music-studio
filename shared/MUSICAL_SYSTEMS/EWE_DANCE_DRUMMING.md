# Ewe dance-drumming

## Cultural context

Ewe dance-drumming is the ensemble drumming, singing, and dance practice of the Anlo Ewe people of
southeastern Ghana, southern Togo, and adjacent Benin. Named repertoires belong to occasions, dances,
and social groups: **agbadza** is social and funerary dance-drumming, documented in detail through
David Locke's critical edition built on his apprenticeship with Gideon Foli Alorwoyie; **Drum Gahu**
is a purely recreational social dance, historically adopted from a neighbouring people; **Yewevu** is
the drum music of the Yeve religious order. This file treats Yewevu as restricted repertoire and does
not describe its musical content; see "What must not be casually universalised" below and, for the
full reasoning, `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md`, "Restricted and ceremonial
repertoire." Physical instrument construction and playing technique (gankogui, axatse, kagan, kidi,
sogo, atsimevu) live on that instrument page, linked throughout rather than repeated here.

Agawu (2003) argues that this music is **not categorically other**: treating Ewe (or any West
African) rhythm as a different kind of thing from rhythm elsewhere is a habit of representation, not
a finding. A composer's task here is ordinary musical study of an unfamiliar, specific repertoire,
not an encounter with an alien system.

## Pitch organisation

Largely not applicable in the melodic-scale sense. The drums carry relative pitch/timbre vocabularies
set by strike technique and position, not a tuned scale (see the instrument page for the full
per-drum stroke vocabularies); song melody follows its own conventions, structured by a
leader-and-group call-and-response, which is a matter for `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/CHOIR_AND_VOICE.md` and the Vocal
Director, not for this file.

## Rhythm and cycle

The **standard pattern** is a seven-stroke figure spanning twelve eighth notes (Agawu, 2006, not
independently re-read this pass; see "Sources and confidence"), played on the double bell
(gankogui) as the reference layer a whole texture of handclaps, rattles, support and response drums,
lead drum, and voices is heard against. David Locke's transcription of agbadza confirms the bell
plays this pattern without variation for the duration of a piece.

The twelve-pulse cycle supports **simultaneous four-beat and six-beat hearings**, and the music uses
that deliberately. Locke's *metric matrix* (Music Theory Online 16.4, 2010) is a hybrid theory in
which the cross-rhythm is not an ambiguity to be resolved but a structure that different parts, and
different listeners, occupy at once:

```text
12 pulses
heard as 4 beats of 3   |x . . |x . . |x . . |x . . |
heard as 6 beats of 2   |x . |x . |x . |x . |x . |x . |
the bell pattern sits across both
```

**Whether this music has metre in the Western sense is an open question in the literature.** Agawu
says yes, and says it is not exotic. Locke says yes, as a simultaneous four-and-six matrix. This file
does not pick one of these silently, and it does not extend Rainer Polak's non-isochrony findings for
Mande drumming (`shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MANDE_JEMBE_MUSIC.md`) to Ewe material, which this pass did
not find measured.

## Phrase structure and form

Form is driven by the dance and by the lead drum (sogo or atsimevu, depending on the piece and
ensemble). The lead drummer signals section changes, entries, exits, and dance figures, and closes a
piece with a fixed ending-signal phrase; documented lead-drum rolling passages cue dancers directly,
rather than functioning as generic fills. Call-and-response is the primary formal device at two
levels: between lead drum and response drum, and, independently, between a song leader and a chorus.

## Ornamentation

Not decoration of a melodic line: the lead drum's pitch/timbre vocabulary is itself read as **vugbe**,
"drum speech," a voice surrogate taught through vocal syllables (vocables), with named fast
connective figures functioning like the joining of syllables into words, and a documented
duration-shortening technique explicitly compared to closing the mouth after a spoken sound. Variation
lives in this speech-like phrase vocabulary and in the response drum's bounded theme choice (see next
section), not in melodic ornament.

## The role of improvisation

The lead drummer improvises within a vocabulary of known phrases carrying meaning to dancers and to
the ensemble, balancing steady recycling of standard material against occasional thematic
development; one documented player's recycling filled roughly the great majority of playing time.
**A prior version of this pack's material stated that Ewe supporting parts do not improvise at all.
That is corrected here.** The response drum (kidi) does not improvise freely, but it actively
**answers** the lead drum's specific calls, selecting among a memorised inventory of response themes
tied to those calls and to the bell phrase. Freedom is concentrated in the lead role and is real, but
bounded, in the response role; the bell, rattle, and support drum are not documented as varying
within a piece.

## Ensemble behaviour

Stratified roles, all measured against the bell: bell (gankogui) and rattle(s) (axatse) hold the
timeline layer; the support drum (kagan) holds a fixed offbeat-marking part; the response drum (kidi)
holds and actively answers within a bounded theme inventory; the lead drum (sogo and/or atsimevu)
calls, leads, and, when not leading, may double the response drum's part instead. Each part is
timbrally distinct, which is how a listener separates the layers. Construction and technique for
every instrument named here are on `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md`.

## What generalises

- Building a texture where parts are heard against a steady reference layer, rather than against a
  conductor's beat, is a transferable arranging approach.
- Deliberately sustaining two hearings of one cycle (four against six) gives a groove depth without
  adding notes.
- A supporting part can be genuinely responsive to a lead part without being freely improvised: a
  bounded, known inventory of answers, chosen in relation to specific calls, is a real third option
  between "fixed ostinato" and "free improvisation," and a useful one for arranging call-and-response
  textures generally.
- Reading a lead part's pitch/timbre vocabulary as a "speech" grammar, with its own connective
  figures and duration markers, is a compositional model worth having beyond this tradition.

## What must not be casually universalised

- **"African rhythm" is not one thing, and Ewe dance-drumming is not interchangeable with Mande
  jembe music or any other West African tradition.** See
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/MANDE_JEMBE_MUSIC.md` for a differently constructed tradition from a
  different people, region, and repertoire, documented in a different instrument page.
- Do not assume a single technique for a same-named drum across repertoires: agbadza's sogo is
  bare-handed; the comparable drum in the Yewevu ensemble Locke describes is played with sticks, or
  stick and hand.
- Do not present the metre question as settled, and do not import Mande non-isochrony findings into
  Ewe material without a source that measures it there specifically.
- **Yewevu is restricted repertoire and is not source material for this studio.** Locke's own
  account of the tradition states plainly that Yewe is guarded, secretive, and withheld from the
  uninitiated; this file and the instrument page decline it and work instead from agbadza and Drum
  Gahu, both public, openly taught repertoire. A request specifically for Yewevu content is declined
  with that reason given, and agbadza or Gahu offered instead.
- Do not treat a named piece tied to a funeral, an initiation, or an association as a loop library.

## Working with this in the studio

**Composer** sets: the twelve-pulse cycle, the bell pattern as a grid, which simultaneous hearings
(four, six, or both) are intended, the named repertoire and lead drum (sogo or atsimevu, since
technique differs), the response drum's theme relationship to the lead's calls, and which fixed parts
stay fixed.

**Performance Director** needs: the lead drum's stroke/pitch vocabulary from
`shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md`, the instruction that the response drum answers specific calls rather than
looping independently, and the fixed ending-signal phrase where the form calls for one.

**MIDI Builder and Plugin Auditor** must check that the sequencer can express a twelve-pulse cycle
without forcing it into sixteenths, and that the response drum's part can be authored as a function
of the lead drum's calls rather than as an independent fixed pattern. Retuning is not usually
relevant; timbral separation between the bell, the rattle, and each named drum is, and the audit
should confirm distinct instruments and articulations rather than one kit piece repitched. See
`shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/EWE_DANCE_DRUMS.md` for the full per-instrument articulation vocabularies.

**Listener Model** should not assume the listener's beat perception matches the sequencer bar line,
should not model the four-against-six relationship as a conflict to be resolved, and should not score
the response drum's bounded theme choice as either randomness or rigid repetition.
