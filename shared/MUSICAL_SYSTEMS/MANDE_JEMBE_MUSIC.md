# Mande jembe music

## Cultural context

Mande jembe music is the drum ensemble music of the Mande peoples of Mali and Guinea, centred on the
goblet-shaped jembe and the cylindrical dunun. It is documented here mainly through Rainer Polak's
long-term fieldwork and apprenticeship in Bamako, and through Polak and London's fieldwork on two
further, closely related Mande regional drumming styles: **Bamana bòn** drumming (Segu region,
central Mali) and **Khasonka dundunba** drumming (Khaso region, western Mali). Bòn uses its own
instruments (cunba, ngangan), not jembe or dunun, and is named here only for the rhythm and timing
findings its fieldwork shares with jembe and dundunba material; it is not otherwise covered by this
studio. Physical instrument construction and playing technique for jembe and dunun live on
`shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md`, linked throughout rather than repeated here.

Traditionally, jembe music accompanies song and dance at social occasions: life-cycle events,
communal agricultural labour, harvest rites, and religious festivals. Since the 1960s it has
urbanised and commercialised across francophone West Africa, was institutionalised as national
folkloric ballet repertoire, and, since the 1980s, has spread internationally through migration,
instrument export, tourism, and media, developing pedagogical contexts (drum circles, school music,
social pedagogy) with their own conventions, sometimes at odds with the source tradition's own
practice.

## Pitch organisation

Largely not applicable in the melodic-scale sense. Jembe and dunun carry relative tone/timbre
vocabularies set by hand or stick technique (see the instrument page for the full stroke
vocabularies), not a tuned scale. Polak's own framing is worth carrying forward: jembe phrases are
not "just rhythm," since their stroke sequences form recognisable timbre sequences that function
partly as melody.

## Rhythm and cycle

**Subdivision is not always isochronous, and this is a documented, structural feature rather than
an imprecision.** Polak ("Rhythmic Feel as Meter", *Music Theory Online* 16.4, 2010) and Polak and
London ("Timing and Meter in Mande Drumming", *MTO* 20.1, 2014) present chronometric measurement of
Malian drum ensembles showing systematically unequal beat subdivision as a stable, named property of
individual pieces, not a performance imperfection. Two documented instances, read at section depth
(`POLAK-LONDON-2014`):

```text
Bire (Khasonka dundunba, bell): long-short subdivision, grand average 58.6:41.4 (roughly 59:41)
  across four recordings; individual players average closer to 60:40 and 57:43. The ratio shifts
  slightly as tempo accelerates within a performance (for example, from about 59:41 to 61:39 across
  one recording).
Ngon Fariman (Segu Bambara bon): long-short-short subdivision, grand average about 41:31:28.
```

Tempi accelerate substantially within a single performance: documented ranges include about 85 to
125 bpm for one Bire recording and about 135 to 205 bpm for a jembe piece (Manjanin) documented in
Polak's earlier article. **This research found no evidence of rhythmic layers being dropped as tempo
increases**; the accompaniment and hook parts continue through the acceleration, and where an added
instrument (a third player, an accompanying jembe) is present, it is reported to follow the
established subdivision timing rather than simplifying it.

Two further named timing patterns recur across the jembe repertoire specifically: a ternary
short-flexible-long (SFL) feel and a long-flexible-flexible (LFF) feel, distinguishing pieces that
are otherwise notated identically as 12/8. **Whether this music has metre in the Western sense is an
open question this file does not resolve silently**: Polak argues these timing patterns individuate
genuinely distinct meters (after Justin London's "many meters" hypothesis), which is a stronger claim
than treating non-isochrony as expressive deviation from an isochronous norm.

Performances are organised around a **metric cycle** of four beats (in most pieces), subdivided
ternary or quaternary depending on the piece, with the accompaniment and hook parts stating the
subdivision most clearly and the lead part building faster, nested subdivisions on top of it (a
binary subdivision nested inside a ternary one, or a ternary one inside a quaternary one).

## Phrase structure and form

Three functional layers recur across the pieces documented here: a **core metrical accompaniment**
(dense, regular, and the clearest carrier of the subdivision timing), a **repertoire-determinative
hook** (a short, catchy, often two-tone melodic phrase that identifies the specific piece and, in
classical Bamako jembe style, is carried by the dunun), and an **improvising, regulative lead part**
(jembe or lead dundunba). Structured breaks (**blocage** in French; **tigè**, "to cut," in
Bamanankan) end sections of heightened solo dance performance (**échauffement**) without providing
room for spectacular ensemble arrangement, unlike breaks in some other genres.

## Ornamentation

Not the right frame in the melodic-decoration sense. The lead part's variation is structural: it
recombines a stable body of known phrases and themes ("performance-composition," after Nzewi),
balancing steady recycling against thematic movement, rather than ornamenting a fixed melodic line.

## The role of improvisation

Jembe performance is **"the improvisational interpretation of pre-composed materials and models"**:
a lead player draws on a body of thematic material, much of it shared across pieces for accompaniment
motives and largely piece-specific for lead themes, and recombines it live. One documented
professional's recording shows standard-phrase recycling filling roughly the large majority of
playing time, with only occasional non-repetitive bridges; that player's style is valued locally as
"calm, serene, staid," in explicit contrast to more heated, individualistic soloing associated with
younger players. Accompaniment and hook parts are close to invariant within a piece.

## Ensemble behaviour

The smallest typical ensemble is a duo (one jembe, one dunun); trios and quartets are also
conventional in classical Bamako style, and larger ensembles are common today. In Khasonka dundunba
style, a single performer plays both drum and bell simultaneously with two hands, a genuinely
demanding bimanual task documented to carry measurably more timing variability than playing the drum
alone; whether a dunun-family performer plays drum and bell together, or the drum only, is a
documented regional/stylistic variable, not a constant. Construction and technique for jembe and
dunun are on `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md`.

## What generalises

- Chronometrically measured, systematically non-isochronous subdivision is a real, learnable
  compositional resource, available in any genre, and is not the same thing as swing applied by a
  percentage slider: it is a named, stable pattern, not a randomised deviation.
- Three functional layers (a steady accompaniment stating the subdivision, a short identifying hook,
  and an improvising lead) is a transferable way to structure a polyrhythmic ensemble texture.
- Tempo acceleration across a performance, with the ensemble's rhythmic layers held intact rather
  than thinned out, is a usable structural device for building intensity without changing the
  arrangement.
- A bimanual, two-instrument technique played by one performer (drum and bell together) is a real
  performance-practice option worth knowing as a model, distinct from either two separate performers
  or a single-voice part.

## What must not be casually universalised

- **"African rhythm" is not one thing, and Mande jembe music is not interchangeable with Ewe
  dance-drumming or any other West African tradition.** See
  `shared/MUSICAL_SYSTEMS/EWE_DANCE_DRUMMING.md` for a differently constructed tradition from a
  different people, region, and repertoire, documented in a different instrument page.
- **Do not quantise jembe or dunun material to an isochronous grid and describe the result as the
  tradition.** The non-isochrony documented here is the finding, not noise to be corrected.
- Do not treat Bamana bòn, Khasonka dundunba, and classical Bamako jembe-ensemble style as one
  undifferentiated "Mande drumming": they use different instruments, different accompaniment
  structures, and, as documented, different long-short-versus-long-short-short subdivision patterns.
- Do not present the specific measured ratios above (Bire's 58.6:41.4; Ngòn Fariman's roughly
  41:31:28) as fixed constants of "swing" in this tradition: they are per-recording, per-piece
  measurements, reported with the corpus they came from, per `shared/HUMAN_PERFORMANCE_SCHEMA.md`
  section 3's treatment of corpus-specific timing figures.
- Do not treat a named piece with a ritual or initiatory dimension (Manjanin) as a loop library
  without checking its status; see `shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md`, "Restricted and ceremonial repertoire."

## Working with this in the studio

**Composer** sets: the metric cycle and its beat count, which named subdivision feel (SFL, LFF, or
another documented pattern) applies, the three functional layers and which instrument carries each,
and whether a tempo-acceleration arc is wanted.

**Performance Director** needs: the non-isochrony decision with explicit target timing ratios drawn
from a named corpus (never invented), the lead part's phrase vocabulary from
`shared/VIRTUAL_INSTRUMENT_GUIDE/JEMBE_AND_DUNUN.md`, and, for a Khasonka-style drum-and-bell part, the instruction that the bell
carries genuinely looser timing than the drum, not identical timing copied over.

**MIDI Builder and Plugin Auditor** must check that the sequencer can express non-isochronous
subdivision as named, per-position timing offsets rather than re-quantising them on import or
export, and that a tempo-acceleration arc, where used, is written as a real tempo curve rather than
approximated with swing. Retuning is not usually relevant; timbral separation between jembe strokes
and between drum and bell (where one performer plays both) is, and the audit should confirm distinct
sample sets rather than one drum patch doing duty for the ensemble.

**Listener Model** should not treat non-isochronous subdivision as timing error to be corrected, and
should not assume tempo will stay constant across a piece modelled on this tradition.
