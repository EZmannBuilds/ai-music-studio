# West African polyrhythm

## Cultural context

This file covers ensemble drumming and timeline practice in West Africa, primarily the Ewe
dance-drumming of Ghana and Togo and the Mande jembe music of Mali and Guinea, with reference to
the wider literature on timeline patterns.

These are specific practices with named repertoires, named pieces, and social functions: a piece
belongs to an occasion, a dance, an age group, or an association. Drum Gahu, for example, is a
named Ewe repertoire, not a style. Mande jembe music has its own instruments, its own ensemble
roles and its own regional variants.

Agawu (2003) argues that this music is **not categorically other**: treating African rhythm as a
different kind of thing from rhythm elsewhere is a habit of representation, not a finding. Start
there. A composer's job here is ordinary musical study of an unfamiliar repertoire, not an
encounter with an alien system.

## Pitch organisation

Largely not applicable. The ensembles discussed here are percussion and voice; pitch appears as
the tuned relationships among drums and bells and as the vocal parts, which follow their own
melodic conventions. Where a file is needed for melodic organisation, this is not it.

## Rhythm and cycle

The **standard pattern** is a seven-stroke figure spanning twelve eighth notes (Agawu, 2006),
played on a bell as one layer in a texture that also carries handclaps, rattles, support drums,
lead drums and voices (Locke).

The twelve-pulse cycle supports **simultaneous four-beat and six-beat hearings**, and the music
uses that deliberately. Locke's *metric matrix* (MTO 16.4, 2010) is a hybrid theory in which the
cross-rhythm is not an ambiguity to be resolved but a structure that different parts and different
listeners occupy at once.

```text
12 pulses
heard as 4 beats of 3   |x . . |x . . |x . . |x . . |
heard as 6 beats of 2   |x . |x . |x . |x . |x . |x . |
the bell pattern sits across both
```

**Subdivision is not always isochronous.** Polak ("Rhythmic Feel as Meter", MTO 16.4, 2010) and
Polak and London ("Timing and Meter in Mande Drumming", MTO 20.1, 2014) challenge the assumption
of pulse isochrony: some Malian drumming uses systematically non-isochronous subdivision as a
metric feel, with corpus evidence in London, Polak and Jacoby (2016). A quantised grid can
therefore mis-render the music, not merely stiffen it.

Timelines are **learned together with the main beats as composites**, and are commonly taught
through onomatopoeic phrases rather than as counts (Kubik, 1994). The phrase carries both the
pattern and its alignment.

**Whether this music has metre in the Western sense is an open question in the literature.** Agawu
says yes and says it is not exotic. Locke says yes, as a simultaneous four-and-six matrix. Polak
says yes, but not necessarily isochronous. The studio does not pick one of these silently.

## Phrase structure and form

Form is driven by the dance and by the lead drum. The ensemble holds a cycle; the lead drum
signals changes of section, entries, exits and dance figures. Length is a function of the occasion
rather than of a fixed chorus count. Call-and-response between lead drum and ensemble, and between
a song leader and a chorus, is the primary formal device.

## Ornamentation

Not the right frame. Variation appears as lead-drum improvisation and as small timing and dynamic
inflections in supporting parts, not as decoration of a melodic line.

## The role of improvisation

The lead drummer improvises within a vocabulary of known phrases that carry meaning to the dancers
and to the ensemble: cues, calls and responses. Supporting parts do not improvise; their constancy
is what makes the lead drum legible. Freedom is concentrated in one role and bounded by function.

## Ensemble behaviour

Stratified roles, all measured against the bell: bell, rattles and handclaps hold the timeline
layer; support drums hold fixed interlocking parts; lead drum varies and directs; voices carry the
song. Each part is timbrally distinct, which is how a listener separates the layers.

## What generalises

- Building a texture where parts are heard against a reference layer, rather than against a
  conductor's beat, is a transferable arranging approach.
- Deliberately sustaining two hearings of one cycle (four against six) gives a groove depth
  without adding notes.
- Distributing a composite pattern across players so that no one plays the whole figure produces
  interlock rather than doubling.
- Teaching and remembering a rhythm as a spoken phrase, rather than as a count, is a practical
  compositional tool for any drummer or programmer.
- Non-isochronous subdivision is available as a deliberate feel in any genre, and it is not the
  same thing as swing applied by a percentage slider.

## What must not be casually universalised

- **"African rhythm" is not one thing.** Do not use the continent as a genre. Name the practice:
  Ewe dance-drumming, Mande jembe music, and so on.
- Ewe, Mande and Cuban practices are distinct, with different repertoires and social functions,
  even where patterns resemble one another.
- Do not present the metre question as settled. Say which position you are working from and why.
- Do not quantise Mande material to an isochronous grid and describe the result as the tradition.
- Do not treat a named piece tied to a ceremony or an association as a loop library.
- Do not use these ensembles as "percussion energy" under unchanged Western pop harmony and form.

## Working with this in the studio

**Composer** sets: the cycle in pulses, the timeline pattern as a grid, which simultaneous
hearings are intended, the roles and their fixed parts, and whether the subdivision is isochronous
or not.

**Performance Director** needs: the non-isochrony decision with explicit target timings if it
applies, the lead-drum vocabulary and where it signals, and the instruction that supporting parts
stay fixed.

**MIDI Builder and Plugin Auditor** must check that the sequencer can express a 12-pulse cycle
without forcing it into sixteenths, and that per-hit timing offsets survive export. If
non-isochronous subdivision is intended, verify that the grid or the offsets actually carry it
rather than being re-quantised on import. Retuning is not usually relevant; timbral separation
between the bell and the drums is, and the audit should confirm distinct instruments rather than
one kit piece repitched.

**Listener Model** should not assume the listener's beat perception matches the sequencer bar
line, and should not model the four-against-six relationship as a conflict to be resolved.
