# Just intonation

## Cultural context

This is a tuning theory file rather than a tradition file, so "who plays this" has a different
answer: just intonation is a way of specifying pitch by whole-number frequency ratios, and it
appears in many unrelated places.

- As the implicit tuning of unaccompanied vocal ensembles, string quartets and other groups that
  adjust by ear, which drift toward simple ratios because they beat less.
- As the explicit tuning of drone traditions, for the acoustic reason set out in
  `DRONE_TRADITIONS.md`.
- As a composed system in twentieth and twenty-first century Western concert music: Harry Partch,
  Ben Johnston, La Monte Young, and the composers who followed them.
- As a theory tradition in its own right, in Europe and elsewhere, with its own long history.

**"Just intonation" is not one scale.** It is a principle for generating pitch, and the scales
generated from it are many and incompatible. Partch's 43 tones and a five-limit major scale are
both just, and they have almost nothing in common in practice.

## Pitch organisation

A **ratio** is a frequency relationship: 3/2 is a fifth, 5/4 a major third, 7/4 a harmonic
seventh. Cents convert a ratio to a logarithmic measure, with 1200 cents to the octave.

```text
cents = 1200 * log2(ratio)

3/2   = 701.955 cents      12-EDO fifth        = 700       difference  1.955
5/4   = 386.314 cents      12-EDO major third  = 400       difference 13.686
7/4   = 968.826 cents      12-EDO minor seventh= 1000      difference 31.174
```

A **limit** names which prime numbers the harmony admits. Five-limit harmony uses ratios built
from 2, 3 and 5, which gives the familiar triads. Seven-limit admits the harmonic seventh.
Eleven-limit admits intervals with no equal-tempered approximation at all. Raising the limit adds
new consonances rather than merely adding notes.

**Otonality and utonality** are the two directions. Partch describes otonality as the set of
pitches generated by numerical factors over a constant denominator, and utonality as its
inversion, and used otonality as an analogue to major and utonality as an analogue to minor. An
otonal chord is a slice of a harmonic series over a common fundamental; a utonal chord is its
mirror.

The **tonality diamond** arranges otonal and utonal sets across a chosen limit into a grid. Read
it as a chord map: each row is an otonality, each column a utonality, and moving through it is
moving between chords. Partch's 43-tone scale is built from an eleven-limit diamond, with fourteen
secondary ratios added by the late 1950s to the basic 29 pitches.

## Rhythm and cycle

Not applicable. Some just-intonation composers extend the ratio principle to tempo and duration,
but that is a compositional choice, not part of the tuning system.

## Phrase structure and form

Not applicable as a property of the tuning. What the tuning does change is the pacing of harmony:
because the consonances are more exact, small changes are more audible and a piece can afford to
move more slowly.

## Ornamentation

Not applicable.

## The role of improvisation

Not applicable as a system property. Improvisation in just intonation is possible, and is bounded
in practice by the instrument: a retuned keyboard offers only the pitches it has, while voices and
fretless strings can move freely within the lattice.

## Ensemble behaviour

Omitted: this is a tuning theory, not an ensemble practice. The practical constraint is that every
instrument in an ensemble has to be in the same just system, tuned from the same reference, or the
consonances that motivate the system do not appear.

## Commas, and why just intonation does not transpose freely

Stacking pure intervals does not close. Twelve pure fifths do not equal seven octaves; four pure
fifths do not equal a pure major third two octaves up. The small leftovers are **commas**, and
they are the central practical fact of the system.

The consequence: **a just scale is built around a reference, and it does not transpose.** A
five-limit major scale on C contains a pure third above C, but the same twelve pitches do not give
a pure third above D. Play the same progression in another key and different intervals turn sour.
This is not a flaw to be fixed; it is the property that historical temperaments exist to trade
away, which is the subject of `HISTORICAL_TEMPERAMENTS.md`.

Two ways around it:

1. **More pitches.** Provide separate pitches for the versions of a note that different chords
   need. This is what large just scales do, and why Partch needed 43 tones and a custom
   instrumentarium.
2. **Adaptive tuning.** Retune notes on the fly so that each chord is tuned justly and the
   reference drifts or is corrected as the music moves. Sethares discusses adaptive tuning at
   length; commercial implementations exist, and every one of them makes choices about what to
   sacrifice.

## What generalises

- Specifying pitch as ratios rather than as semitones makes the relationship between notes
  explicit, and it is a useful way to think even in 12-tone equal temperament.
- Choosing a limit is a compositional decision about the harmonic vocabulary, comparable to
  choosing a mode. Deciding to admit the seventh harmonic changes what the music can do.
- Otonal and utonal are a real alternative to major and minor as a pair of basic chord qualities.
- Knowing that a tuning does not transpose forces a composer to decide where the piece lives,
  which is often a productive constraint.
- The beating produced by a mistuned interval is information, and it can be used deliberately.

## What must not be casually universalised

- **"Just intonation" is not one scale, and it is not a preset.** Anything that offers "JI" as a
  single option has failed to state which JI.
- Do not describe just intervals as "in tune" and equal-tempered ones as "out of tune". They are
  different trades, and the trade is the subject of `HISTORICAL_TEMPERAMENTS.md`.
- Do not assume a just scale can be transposed, or that a progression that works in one key works
  in another.
- Do not use the ratios of one tradition as a generic just tuning. Highland pipe ratios,
  tanpura-derived tunings and Partch's diamond are not interchangeable.
- Do not present La Monte Young's tunings as generic just intonation. They are the composer's own
  system.

## Working with this in the studio

**Composer** sets: the limit, the reference pitch (the 1/1) with its frequency in Hz, the set of
ratios in use, and whether the piece transposes at all. State the ratios, not only the cents.

**Performance Director** needs: which notes are which version (a comma-shifted note is a different
note, not an expressive inflection), and whether any player is expected to adjust by ear against a
reference.

**MIDI Builder and Plugin Auditor** must check that every instrument in the piece can load the
same scale and can be given the same reference. A scale file says nothing about which key it
starts on, so the reference must be set explicitly per instrument or the same file will land in
different keys. Check also whether the instrument's filters and effects track the tuning, since
some do not. If an instrument cannot be retuned, say so and offer a substitution or a bend-based
fallback rather than re-quantising to 12-tone equal temperament. See `shared/TUNING_AND_MPE.md`.

**Listener Model** should not assume a listener trained on equal temperament hears just thirds as
more correct. Unfamiliarity is a real effect, and the low beat rate can read as unusual before it
reads as pure.
